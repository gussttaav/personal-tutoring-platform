A company that makes invoicing software (call it Albarán) has an internal assistant for its support
team: a language model that answers questions about the company's policies. Someone on the team
asks it:

> How many days does a Team plan customer have to ask for a refund on an annual renewal?

And the assistant answers, with a citation:

> 14 days from the charge. *[Refunds FAQ]*

The citation exists, and it says what the answer says it says. The FAQ states, word for word, that
"as a rule, refunds can be requested within 14 days of the charge". But the Team plan terms, another
of the documents the assistant has to hand, give annual subscriptions 30 days. The answer is wrong,
and it comes with a source that backs it up.

The case is invented, but the failure is one of the most common there is. The assistant follows the
RAG pattern (*Retrieval-Augmented Generation*): before answering, it searches the company's
documents for the few fragments that look most like the question and passes them to the model along
with it. The search returned the FAQ, which talks about "refunds", "days" and "the charge", just as
the question does. The right paragraph is in section 7 of the Team plan terms. It's headed "Returns"
and says that "annual subscriptions have 30 calendar days from the date of the charge". It doesn't
name the plan, because only the document's title does that, and it never uses the word "refund". Cut
off from its document, that fragment looks less like the question than the general rule does, and it
didn't make the cut.

There are fixes for this, and they work: search for exact words as well, add a sentence to every
fragment saying which document it comes from, rerank the results with a second model. Each one is
another piece to build, measure and maintain. But Albarán has a simpler way out, because its policy
documents are few: forty of them, about sixty pages. A language model doesn't read words but
*tokens*, pieces of words (a page of text is about five hundred), and what fits into one request is
called its **context window**. Albarán's policies come to about 32,000 tokens, and today's models
have windows of 128,000 tokens to a million. They fit whole. If the model reads all of them before
every question, there's no search left to fail: section 7 is right there, with the plan's name above
it.

The objection is cost. The support team is 25 people asking about 600 questions a day, and each one
would pay to reread 32,000 tokens that haven't changed since the last. Unless it doesn't have to
reread them. A model that has just read a text keeps an intermediate result of that reading, the
**KV cache** (KV stands for *key* and *value*), and if the next request starts the same way, it can
skip the part it has already read. In December 2024, a paper with a deliberately provocative title,
[*Don't Do RAG*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.15605), gave a name to using that on purpose: **CAG**
(*Cache-Augmented Generation*). You read the documents once, keep the cache, and answer each question
from it, without searching for anything.

CAG is usually presented as another flavour of RAG, or as its replacement. It's neither. It's the
oldest option there is, giving the model the whole context, at the moment it stopped being
expensive. And the question it raises isn't whether to search, but which part of what an assistant
knows is small and stable enough to read once, and which part still has to sit behind a search. At
Albarán, the policies pass that test. The support tickets, tens of thousands of them with new ones
every hour, don't. What follows explains how the cache that makes this possible works, what the
paper measured, what it costs and where it fails, the history the idea comes from, and when it pays
off.

## Three places to put what the model doesn't know

A language model knows what it learned in training, spread across its weights. It knows nothing
after the date its data was frozen, and nothing that wasn't public: the Team plan terms were never on
the internet. There are three places to put what it's missing, and CAG is a new way of using the
third.

| | In the weights (fine-tuning) | In an index (RAG) | Everything in context | Everything in context, cached (CAG) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Updating a document | retrain | reindex that document | edit the text | edit the text and recompute the cache from there |
| Cost of each question | the usual | proportional to what's retrieved | proportional to everything | a fraction of everything |
| A search can fail | there's no search | yes | no | no |
| How much knowledge it takes | little, and hard to control | almost unlimited | whatever fits in the window | whatever fits in the window |

**The weights** are the wrong place for facts. Fine-tuning, training the model further on your own
data, is good at teaching ways of doing things: a tone, a format, a domain's vocabulary. For specific
facts it's expensive and opaque: every policy change means another training run, and there's no way
to check exactly what the model has learned.

**The index** is what Albarán's assistant does. The documents are split in advance into fragments of
a few hundred words, and each one is turned into a vector that represents its meaning (an
*embedding*). For every question, you look for the few chunks whose vectors sit closest to the
query's. Each question pays for what it retrieves, not for everything there is, and the index can
grow almost without limit. The price is that there's a search in the middle, and it can fail, as it
just did.

**The context** removes the search: you paste everything into the request. It has two obvious limits.
It only works for what fits in the window, and every question pays to read all of it, whether it
needs to or not. CAG tackles the second. The first remains, and it has a less obvious version that
comes up later on: fitting in the window doesn't mean the model reads it well.

## What a model does when it reads

To see what the cache holds, and why keeping it saves so much, you need to look at how a model
processes a text before it answers.

### Two phases: reading and writing

A language model generates text one token at a time, and each one depends on all those before it.
Answering a request has two phases that behave very differently.

The first reads the input, and it's usually called the *prefill*. All of its tokens are known from
the start, so the model processes them together, in a single pass, and the phase is limited by the
chip's computing power. Its cost grows with the length: with a model of 8 billion parameters, reading
32,000 tokens takes about $5 \times 10^{14}$ operations, plus those of attention, which grow with the
square of the length. While it runs, the user sees nothing: it's the wait before the first word of
the answer appears.

The second phase, **decoding**, writes the answer. Each token depends on the one before, so they're
generated one at a time, and for each one the chip has to read every parameter of the model from
memory. This phase is limited by memory, not by computation. Why that is, and why it explains that
these calculations run on GPUs, the processors in graphics cards, is covered in detail in
[this article about GPUs](/blog/de-videojuegos-a-llm). Here the difference is enough: reading is
expensive because of how much there is to compute, and writing because of how much memory there is
to get through.

### The KV cache

The part of a language model that relates tokens to one another is **attention**, and it's repeated
in every layer. In each layer, every token produces three vectors: a query, a key and a value. To
compute a token's output, its query is compared with the keys of every earlier token and its own,
and the result decides how much each one's value weighs in the mix.

A token only looks backwards. Its key and value depend on itself and on what came before, and on
nothing that comes after, so once they've been computed they never change. That's why they're kept:
when the next token arrives, the keys and values of all the earlier ones are already in memory and
only its own need computing. That memory is the **KV cache**. Without it, writing each token of the
answer would mean recomputing the keys and values of the whole text before it.

Every model server uses the KV cache within a request. The idea behind CAG is to keep it **between**
requests. And the same property that makes this possible sets its most important condition: since a
token's key and value depend on everything before it, the policies' cache is only valid if that text
sits at the start, unchanged. If anything changes ahead of them, even a single character, their keys
and values are no longer the same.

### How much room it takes

For every token and every layer, the cache stores a key and a value. Attention isn't computed once
per layer but several times in parallel, in what are called **heads**, and each head has its own key
and its own value. Llama 3.1 8B, Meta's open model with 8 billion parameters, has 32 layers; its 32
query heads share 8 key-value heads (a trick that cuts the cache to a quarter), and each is a vector
of 128 two-byte numbers:

$$
2 \times 32 \times 8 \times 128 \times 2 = 131\,072 \text{ bytes} = 128 \text{ KiB per token.}
$$

Albarán's 32,000 policy tokens come to about 4.2 GB: a quarter of what the model's own weights take
up, 16 GB at 16 bits. With Llama 3.1 70B, which has 80 layers, it's 320 KiB per token and 10.5 GB.
That isn't nothing, but it's computed once, and storing it costs far less than recomputing it for
every question: it can stay on the GPU if it's about to be used, or move to the server's memory or to
disk if not.

There's one cost that doesn't go away. During decoding, each token of the answer has to read the
weights and the whole cache as well: 16 GB plus 4.2, roughly a quarter more bytes than without the
policies. With the 8-billion-parameter model, each token of the answer takes roughly a quarter
longer. Keeping the cache saves reading the policies, but not going through their keys and values
for every token.

## CAG, step by step

The 2024 paper splits the method into three phases. In the first, done once, the model reads every
document and its KV cache is saved in memory or on disk. In the second, each question is appended
after that cache and the model answers without searching for anything. In the third, the cache,
which has grown with the question and the answer, is **trimmed** back to where the documents ended,
ready for the next query without recomputing anything.

```python
# Once, and again whenever the policies change
cache = model.read(policies)    # prefill of 32,000 tokens → KV cache
end = len(cache)

# For every question
def answer(question):
    reply = model.generate(question, cache)   # the cache grows with the question and the reply
    cache.trim(end)                           # back to the policies, nothing recomputed
    return reply
```

The same assistant with RAG, in its simplest form, searches for the $k$ fragments most like the
question and builds a prompt from them:

```python
def answer(question, index, k=4):
    chunks = index.search(embed(question), k)
    return model(prompt(question, chunks))
```

What disappears going from one to the other is everything around `index.search`: the
embedding model, the fragment size, the overlap between fragments, the value of $k$, the reranker,
the database that stores the vectors. What's left is one model and one text. The figure puts the two
systems side by side.

### What the paper measured

The work comes from four researchers at National Chengchi University and Academia Sinica, in Taiwan,
and it's short: five pages in the companion proceedings of WWW 2025, the international conference on
the web. They used Llama 3.1 8B, which accepts 128,000 tokens, on eight NVIDIA V100 GPUs, a chip from
2017. They compared it with two RAG systems on the same model: one that searches by words with BM25,
the classic formula that scores a document by which of the question's terms it contains and how rare
they are, and another that retrieves by embedding similarity. Both retrieved 1, 3, 5 or 10 fragments.

The questions come from two public datasets. In [SQuAD](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.05250), the answer
is in one passage of a Wikipedia article. In [HotPotQA](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.09600), it takes
two paragraphs from two different articles. From each they built three document collections of
growing size: from 21,000 to 85,000 tokens for HotPotQA (16, 32 and 64 documents) and from 21,000 to
50,000 for SQuAD (3, 4 and 7). They measured quality with [BERTScore](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.09675),
which compares the generated answer with a reference answer word by word through their embeddings,
so it scores highly whatever resembles the reference.

CAG gets the best score in five of the six cases. On the small HotPotQA collection it scores 0.795,
against 0.768 for the best RAG. On the large one, the 85,000-token collection, it loses: 0.741,
against 0.754 for BM25 with five fragments. The gap narrows as the collection grows, and the paper
itself points out that, although Llama 3.1 8B accepts 128,000 tokens, its measured effective context
is about 32,000.

The timing table is the most interesting one, and it reads better than the text that discusses it.
On the large HotPotQA collection, handing the model all 64 documents without a cache takes 92 seconds
per question, nearly all of it spent reading them. With the precomputed cache, 2.3 seconds: forty
times less. And BM25 with three fragments takes 0.67 seconds, under a millisecond of search and the
rest spent generating. The cache is what makes reading everything feasible, but it doesn't make it
faster than a simple search over the same documents.

There are three things to bear in mind when reading it. The collections are small, and searching 16
or 64 documents isn't hard either. The model is small, and bigger models improve both systems. And
BERTScore rewards resemblance, not correctness: "14 days from the charge" and "30 days from the
charge" look very much alike, word for word, and only one is true at Albarán. Differences of two or
three hundredths don't tell you how many more answers are right. What the paper claims, and its data
supports, is more modest than its title: with a small knowledge base, giving the model everything
with a precomputed cache answers as well as or better than RAG, without RAG's complexity.

## Two ways to get it

The paper computes the cache, stores it and trims it by hand, with the open model's code. Hardly
anyone does it that way. There are two ways to get the same result without writing those lines,
depending on who runs the model.

### If the model is yours

Open-source inference servers, the programs that load a model and serve queries, reuse the cache
across requests without being asked. [vLLM](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/design/prefix_caching.html)
stores the cache in blocks of 16 tokens, an idea from its
[2023 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06180) that borrows from an operating system's virtual memory,
and identifies each block by a *hash* (a numeric fingerprint) of the whole text from the start up to
it. If a new request starts the same way as an earlier one, its first blocks have the same
fingerprint and get reused. [SGLang](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07104), another open server, keeps
every opening it has seen in a tree so it can find the longest match.

At Albarán, this means it's enough to always put the policies at the start of the prompt: the server
does the rest. And because blocks are shared, twenty-five people asking at once use a single copy of
the 4.2 GB, each adding only their question and answer. If the cache doesn't fit on the GPU, libraries
such as [LMCache](https://github.com/LMCache/LMCache) move it to the server's memory or to disk and
bring it back when needed.

### If you use a provider's

With a model used through a provider's API (application programming interface), you neither see nor
store the cache: you send the whole text with every request, and the provider recognises the opening
it has already read. Google announced it for Gemini in May 2024, Anthropic launched it for Claude
[in August](https://www.anthropic.com/news/prompt-caching) and OpenAI in October. As of October 2026,
all three do it by default on their recent models. With caching, CAG through an API is exactly
pasting the policies at the start of every request: the saving lies in the provider not reading them
again.

The terms vary from provider to provider and from year to year, but they share a shape. Reading a
token from the cache costs around a tenth of an ordinary input token, and a little less on some
models. Writing it the first time costs more than an ordinary token: 25% more for entries that last a
few minutes, double or more for ones that live for hours. An entry expires if nobody uses it for that
long, and every read renews it. And there's a minimum, between 512 and 4,096 tokens depending on the
model: a shorter opening isn't stored.

### The prefix rule

Both routes share the rule from the KV cache: only a **prefix**, the start of the text, is reused,
and only if it's identical byte for byte. If one character changes in the third document, everything
after the change has to be read again. That has three practical consequences.

The first is that order matters. Albarán's policies fall into five groups of documents: the terms of
the three plans (Basic, Team and Business), 13,500 tokens; billing and payments, 8,000; privacy and
data, 5,500; the service level agreements, 2,000; and the FAQ, 3,000. All of them change once or
twice a month, except the FAQ, which gets touched up every week. If it opens the prompt, every
touch-up forces a reread of all 32,000. If it goes last, only its own 3,000. What changes least goes
first.

The second is that nothing variable can come before the documents. A classic case is putting the
date and time at the top of the assistant's instructions so it knows what day it is: every request
then starts differently and the cache is never reused. The date belongs after the documents, next to
the question.

The third is that the text has to be generated the same way every time. If the documents come from a
query that doesn't guarantee their order, or are converted to JSON (a text format for data) with the
keys in a different position each time, the prefix changes without anyone having touched anything.

## What it costs, and where it breaks

### What each question costs

To compare without depending on any one provider's prices, it helps to count in a single unit: what
one ordinary input token costs. Without a cache, every Albarán question pays 32,000 units for the
policies. With a cache, the first pays for the write, 40,000 (25% more), and each one after that
pays 3,200 (a tenth). The RAG system from the opening, with four fragments of 500 tokens, pays 2,000.
In every case this leaves out the question, the answer and the instructions, which cost the same for
all three.

Over a day, the 600 questions arrive well under five minutes apart, so the entry doesn't expire
during working hours. If it's written again ten or so times, after lunch or first thing in the
morning, the day costs about 2.3 million units with a cache, against 19.2 million without it and 1.2
million with RAG. The cache cuts the cost by a factor of eight and puts CAG in the same order of
magnitude as RAG. If the provider charges a twentieth for reads, as some 2026 models do, the gap with
RAG all but disappears.

Latency changes in the same way. The wait for the first word no longer depends on the 32,000 policy
tokens: when Anthropic launched its cache, it announced up to 85% less waiting on long prompts. What
doesn't change is the decoding cost from the previous section: every token of the answer still reads
the whole cache.

### Fitting isn't the same as being read

A model's context window is what it accepts, not what it uses well. In 2023,
[*Lost in the Middle*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172) measured that models make better use of what
sits at the start and end of the context than of what's left in the middle: in its experiment with
twenty documents, GPT-3.5-Turbo got fewer answers right with the relevant one in the middle than with
none at all. In 2024, [RULER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.06654), an NVIDIA benchmark with tasks harder
than finding a hidden sentence, tested seventeen models that advertised 32,000 tokens or more, and
only half held up acceptably at that length.

In 2025, [NoLiMa](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05167), another benchmark, raised the bar: questions
whose answer shares no words with the question, which is exactly the case of the Team plan's returns
clause. At 32,000 tokens, eleven of thirteen models fell below half of what they scored on short
texts, and GPT-4o dropped from 99.3% to 69.7%. The same year, Chroma, the company behind the vector
database of the same name, published a report on what it called
[*context rot*](https://www.trychroma.com/research/context-rot) covering eighteen models, among them
GPT-4.1, Claude 4 and Gemini 2.5. All of them got worse as the input grew, even on simple tasks, and
two of its findings hit Albarán squarely. A passage that resembles the right one but is wrong, a
distractor, lowers accuracy. And a coherent text, like a company's policies, does more harm than the
same sentences shuffled.

At Albarán, the distractor exists and has a name: the FAQ. With CAG, the general rule's "14 days" is
still in the context, a few pages from the Team plan's "30 days", and the model has to choose. The
RAG system failed because it never saw section 7. CAG can fail by seeing it and sticking with the
general rule. What it does is swap a search failure for a reading failure, and the second depends on
the model, on the length and on where each thing sits. The instructions can help ("each plan's terms
take precedence over the FAQ"), but the only way to know whether that's enough is to test it with real
questions, at the real length.

### What changes

CAG is for things that change little. Albarán's policies change once or twice a month, and each
change costs one cache write: next to nothing. Support tickets come in every few minutes. Putting
them in the prefix would mean rewriting it constantly, and every write costs more than an uncached
read. And even if they didn't change, a year's worth wouldn't fit in any window.

Deleting, on the other hand, is easy, as it is with RAG and unlike with the weights: if a piece of
data has to go, you remove it from the text and the cache is recomputed from that point.

### Who can read what

Among Albarán's policies is a document with the commercial discount margins, which only team leads
may see. With RAG, permission is a filter on the search: each user searches only what they're allowed
to read. With CAG, permission is the prefix: if one group can read a document another can't, each
needs its own prefix and its own cache. With two groups, that's nothing. If a company serves fifty
clients with contracts of their own, it needs fifty caches, each with its own write and its own
memory. And the temptation to put everything in one shared prefix and ask the model not to show
anyone what they shouldn't see is a mistake: instructions aren't access control.

### What you look at when it fails

A RAG system leaves a trail. Every answer comes with a list of retrieved fragments, and when the
answer is wrong you can check whether the right fragment was among them: if it wasn't, the search
failed, and if it was, the reading did. With CAG, the model has everything in front of it for every
question, and when it fails it's always the reading. It helps to ask it to quote the exact passage it
relies on and to check, with a program, that the quote exists in the text. And there's a new metric
to watch: the share of input tokens read from the cache. If it drops, something has started changing
the prefix. The team at Manus, a company that builds agents (models that chain steps and use tools on
their own), wrote in 2025 that it's
["the single most important metric for a production-stage AI agent"](https://manus.im/blog/Context-Engineering-for-AI-Agents-Lessons-from-Building-Manus).

## Between CAG and RAG

The prefix rule leaves out an obvious case: reusing the cache of the fragments a search returns. If
each fragment had its cache computed in advance, a RAG system could glue those together instead of
reading the fragments. [Prompt Cache](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04934) (2023) already proposed
declaring reusable pieces of a request, and [RAGCache](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.12457) (2024),
keeping the cache of the most frequently retrieved documents. The problem is that a fragment's cache
computed on its own hasn't seen what comes before it in the prompt: its keys and values are those of a
text that starts there, and gluing them together as they are makes the answers worse.
[CacheBlend](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16444), which won best paper at EuroSys 2025, a systems conference, recomputes only
the few tokens whose cache changes most when the fragments are joined, and gets the first word out 2.2
to 3.3 times sooner than reading everything. [TurboRAG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.07590) takes the
other route: it fine-tunes the model to accept fragments computed separately.

Further out still, [KBLaM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.10450), from Microsoft (presented at ICLR 2025, one of the major machine learning
conferences), doesn't
store text at all: it turns each fact in a knowledge base into a key-value pair that the model attends
to through a modified attention, and fits more than 10,000 facts into an 8-billion-parameter model on
a single 80 GB A100 GPU. All of these techniques are for people who run their own model. No provider
offers them in its API today; APIs only reuse prefixes.

## Where it came from and where it's going

In 2020, when the [paper that named RAG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401) came out, its generator,
BART, a language model from Facebook, read at most 1,024 tokens, and GPT-3, the largest model of its day, 2,048. Putting the documents
in the context wasn't an option. Searching was the only way to give a model knowledge that wasn't in
its weights, and RAG was born of that constraint as much as of its own merits.

In 2023, windows multiplied: 32,000 tokens for GPT-4 in March, 100,000 for Claude in May, 200,000 for
Claude 2.1 in November. The same year, servers learned to manage the KV cache in blocks (vLLM, in
September) and to reuse it across requests (Prompt Cache in November, SGLang in December), and *Lost
in the Middle* warned that fitting isn't the same as being read well.

In 2024 came the million-token window, with Gemini 1.5 in February, and provider caching, between May
and October. In July, a group from Google DeepMind and the University of Michigan published the
[most cited comparison](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.16833) between long context and RAG, with
Gemini-1.5-Pro, GPT-4o and GPT-3.5-Turbo. Given enough resources, handing over everything won on
average; RAG cost far less; and on 63% of the questions the two gave exactly the same answer. That
led to Self-Route: ask with RAG first, let the model say whether it can answer from what was
retrieved, and only if it can't, give it the full context. With Gemini-1.5-Pro it cut the cost by 65%
for a comparable result. In December, the CAG paper named the other half of the argument: if the
cache makes reading everything cheap, maybe the first round isn't needed at all.

In 2025, caching stopped being something you had to ask for. Google made it implicit for Gemini 2.5
in May, as OpenAI's had been from the start, and agents turned it into their main cost concern: an
agent sends the same instructions and the same tool definitions at every step, and pays for them at
the cached price or the full price depending on whether the start of the prompt was left alone. The
phrase that caught on that year for all of this, "context engineering", says a lot: what mattered was
no longer how to word a request, but what to put in the context and in what order. In 2026, cache
entries last longer and reads cost less.

Seen this way, CAG hasn't taken off as an architecture, but it has won as a mechanism. Few systems
call themselves CAG, and nearly all of them use it: every coding agent reads its instructions from
the cache, and every long conversation is a growing prefix. What has changed for good is the standing
of "put everything in the context", which went from being the naive option to being the first one
worth ruling out. The paper itself ended by proposing a mixed system, with a fixed base in the cache
and search only for the cases it doesn't cover, and that's what's being built. What remains open is
what limits CAG today: whether effective context will grow as fast as nominal context, whether caching
loose fragments will reach the APIs, and how to keep per-user caches without multiplying their cost.

## When to use which

Given all this, the choice depends on three things about the knowledge, not on the latest buzzword:
how much room it takes, how often it changes, and how many questions it gets before the cache
expires.

| If the knowledge… | At Albarán | Use |
|---|---|---|
| fits comfortably, changes little and gets many questions | the policies | CAG: everything in the prefix, in order of stability |
| fits, but is consulted rarely | a contract someone reviews one afternoon | everything in context; the cache doesn't matter |
| doesn't fit, or changes by the hour | the support tickets | RAG, or a tool that queries the ticket database |
| has parts not everyone may read | the commercial margins | one prefix per group if there are few; filtered search if there are many |
| forces you to combine the stable with the changing | "Is the customer on ticket 4812 entitled to a refund?" | both: the policies in the prefix and the ticket through a tool |

"Fits comfortably" means it fits within the length at which the model still reads well, not within
the window it advertises, and the only way to know that is to test. Before building anything, take
fifty real questions and run them through both systems: with everything in context and with your RAG,
on the same model. If they give the same answers, keep whichever is simpler to maintain. If they
don't, look at where each one fails, because they fail for different reasons: one because it didn't
find the passage, the other because it didn't read it properly.

## What to take away

If you take away only one thing, make it this: **CAG isn't an alternative to RAG; it's deciding which
part of what your assistant knows is small and stable enough to read once.** That part goes first,
whole and cached. Whatever changes or doesn't fit goes after it, behind a search or a tool. For
Albarán, the prompt ends up like this.

And if you take away three more, make them practical.

**Measure before you choose.** Count the tokens in your stable knowledge, how many questions arrive
within the life of a cache entry, and how often it changes. If it fits, gets many questions and
changes little, the cache makes it almost as cheap as retrieving fragments, and you can do without
the search engine.

**Look after the prefix.** The most stable content first, the variable content after, and the text
always generated the same way. Watch what share of input tokens is read from the cache: it's the
number that tells you something has broken the prefix.

**Don't confuse fitting with being read well.** The policies fitting in the window doesn't mean the
model will pick the plan's rule over the general one. CAG swaps search failures for reading failures,
and the latter only show up when you test with your own questions, at your own length.
