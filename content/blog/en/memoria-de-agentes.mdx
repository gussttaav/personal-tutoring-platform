A company that makes invoicing software (call it Albarán) has an internal assistant for its support
team: a language model that answers questions about customers and company policy. Since the spring
it has had memory. It remembers what each person on the team told it in earlier conversations, so
nobody has to repeat themselves. On 10 September, Lucía, who works in support, asks it:

> Talleres Ruiz wants a refund on their annual renewal, charged on 15 July. Are they still in time?

The assistant answers, with a citation:

> Yes. Talleres Ruiz has an agreement with sales that gives them 60 days to ask for a refund on the
> annual renewal, so the window closes on 13 September. *[Memory: conversation of 10 March]*

The citation is real, and it says what the answer claims it says. On 10 March, Lucía told the
assistant exactly that. But on 18 June, at the end of a long conversation about a bug in the invoice
export, she added one line: "By the way, Ruiz's 60 days are over: sales isn't renewing the deal, and
from the July renewal they're back on the normal terms." The normal terms of Talleres Ruiz's plan,
the Team plan, give annual subscriptions 30 days. The window closed on 14 August, and the answer is
wrong, with a source that appears to back it up.

Nobody deleted the June message. The memory turned it into something else. This assistant doesn't
keep conversations: after each one, a model reads it and pulls out short facts, and a second step
decides whether each new fact is added to what's already there, corrects it, deletes it, or changes
nothing. March produced "Talleres Ruiz: 60-day refund window (sales agreement)". June produced
"Talleres Ruiz is back on the normal terms of its plan". The second doesn't contradict the first
word for word, since it mentions neither the days nor the refund, so it was added alongside. Neither
carries a date. In September, the search for what the memory knew about Talleres Ruiz's refunds
brought back the first, which talks about refunds, and not the second, which doesn't.

The case is invented, but the failure is one of the most studied in the field. Had the conversations
been kept as they were written, with their dates, the same question would have found the June
message, which talks about "Ruiz's 60 days" and the renewal, next to the March one, and the model
would have read the two in order. It doesn't take much reasoning to see that the second cancels the
first.

Memory for agents is usually told as an escalation. First, a model that forgets everything. Then
systems that extract facts, update them, organise them into graphs and consolidate them the way a
brain might. Each step adds a call to a model that rewrites what was said, and it rewrites it before
anyone has asked a question. In October 2026, a paper with a provocative title,
[*Is Complex Agentic Memory Always Needed?*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30755), compared twelve of
those systems with the most naive version possible: keep the conversations as they are and search
them with an ordinary search engine. The naive one came first in six of seven comparisons.

What follows covers why an agent needs memory, what kinds of memory there are, how a memory system
works inside, how the idea has evolved since 2023, how memory is measured, what that paper found,
and where structure still helps.

## What a model doesn't remember

A language model remembers nothing from one request to the next. Each time you ask it something, it
reads a text and writes an answer, and that's the end of it. When a chat seems to remember what was
said ten messages ago, it's because the application sends the whole conversation again on every
turn. The model doesn't read words but *tokens*, pieces of words (a page of text is about five
hundred), and the amount that fits in one request is called its **context window**. When the
conversation ends, nothing is left, unless someone has saved it.

For an assistant you use once, that's enough. For one that works with a team for months, it isn't.
Still less for an **agent**, a model that chains steps and uses tools on its own to complete a task,
because its tasks outlast a single conversation and what it learns in one is useful in the next.

The obvious fix is to send it everything that came before, with every request. That has three
problems. The first is size: if Lucía has three conversations a day of about 3,000 tokens each, a
working year comes to nearly two million, more than almost any model's window allows. The second is
cost: every question would pay to read the whole history, whether it needs it or not. And the third
is that fitting isn't the same as being read well. [LongMemEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.10813),
the most widely used memory benchmark, found in 2024 that models with long windows got 30% to 60%
fewer answers right when they read a history of about 115,000 tokens than when they read only the
sessions that held the answer. Why that happens, and how much cheaper reading everything can get, is
the subject of [this post on cache-augmented generation](/en/blog/cuando-no-hace-falta-buscar).

So the history is kept outside the model, and with each question the model is handed what it needs.
That is a **memory** for an agent. Mechanically, it's nothing more than the RAG pattern
(*Retrieval-Augmented Generation*): find the fragments that look most like the question and pass them
to the model along with it, as [this post on RAG and its variants](/en/blog/de-rag-a-rag-agentico)
explains. But the material differs from a RAG over documents in four ways.

**The agent writes it.** A company's documents are written by someone, with headings and a structure
meant to be read. A memory comes out of conversations: half-finished sentences, topics jumbled
together, something important in the last line of a chat about something else.

**It grows every day.** A document collection gets refreshed now and then. A memory adds every
conversation, forever.

**It changes.** A document collection usually holds the current version of each document. A history
holds every version of every fact, in the order they were said: the March agreement and its end in
June. Knowing which one holds means knowing when each thing was said.

**Who said it matters.** Lucía telling the assistant something isn't the same as the assistant saying
it, since the assistant may have got it wrong. LongMemEval measures the two separately.

## Four kinds of memory

In 2023, four researchers at Princeton University proposed [CoALA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02427)
(*Cognitive Architectures for Language Agents*), a framework for describing agents built on language
models. From cognitive psychology, and from the cognitive architectures that artificial intelligence
built on it from the 1980s onwards, they took four kinds of memory. Almost everyone uses them today.

| Memory | What it holds | At Albarán | Where it usually lives |
|---|---|---|---|
| Working | what's active right now | the current conversation, the result of the last search | the context |
| Episodic | what happened: what, when, with whom | the conversation of 18 June | an external store |
| Semantic | what is known about the world and oneself | "Talleres Ruiz is on the Team plan" | an external store, or the weights |
| Procedural | how things are done | "before answering about a refund, check the customer's plan" | the weights, the code, the instructions |

**Working memory** is what the agent has in front of it right now. In an agent built on a language
model, it's mostly the request's context, and it's small and expensive. **Episodic** memory holds
specific experiences, with their moment and the people involved. **Semantic** memory holds general
knowledge, already separated from the situation in which it was learned. And **procedural** memory
holds ways of doing things: part of it sits in the model's weights, learned in training, and part in
the agent's code and instructions. CoALA warns that letting an agent rewrite its procedural memory is
the riskiest of the four, because it can introduce bugs or sidestep what its designers intended.

There are three places to put all of this: in the weights, in the context, or in an external store.
The weights are the wrong place for specific facts: changing them means retraining, and there's no
way to check exactly what the model has learned. The context is working memory, and it has no room
for more. Everything else goes into an external store, and that's where all the design lies: what
gets written to it and how it's read.

The Talleres Ruiz failure has a name in this vocabulary. The conversation of 18 June was an episode,
and the memory turned it into a semantic fact: it kept the content and threw away the moment and the
link to what it already knew. A good part of the research on memory for agents is about that
conversion, from episodic to semantic, and about when it's worth making.

## Write, store, read and maintain

Every memory system, however complex, makes four decisions. LongMemEval described them as three
stages (indexing, retrieval and reading); to those you have to add maintenance, what happens to what
has been stored as new information arrives.

### Write: what to keep

The simplest option is to keep everything, as it is. The alternative is to have a model read each
conversation and keep what matters: loose facts, a summary or a note with keywords. Some systems
also score what they keep. The agents in [Generative Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03442), the
2023 work from Stanford and Google that opened the field, asked the model to rate each memory from 1
to 10 for importance: in its examples, tidying the room scored a 2, and asking someone out, an 8.

Extracting has obvious advantages. What's kept takes up less space, reads faster and can be given
structure. But whatever is thrown away at write time can't be recovered at read time, and it's
thrown away without knowing what will be asked. One of the recent papers on the subject sums it up by
calling every transformation a bet placed before the question is known. The June extraction bet that
what mattered was "back on the normal terms", and the September question needed "the 60 days".

### Store: in what form

What's written can be stored as text fragments, each with its date; as loose facts, each turned
into a vector that represents its meaning (an *embedding*) so it can be searched by similarity; as
notes linked to one another; as a graph of entities and relations; or in several tiers, some closer
to hand than others. Graphs that keep relations with dates, instead of deleting the old ones, get a
section of their own in [this post on GraphRAG](/en/blog/cuando-hace-falta-graphrag).

### Read: how it's found

To read, you first have to find, and the most common way is to search by similarity: turn the
question into an embedding and fetch the nearest memories. Generative Agents added two more
criteria. Each memory's score was the sum of three numbers between 0 and 1: how **recent** it was
(with an exponential decay of 0.995 for every hour of game time since it was last recalled), how
**important** it was (the 1 to 10 rating) and how **relevant** it was to the situation (the
similarity between embeddings). All three weighted equally.

There's room for refinement on top of that. A **reranker** is a second model that reads the question
and each candidate together, instead of comparing two vectors computed separately, and orders them
better at the cost of being slower, which is why it only gets the top hundred or two. Questions with
time references ("what we talked about last week") can be handled by restricting the search to a
date range. And instead of always searching the same way, the model can be given search as a tool,
so it decides when to search and what for.

### Maintain: update, consolidate and forget

If the memory holds facts, someone has to keep them up to date. The most widespread design is
[Mem0](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.19413)'s, a company and an open-source library that published its
method in 2025. After extracting the facts from a conversation, it searches, for each one, the stored
memories that look most like it, and asks a model to choose one of four operations: add it (ADD),
update an existing one (UPDATE), delete one that the new fact contradicts (DELETE), or do nothing
(NOOP).

```python
# Pseudocode: Mem0-style maintenance
def save(conversation):
    for fact in model.extract_facts(conversation):
        similar = memory.search(embed(fact))
        operation = model.decide(fact, similar)   # ADD, UPDATE, DELETE or NOOP
        memory.apply(operation)
```

That's what failed at Albarán. To delete or correct "60-day refund window" on reading "back on the
normal terms", the model had to know that the 60 days depended on the agreement: a relation between
two facts that wasn't written in either of them. Without it, the reasonable operation was ADD.

There are two other kinds of maintenance. **Consolidating** means summarising many memories into one
at a higher level. Generative Agents did it when the summed importance of an agent's latest
experiences passed 150: it then generated **reflections** ("Klaus is passionate about research"),
which were stored as one more memory and could, in turn, feed other reflections. **Forgetting**
means letting memories lose weight over time: [MemoryBank](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10250), also
from 2023, applied Ebbinghaus's forgetting curve, with memories that fade unless they're used and
grow stronger when they are. Forgetting is sometimes an obligation too, when someone asks for their
data to be deleted, and that gets harder once what has to go has already been copied into facts,
summaries and reflections.

## From Smallville to memory operating systems

Memory for agents built on language models is barely three and a half years old. Here is its
history in four stages, with the papers that get cited again and again, and a figure at the end
that sums it up.


### 2023: the ideas

In April, **Generative Agents** set twenty-five agents loose in Smallville, a town inspired by the
video game *The Sims*. Each agent had a **memory stream** holding everything it observed, written in
natural language, the recency, importance and relevance score to retrieve from it, and reflections
to summarise it. Almost everything that came after is a variation on one of those three pieces.

The same year brought two other uses of memory. [Reflexion](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11366) is a
method for letting an agent learn from its mistakes without retraining: when it fails a task, it
writes down in natural language what it did wrong, and that reflection is stored as episodic memory
and handed to it on the next attempt, so it doesn't make the same mistake twice. And
[Voyager](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16291), an agent that played *Minecraft*, stored a skill library
as procedural memory: programs it had written that had worked.

In October, a group at the University of California, Berkeley published
[MemGPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.08560), the most influential idea of the year. It treated the
model's context as a computer's main memory, its RAM, and the external store as its disk, and gave
the model itself functions to move data between the two. The context was split into three parts:
fixed instructions, a block of text that only the model could edit, through function calls, and a
queue of the latest messages. When the context passed 70% of the window, the system added a "memory
pressure" warning so the model would save what mattered. On reaching 100%, it took some of the
messages out of the queue, half the window for example, summarised them together with the previous
summary, and sent them to a store where they could still be searched. The project later carried on
as a company, under the name Letta.

### 2024: products and benchmarks

In February 2024, OpenAI started testing a memory in ChatGPT, which reached all its users in
September: the assistant kept facts about the person and put them into the context of every new
conversation. The same February saw the publication of [LoCoMo](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.17753),
the first benchmark of long conversations, and October, LongMemEval. The next two sections come back
to both.

### 2025: memory as a layer

In 2025, memory became a piece of infrastructure you buy or install, with companies devoted to it.
In January, Zep, a company that sells memory for agents as a service, published
[its product's architecture](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.13956): a knowledge graph in which every
relation carries the date it became true and, once it stops being true, the date it stopped, instead
of being deleted. In February, Rutgers University published [A-MEM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.12110)
(*Agentic Memory*), an open-source memory system that organises memories as a box of linked notes in
the style of the *Zettelkasten*, a German sociologist's note-taking method: each new memory is
written as a note with a description, keywords and tags, is linked to similar notes, and can cause
those to be rewritten.

In April, Mem0 published its method for extracting and maintaining facts, and measured it on LoCoMo.
Its main table says more than its abstract. Mem0 scored 66.88% according to a model acting as judge,
against 52.90% for ChatGPT's memory. But giving the model the whole conversation, about 26,000
tokens, scored 72.90%: more than any memory. What Mem0 gained was speed and cost. At the 95th
percentile, an answer with the whole conversation took 17.1 seconds, and with Mem0, 1.44.

In May came [MemOS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22101), a system that manages a model's memory the
way an operating system manages a computer's resources. It handles three different memories with the
same operations: text stored outside the model, the KV cache (the intermediate result a model keeps
after reading a text, so it doesn't have to read it again) and the weights themselves, and it moves
each memory from one to another depending on how it's used.

In August, researchers at Ludwig Maximilian University of Munich and elsewhere presented
[Memory-R1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.19828), a method for training the models that manage a
memory. Its name nods to DeepSeek-R1, the model that learned to reason through reinforcement
learning, and that's how it goes after maintenance's weak spot: it uses reinforcement learning to
train the model that chooses between adding, updating, deleting or nothing, rewarding it when the
final answer is right. Its example is the Talleres Ruiz failure in miniature: someone says they've adopted a dog, Buddy, and
later that they've adopted another one, Scout. An untrained model reads this as a contradiction: it
deletes the first memory and adds the second, and the memory is left with a single dog. The trained
model makes one update: "Andrew adopted two dogs, Buddy and Scout."

Products split into two philosophies. In April, ChatGPT also started drawing on every past
conversation, on top of the saved facts. In August, Claude, Anthropic's assistant, got the opposite
kind of memory: two tools the model calls when it thinks it needs them, one to search past
conversations and another to fetch the most recent ones, with no profile or summary prepared in
advance, and every search visible to the user. September also brought a memory with a summary you
can view and edit, first for businesses and in October for the individual paid plans. And at the end
of September, Anthropic added a memory tool to its API that consists of files: the model creates,
reads and edits files in a directory kept by whoever builds the application. Coding agents had been
doing something similar for a while, with instruction files written by people and, increasingly, by
the agent itself: procedural memory in plain text.

### 2026: proliferation, and a step back

In 2026, memory systems multiplied, many with operating-system names and each with its own
structure: EverMemOS organises memories by itself into structures meant for reasoning over long
periods, MemMachine keeps the original text alongside whatever it derives from it, and Memora
combines abstract memories with concrete ones. But the first papers asking how much of that
structure was needed also appeared.

In August, researchers at the University of Science and Technology of China and the company
MetaStone published [ReFind](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12888), a search interface for agents that
builds nothing on top of the conversations. It gives the agent a keyword search over the unmodified
history, with controls designed for chats (grouping by session, widening the context around a
result, narrowing by date, not rereading what it has already read), and leaves it to the agent to
decide what to search for and when to stop. On about 2,800 questions on precise retrieval and fact
tracking, it scored 58.2%, ahead of the best graph-based system's 53.2%, and on the short version of
LongMemEval it reached 93.2%. In October came the paper this post is about.

The field has been moving in two directions at once. One adds structure: graphs, notes, operating
systems, learned maintenance. The other takes it away: Claude's searches over unsummarised chats,
MemMachine, ReFind, and the October paper's naive RAG, which it calls Raw-RAG. The figure puts the systems together with the
frameworks, the products and the benchmarks, MemoryAgentBench and MEME included, which the next
section introduces. To know which direction is right, you first have to see how memory is measured.

## How memory is measured

A memory benchmark is a long history of conversations and a list of questions whose answers are in
it. The four that matter for this post measure different things.

**LoCoMo** (2024), in its published version, has ten very long conversations between two fictional
people, each of about 300 turns and up to 35 sessions. They were written by agents given a
personality and a list of events, and corrected by people to keep them consistent. The questions
are single-hop (one fact), multi-hop (combining facts from different sessions), temporal and
open-domain. It's the benchmark most systems cite, and also the easiest: a whole conversation fits
in the window of any current model.

**LongMemEval** (2024) has 500 questions, each with its own history, measuring five abilities:
extracting a fact, reasoning across several sessions, reasoning about time, keeping the updated
information when something changes, and abstaining when the answer isn't there. The short version
has about 115,000 tokens per question; the long one, 500 sessions and close to a million and a half.
Its authors didn't stop at measuring: they tested every design decision in a memory. Storing
conversations by rounds (a question and its answer) worked better than by whole sessions. Replacing
the messages with summaries or extracted facts made the answers worse, because of the information
lost, except on questions that span several sessions. And using the extracted facts not as content
but as search **keys**, pointing to the original message, improved retrieval by 9.4% and accuracy
by 5.4%. In 2024, the most cited benchmark was already saying that facts are for finding, not for
replacing.

[**MemoryAgentBench**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.05257) (2025), from the University of California,
San Diego, measures four competencies: retrieving accurately, learning during use (applying examples
seen in the conversation, without changing the weights), understanding a long text as a whole, and
resolving conflicts between old and new information.

[**MEME**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12477) (2026), from a group including researchers at KAIST and
the University of Tübingen, measures what the others barely touched: facts that change and depend
on one another. It has six tasks, and two of them are exactly the Talleres Ruiz case. In a
**cascade**, a fact changes and a rule has to be applied to work out the new value of another fact
that depended on it. In an **absence**, a fact changes and you have to recognise that the value
depending on it can no longer be trusted, even though nobody has said what the new one is. The six
memory systems they tested collapsed: 3% correct on average on cascades and 1% on absences. Only an
agent that kept its memory in files, with Claude Opus 4.7 as its model, closed part of the gap, at
about seventy times the cost.

These numbers need the caution that any benchmark deserves, covered in
[this post on what a benchmark really measures](/en/blog/bancos-de-pruebas). The conversations are
synthetic. Many answers are scored by another model acting as judge. The collections are small:
LoCoMo is ten conversations. And there's a problem peculiar to this field: each system publishes
its numbers next to everyone else's, configured by itself. When Mem0 compared itself with RAG, the
RAG retrieved one or two fragments per question, and topped out at 61%.

## Is all that machinery needed?

The paper behind this post is by Zhengye Bao, Shengtao Zhang, Haokai Zhang, Shiqi Lou, Muning Wen
and Minnan Luo, and it came out on 22 October 2026. Its question is the one in its title: when is
the added complexity of agentic memory needed? To answer it, they built the simplest version they
could think of and called it **Raw-RAG**, RAG over the raw history.

### Naive RAG, done properly

Raw-RAG uses no model to write. Each conversation is stored as text, with each message's date (and,
if there are images, their descriptions), cut into windows of 256 tokens that overlap by 32, and
each window is turned into an embedding. At question time, it searches for the 128 windows most
similar to the question, a reranker picks the best 15, and the model that answers, which they call
the **reader**, gets them along with the question. It's the reader, not the memory, that has to
work out what happened first, what changed and what holds now.

```python
# At save time: nothing to decide
def save(message, date, author):
    history.append(f"[{date}] {author}: {message}")
    index.update(windows(history, tokens=256, overlap=32))

# At question time
def recall(question):
    candidates = index.search(embed(question), k=128)
    best = reranker.rank(question, candidates)[:15]
    return model(prompt(question, best))
```

Compared with the Mem0 pseudocode, what disappears is everything that happens at save time. What's
added, compared with many baseline RAGs in earlier papers, is two things: searching broadly (128
candidates, not one or two) and a reranker. The authors reviewed fourteen papers and found that
several of their RAG baselines cut the text into fragments that were too large, retrieved too few,
or didn't rerank.

### The results

They compared it with twelve systems (EverMemOS, MemOS, EMem, SimpleMem, MemU, MemMachine, Mem0,
Memora, Nemori, MemoryOS, A-MEM and RecMem) on LoCoMo, LongMemEval, MEME and MemoryAgentBench, with
several models as reader: OpenAI's GPT-4o-mini and GPT-4.1-mini, and DeepSeek-V4-Flash. On LoCoMo and
LongMemEval they reproduced the conditions of the published comparisons; on MEME and
MemoryAgentBench they ran every system under the same procedure.

| Benchmark | Reader | Raw-RAG | Best memory system | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| LoCoMo | GPT-4o-mini | 89.07 | MemMachine, 88.12 | +0.95 |
| LoCoMo | GPT-4.1-mini | 93.70 | EverMemOS, 93.05 | +0.65 |
| LongMemEval | GPT-4.1-mini | 87.53 | Memora, 87.40 | +0.13 |
| MEME | GPT-4o-mini | 39.30 | MemMachine, 37.06 | +2.24 |
| MEME | DeepSeek-V4-Flash | 43.19 | MemMachine, 44.71 | −1.52 |
| MemoryAgentBench | GPT-4o-mini | 48.32 | Memora, 43.84 | +4.48 |
| MemoryAgentBench | DeepSeek-V4-Flash | 53.41 | Memora, 47.88 | +5.53 |

Raw-RAG wins six of the seven. On LoCoMo and LongMemEval it's close, under a point, which means those
two benchmarks no longer tell systems apart very well. On the two harder ones the margin is wider:
up to five and a half points on MemoryAgentBench. And it loses one, MEME with DeepSeek, where giving
the reader the whole history also scores almost twenty points more than any memory.

### Why it wins

The most interesting part of the paper isn't the table but the explanation. A correct answer takes
two things: the memory has to bring the reader enough evidence, and the reader has to use it well.
The authors measured the two separately. **Coverage** is the percentage of questions for which the
memory brings back enough evidence to answer. **Utilisation** is the percentage of those questions
the reader gets right.

On MemoryAgentBench, the systems' coverage runs from 19% to 45%, and utilisation only from 80% to
90%. On MEME, coverage varies by 41 points and utilisation by 21. Systems differ mainly in whether
they find what's needed, and Raw-RAG finds it most often on both. Its readers use the evidence a
little worse than other systems' readers, perhaps because they read raw text rather than clean
facts, but they have it more often, and that counts for more.

The second explanation is the one closest to Talleres Ruiz. In MEME, knowing the current value of
something takes more than finding the value: you need the **path** that leads to it, the order of
the changes or the rule linking one fact to another. Among the questions where each system found the
value it was after, Raw-RAG also brought back the complete path 62.34% of the time, MemMachine (which
also keeps the original text) 56.70%, and MemU, which transforms everything, 9.62%. An extracted fact
can keep the value, "60 days", and lose the path: where it came from and what cancelled it.

The third explanation is the hardest to get around. The authors gave each system the best possible
access to what it had stored: knowing the answer, they picked the most useful items from its whole
store, to rule out the search as the problem. The two systems that keep the original text, Raw-RAG
and MemMachine, reached 57.54% and 57.46% on MEME, close to the 61.60% the reader scored with the
exact original evidence. Those that transform it stayed, with the same perfect access and no space
limit, between 7.58 and 29.38 points below. What's lost at write time, no search can bring back.

### What the updates don't do

Before all that, the authors audited Mem0. On LoCoMo, of 33,902 write decisions, only 4.39% were
updates or deletions; on LongMemEval, 2.46% of 428,858. Many were duplicates or malformed, and the
ones that actually changed some stored text came to 1.90% and 1.34%. Then they tried forbidding
them: turning every update into an addition and every deletion into nothing. Accuracy on LoCoMo rose
from 67.36% to 69.42%, and didn't fall in any category. Temporal questions gained 3.32 points.
Without updates, the memory keeps every version, and the reader can see which is the latest.

What if the reader struggles to resolve the changes? To check, they gave it, on MEME, exactly the
messages it needed, in order, with no retrieval errors. DeepSeek-V4-Flash got 82.32% of the cascades
and 91.54% of the absences right. GPT-4o-mini, 1.83% of the cascades. A capable reader resolves the
changes from the text; a weak one doesn't, even with the text in front of it.

### What the structure doesn't do

On Raw-RAG itself, they tried adding what complex systems add, changing nothing else, on
MemoryAgentBench. Replacing the windows with atomic facts cut the score by 19.62 points. Adding
summaries cut it by 5.81, and widening the search by following a graph, by 5.78. In the other
direction, removing the reranker cost 3.92 points, and fetching a single item instead of fifteen,
11.73. What matters is searching well and bringing back enough; the exact window size, from 128 to
1,024 tokens, made no difference.

And then there's the cost. On ten MEME episodes, preparing the memory and answering 110 questions
took Raw-RAG 306 seconds, not counting writing the answers. The next fastest, EMem, took 1,562, five
times as long. EverMemOS took 9,301, thirty times as long. With a conversation of 1,710 messages
approaching a million tokens, storing it took Raw-RAG 0.33 hours, and Memora, 26.95. Writing with a
model is expensive, and complex memory does it with every message.

## Where structure does help

The paper doesn't say complex memories are useless, and its own data points to where they can help.

**With weak readers.** Raw-RAG leaves all the work of reconstructing the state to the reader, and the
gap between DeepSeek and GPT-4o-mini on cascades, from 82% to 2%, shows how much depends on it. A
system that maintains state at write time does that work once, instead of on every question, and can
help a small model. The authors leave it as an open question.

**Where raw does worse.** On LongMemEval, Memora beats Raw-RAG on updated-information questions
(97.40 against 91.03) and on temporal ones (89.50 against 81.45). And on MEME's deletion tasks, where
someone asks for a fact to be forgotten and is later asked about it, Raw-RAG trails several systems,
and it's easy to see why: the original text still contains what was meant to be forgotten, and the
reader finds it.

**For state that others use.** Benchmarks only ask questions. A memory in production may have to hand
another tool the current value of something, trigger an action when something changes, or really
delete a piece of data. That takes explicit state, not a history to be interpreted.

**In what wasn't tested.** The tests are recorded conversations, with no agents acting in an
environment. There's no procedural memory, like Voyager's skill library, no histories spanning
several years, and no limited storage. A raw history grows without end, and every question pays
again to reason over it.

**And on privacy.** Keeping everything as it is means also keeping what should never have been kept.
The authors mention it as a limitation. A memory of facts can, at least in theory, keep only what's
needed; a raw history requires deciding separately what's kept, for how long, and who can read it.

## What comes next

With the October paper and August's ReFind, a consensus is starting to take shape, and it has four
parts.

**Naive RAG, done properly, is the baseline.** The paper's conclusion is a demand about method: any
new memory system should show that it beats RAG over the raw history, with a broad search and a
reranker, and explain what its complexity adds. Until now, several compared themselves with a RAG
that fetched one or two fragments.

**The original text is the source of truth, and what's derived is an index.** That's what
LongMemEval measured in 2024 and what MemMachine and Claude's searches do: facts, summaries and graphs
help to find things, but each one points to the message it came from, and the reader reads the
message. That way, a wrong extraction makes something harder to find, but doesn't erase it.

**The agent decides how to search.** MemGPT already gave the model functions to manage its memory,
and ReFind and Claude's memory give it a search over the history. With models getting steadily
better at using tools, searching in several rounds, narrowing by date or reading the conversation
around a result are decisions the model can make on the spot, once it knows the question. It's the
same evolution that led from naive RAG to agentic RAG.

**State that governs doesn't belong in memory.** The Talleres Ruiz agreement should never have lived
in an assistant's memory. A commercial agreement with a customer is company data, and it belongs in
the system where customers are managed, where the assistant would look it up with a tool and where
sales would have closed it in June. Memory is good for what was said; for what is, there's a
database.

The question of scale is still open. With ever longer context windows and caching that makes
rereading cheaper, giving the model the whole history gains ground wherever it fits, as the MEME
result with DeepSeek showed. With histories spanning years, no window is enough, and we'll need to
know whether a well-built derived index, or learned maintenance like Memory-R1's, is worth its cost
compared with searching the original text better. Today's benchmarks aren't long enough to tell yet.

## When to use which

The choice doesn't depend on the system's name, but on what has to be remembered and what for.

| If what has to be remembered… | At Albarán | Use |
|---|---|---|
| is what was said in conversations | what Lucía has told it about each customer | the raw history, with date and author, a broad search and a reranker |
| is state other systems also use | the plan, the charges, the Talleres Ruiz agreement | its own system, queried with a tool; not the memory |
| is a stable preference that always applies | Lucía prefers short answers, with the section cited | a short, visible, editable profile in the instructions |
| is a procedure | how a refund is processed | an instructions file that someone reviews |
| spans many conversations | how often has the export bug come up since July? | facts or summaries as an index, pointing to the original messages |
| has to be forgettable | a customer asks for their data to be deleted | delete it from the history and from everything derived from it |

Before building anything more complex, it's worth running the test the paper suggests: take fifty
real questions, including things that changed over time, and compare your memory with a RAG over the
raw history, with the same model as reader. If the RAG gets as many right, keep it. If it fails, look
at why: if it didn't find the message, the problem is the search; if it found it and the reader
didn't understand it, the problem is the reader.

## What to take away

If you take away only one thing, make it this: **memory for agents is RAG over what the agent has
lived through, and almost all of its complexity consists of transforming that experience before
knowing what will be asked.** Every transformation is a bet, and in the 2026 comparisons most of them
lose: what's thrown away at write time, no search can bring back. For Albarán, the memory ends up
like this.

And if you take away three more, make them practical.

**Keep what was said, with date and author.** If you extract facts or summaries, use them to find
things and make them point to the original message. The reader has to be able to read what was said
and when, because knowing what holds now depends on it.

**Search broadly and rerank.** The difference between a RAG that loses and one that wins was fetching
128 candidates and keeping the best 15, not one or two. Before adding structure, check that the
search brings back what's needed.

**Separate what was said from what is.** Preferences and procedures in visible, editable text. The
data that runs the business in its own systems. Memory for everything else.
