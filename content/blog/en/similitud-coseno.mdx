If you've ever built a semantic search engine, you chose cosine similarity without ever really
choosing it. A search engine like that turns every text into an *embedding*, a vector of numbers a
model uses to represent it, then compares the question with each document using **cosine
similarity**: the cosine of the angle between the two vectors. It equals $1$ when they point the
same way and falls as they diverge, and the engine returns the texts with the highest values. It's
the default in almost every vector database (the kind that stores vectors and returns the ones most
similar to a given vector), the measure [OpenAI recommends](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/embeddings)
for its embeddings, and the one RAG tutorials take for granted. (*Retrieval-augmented generation*
means finding the passages most similar to a question and handing them to the model so it can answer
from them.)

Many libraries, though, give you not the similarity but its reverse, **one minus the cosine**, so
that a smaller value means "closer", as with any distance. SciPy, Python's scientific library, calls
it ["Cosine distance"](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.spatial.distance.cosine.html).
In [pgvector](https://github.com/pgvector/pgvector), PostgreSQL's vector extension, it's what the
`<=>` operator returns. In scikit-learn, the machine learning library, you ask for it with
`metric="cosine"`. Sorting $1 - \cos\theta$ from smallest to largest is the same as sorting
$\cos\theta$ from largest to smallest, so the search returns the same results either way. What
changes is the name: the similarity, turned inside out, is presented as a distance, and in
scikit-learn even as a metric.

Take three vectors of length one leaving the same point. A points horizontally, C points vertically,
and B sits right in between at 45°. The cosine distance, $1 - \cos\theta$, is $0.29$ between A and B
and another $0.29$ between B and C, but between A and C it's $1$. Going from A to C by way of B costs
$0.59$; going straight costs $1$.

No distance worth the name allows that. The direct route can't be longer than a detour: that's the
triangle inequality, and the cosine distance doesn't satisfy it. So two things need to be kept apart.
Cosine similarity, which ranks the results, isn't a distance at all; it's a score of resemblance.
And the cosine distance built from it isn't a metric.

And yet the cosine is the right choice almost every time. That leaves two questions. The first is
why it has won out over the dozens of ways of comparing two vectors. The second is what it costs
that its distance isn't a metric: nothing in a search, but quite a lot in some other operations.
Along the way we'll meet the measures it competes with and the cases where it stops being the right
tool.

## An embedding is a vector

An *embedding* is a vector a model uses to represent a piece of text: a word, a sentence, a whole
document. A vector of $d$ dimensions is just $d$ numbers, and current models use hundreds or
thousands (384, 768, 1,536 and 3,072 are common sizes). The model is trained so that texts with
similar meanings get similar vectors. A vector of $d$ numbers is a point in $d$-dimensional space,
and it's usually drawn as an arrow from the origin to that point.

Two properties of a vector matter here. The first is its **length**, or *norm*:

$$
\lVert a \rVert = \sqrt{a_1^2 + a_2^2 + \dots + a_d^2}.
$$

The second is its **direction**: where it points.

To compare two vectors, the basic tool is the *dot product*: multiply coordinate by coordinate and
add up the results. It has a geometric reading that ties it to the angle $\theta$ between the two
vectors:

$$
\begin{aligned}
a \cdot b &= a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + \dots + a_d b_d \\
&= \lVert a \rVert \, \lVert b \rVert \cos\theta .
\end{aligned}
$$

Solving for the cosine gives **cosine similarity**:

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{a \cdot b}{\lVert a \rVert \, \lVert b \rVert}.
$$

It's $1$ when the two vectors point the same way, $0$ when they're perpendicular and $-1$ when they
point in opposite directions. And it doesn't change if you stretch or shrink either vector: dividing
by the lengths is precisely what removes them. The vectors $(1, 2)$ and $(2, 4)$ have a cosine of
$1$, even though the second is twice as long and the distance between their tips is
$\sqrt{5} \approx 2.24$.

That distance between the tips is the other classic comparison, the **Euclidean distance**:
$\lVert a - b \rVert$, the length of the vector that runs from one tip to the other. It sees
direction and length together. The cosine sees only direction and throws away whatever was stored in
the length. That act of discarding is the whole story: it explains both why the cosine is almost
always the right choice and what it costs.

## Similarity, distance and metric

Three words that are often used as synonyms actually name three different things.

A **similarity** is any score that grows the more two things resemble each other. It promises
nothing else: no maximum value, not even that something is most similar to itself. The cosine is a
similarity.

A **distance**, in the broad sense libraries use, is the opposite: a score that grows the more two
things differ. Any similarity becomes one when you flip it, and $1 - \cos\theta$ is the "cosine
distance" of SciPy and pgvector. It runs from $0$ (same direction) to $2$ (opposite directions).

A **metric** is a distance that obeys four rules. Here the word has its geometric sense, not the
sense of evaluation metrics such as a classifier's accuracy. For any $x$, $y$, $z$:

1. $d(x, y) \ge 0$. No distance is negative.
2. $d(x, y) = 0$ only if $x = y$. A distance of zero means "they're the same".
3. $d(x, y) = d(y, x)$. It doesn't matter where you measure from.
4. $d(x, z) \le d(x, y) + d(y, z)$. The **triangle inequality**: the direct route is never longer
   than a detour.

The fourth does the most work. It means that knowing two distances bounds the third: if $x$ is close
to $y$ and $y$ is close to $z$, then $x$ can't be far from $z$. Everything that reasons about
distances without computing them all relies on it.

Now let's check $1 - \cos\theta$ against the rules. It meets the first, because the cosine never
exceeds $1$, and the third, because the angle from $a$ to $b$ is the same as from $b$ to $a$. The
second fails: $(1, 2)$ and $(2, 4)$ are different vectors, yet their cosine distance is $0$. At best
it compares directions, not vectors. That one has an easy fix: if you first divide each vector by its
length (**normalising** them, so that they all have length one), two vectors with the same direction
are the same vector.

The fourth fails even then, as the opening example showed. The three vectors have length one, and
$1 - \cos 45^\circ = 1 - \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \approx 0.29$. The detour adds up to $0.59$ while the
direct route measures $1$.

So $1 - \cos\theta$ is a distance in the broad sense, not a metric. The plain cosine isn't even a
distance; it's a similarity. With this vocabulary, the library names from the opening make more
sense. SciPy and pgvector are right to call $1 - \cos\theta$ a distance, in the broad sense.
scikit-learn asks for it through a parameter called `metric`, but there the word just means
"comparison function", not the four rules. The library that says it outright is FAISS, Meta's
library for searching among millions of vectors: its
[documentation](https://github.com/facebookresearch/faiss/wiki/MetricType-and-distances) makes clear
that the cosine is a similarity, not a distance, and it doesn't even offer it as an option. To search
by cosine, you normalise the vectors and search by dot product (`METRIC_INNER_PRODUCT`).

## Two true metrics that rank the same way

Two true metrics are hiding inside the cosine, and both appear when you normalise. If $\hat a$ and
$\hat b$ are $a$ and $b$ divided by their lengths, their tips lie on the sphere of radius one and the
cosine is simply their dot product: $\cos\theta = \hat a \cdot \hat b$.

The first is the **angle**, $\theta = \arccos(\hat a \cdot \hat b)$. On the sphere of radius one, the
angle is the length of the shortest arc between the two tips without leaving the surface, the route a
plane flies between two cities. That distance does satisfy the triangle inequality: with our three
vectors, $45^\circ + 45^\circ = 90^\circ$. The detour through B ties with the direct route because B
lies exactly halfway along it, and it can never beat it.

The second is the **chord**, the Euclidean distance between the normalised vectors. Its relation to
the cosine takes one line: expand the square and use $\hat a \cdot \hat a = \hat b \cdot \hat b = 1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lVert \hat a - \hat b \rVert^2 &= \hat a \cdot \hat a - 2\, \hat a \cdot \hat b + \hat b \cdot \hat b \\
&= 2 - 2\cos\theta .
\end{aligned}
$$

So $\lVert \hat a - \hat b \rVert = \sqrt{2 - 2\cos\theta}$, which is a metric because the Euclidean
distance is one.

Read backwards, the same identity explains the failure from the opening:

$$
1 - \cos\theta = \tfrac{1}{2} \lVert \hat a - \hat b \rVert^2 .
$$

The "cosine distance" is half the **square** of the Euclidean distance between the normalised
vectors, and squaring breaks the triangle inequality. On a line, the points $0$, $1$ and $2$ are
separated by distances of $1$, $1$ and $2$; squared, those become $1$, $1$ and $4$, and
$4 > 1 + 1$. Squaring penalises large distances more than small ones, so one long trip costs more
than two half trips. You can see it in the numbers for small angles: $1 - \cos\theta \approx
\theta^2/2$, so two steps of $10^\circ$ cost $0.015$ each, $0.030$ together, while the $20^\circ$ in
one go costs $0.060$, twice as much.

The important consequence is in the right-hand chart: all three measures always grow with the angle.
A larger angle means a larger chord, a larger $1 - \cos\theta$ and a smaller cosine, so all three
rank any list of candidates in exactly the same way. Searching for the ten closest texts by cosine,
by angle or by Euclidean distance between normalised vectors gives you the same ten, in the same
order. That's what OpenAI's guide says: its embeddings come normalised, so cosine and Euclidean
distance produce the same ranking.

For searching, which is almost always what the cosine is used for, not being a metric doesn't matter
at all. It matters when you do something with the numbers other than sort them.

## When it does matter

There are three situations: when an algorithm uses the triangle inequality to save computation, when
vectors get averaged, and when similarities are chained together.

### Indexes that rule out candidates without looking

Some exact nearest-neighbour indexes, such as the *ball tree*, group the points into balls with a
centre and a radius, and can rule out a whole ball with a single computation. If the query is at
distance $D$ from the centre and the ball has radius $r$, then by the triangle inequality everything
inside is at least $D - r$ from the query. If $D - r$ already exceeds the best candidate found so
far, the ball is skipped without looking inside. With a distance that doesn't satisfy the inequality,
that bound is false and the index would throw away true neighbours.

scikit-learn knows this. Neither its `BallTree` nor its `KDTree` accepts the cosine, and when you ask
for `NearestNeighbors(metric="cosine")` with the automatic algorithm,
[it falls back to brute force](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/neighbors.html): comparing the
query with everything. The fix is the one from the previous section. Normalise the vectors and ask
for the Euclidean distance, and the tree works, with the same ordering.

Graph indexes such as HNSW (*Hierarchical Navigable Small World*, which many vector databases use)
don't prune with the triangle inequality. They walk a graph of neighbours, always jumping to the most
promising one, and they're approximate anyway. That's why [hnswlib](https://github.com/nmslib/hnswlib),
the open-source library maintained by HNSW's first author, offers the dot product and the cosine
alongside the Euclidean distance, with a warning that the dot product is not a metric.

### Averaging

k-means groups points into $k$ clusters by repeating two steps: assign each point to the nearest
centre, then move each centre to the mean of its points. It's built for the Euclidean distance, and
averaging directions has a catch: the mean of vectors of length one doesn't have length one. Two
perpendicular vectors, $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$, average to $(0.5, 0.5)$, which has length $0.71$, and
the more spread out a cluster is, the shorter its centre.

If you run plain k-means on normalised vectors, the assignment step ends up comparing each point with
centres of different lengths. Since $\lVert \hat x - c \rVert^2 = 1 - 2\, \hat x \cdot c + \lVert c \rVert^2$,
the last term favours the clusters with the shortest centres, the most spread-out ones, for a reason
that has nothing to do with resemblance. Spherical k-means
([Dhillon and Modha, 2001](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1007612920971)) fixes this by normalising each
centre again after averaging. The same goes for any average of embeddings: the mean of normalised
vectors has to be normalised again before you compare it with anything.

### Chaining

"A resembles B and B resembles C, so A resembles C." It's the reasoning of anyone who deduplicates a
corpus, clusters by chains or recommends friends of friends. With a metric, the conclusion comes with
a bound. With the cosine too, as long as you go through the angle, which is a metric.

Suppose $\cos(A, B) \ge s$ and $\cos(B, C) \ge s$, with $s \ge 0$. Each of the two angles is at most
$\arccos s$. By the triangle inequality for angles,

$$
\theta_{AC} \le \theta_{AB} + \theta_{BC} \le 2 \arccos s .
$$

The cosine decreases between $0^\circ$ and $180^\circ$, so a larger angle gives a smaller cosine, and
the double-angle formula, $\cos 2\alpha = 2\cos^2\alpha - 1$, finishes the calculation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\cos(A, C) &\ge \cos(2 \arccos s) \\
&= 2 \cos^2(\arccos s) - 1 \\
&= 2s^2 - 1 .
\end{aligned}
$$

With $s = 0.9$, $0.62$ is guaranteed. With $s = 0.8$, it's $0.28$. With $s = 0.7$, it's $-0.02$:
nothing. A and C can be perpendicular, completely unrelated, even though each step looks fairly
similar. The bound is tight, because it's reached when B lies exactly in the middle of the arc
between A and C, which is what happened with our three vectors: $s = \cos 45^\circ \approx 0.71$ and
$2s^2 - 1 = 0$.

A threshold of $0.7$ for "similar" doesn't compose. Two hops of $0.7$ can join texts that have
nothing to do with each other, and a clustering method that merges groups through chains (*single
linkage*) with that threshold ends up connecting anything to anything, given enough intermediate
steps.

## Why the cosine won

If the cosine, the angle and the Euclidean distance between normalised vectors all rank things the
same way, the question isn't why the cosine rather than the Euclidean distance. It's why we throw the
length away. There are four reasons, plus a fifth that gets repeated a lot and doesn't hold up.

### The legacy of information retrieval

In 1975, Salton, Wong and Yang described the
[vector space model](https://doi.org/10.1145/361219.361220) for searching documents: each document
is a vector with one coordinate per term, weighted by its importance (what would later be called
TF-IDF, *term frequency–inverse document frequency*: how many times the word appears in the document,
times how rare it is in the collection). On the first page they already propose comparing documents
by the angle between their vectors and normalising them all to length one, placing them on the
sphere.

The reason was practical. A long document repeats more words, so its vector is longer, and with the
dot product long documents would win every search just for being long. The cosine removes the length,
which in that setting was an accident of the text, not a property of its topic. When
[word2vec](https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.3781), the first popular word-vector model, arrived in 2013,
the habit came with it: its authors already solved analogies by looking for the closest word
"measured by cosine distance". Language processing inherited the measure from search engines.

### Length measures something else

In word vectors, length isn't meaning. [Schakel and Wilson](https://arxiv.org/abs/1508.02297) studied
it with word2vec in 2015 and found that it mixes two things: how frequent a word is and how
consistent its contexts are. A word that always shows up in the same kind of sentence gets a longer
vector the more often it appears. One that shows up in very different contexts, like "the" or "of",
ends up with a short vector, because each context pulls it in a different direction and the pulls
cancel out. They propose using length to measure how much a word matters in a corpus. That's useful
information, but it isn't resemblance: with the dot product, two words would come out closer because
they're important, not because they mean the same thing. The cosine asks only where each vector
points.

### Models are trained with the cosine

This is the strongest reason today, and it's circular in a good way: embedding models are trained
with the cosine inside the loss function. [Sentence-BERT](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.10084) (2019),
the model that popularised sentence embeddings, promised in its abstract vectors that can be compared
with the cosine. Contrastive training, the current standard, pulls together the pairs that should be
similar and pushes the rest apart, measuring with the cosine divided by a temperature, a number that
sets how sharp the contrast is: $0.05$ in [SimCSE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08821), a contrastive
method from 2021. [Wang and Isola](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.10242) (2020) formalised the idea: the
vectors are placed on the sphere of radius one and training pursues two goals, *alignment* (similar
pairs together) and *uniformity* (everything else spread over the whole sphere).

A model trained like this never receives any signal about length, because the loss doesn't see it.
Length is noise, and many models remove it themselves: OpenAI's embeddings come out already
normalised. With them, the cosine, the dot product and the Euclidean distance are interchangeable,
and the dot product is the cheapest. The general rule is that the right measure is the one the model
was trained with.

### It's cheap

You normalise once, at indexing time, and from then on each cosine is a dot product: $d$
multiplications and $d$ additions. Comparing a query with a million documents is a single
matrix-vector product, which is what graphics cards and linear algebra libraries do best. And since
the result lies between $-1$ and $1$, the vectors compress well, for example to 8-bit integers.

The angle has its own shortcut, too. [Charikar](https://doi.org/10.1145/509907.509965) showed in 2002
that if you cut space with a random plane through the origin, the probability that two vectors fall on
different sides is $\theta/\pi$. With 256 random planes, each vector becomes 256 bits, and the
fraction of bits in which two vectors differ estimates their angle divided by $\pi$. The technique is
called SimHash, and it estimates the angle, which is a metric, not $1 - \cos\theta$.

### What doesn't explain it: the curse of dimensionality

A common explanation is that in many dimensions the Euclidean distance loses its meaning while the
cosine doesn't. The first half has a basis: [Beyer and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1007/3-540-49257-7_15)
proved in 1999 that, under fairly general conditions, as the dimension grows the point nearest to a
query and the farthest one end up at almost the same distance. The second half can't be true for
normalised vectors: if the cosine and the Euclidean distance rank things the same way, they suffer
equally.

The cosine concentrates too. Between two random directions in $d$ dimensions, the cosine has mean $0$
and standard deviation $1/\sqrt{d}$. In 768 dimensions that's $0.036$, so almost every pair of random
vectors is almost perpendicular. What saves embeddings isn't the measure but the fact that their
points aren't random: they're crowded into a small, structured part of the space.

So the real choice is whether to keep the length or throw it away. In almost all text embeddings, the
length is noise (the model never learnt to use it) or measures something we don't want to compare
(how frequent or important a word is, how long a document is). That's why we use the cosine.

## The other measures

The table summarises the ones that appear in language processing, what they are and whether they see
length.

| measure | what it is | sees length? | where it appears |
|---|---|---|---|
| cosine | similarity, from $-1$ to $1$ | no | almost every text embedding model |
| angle, $\arccos(\cos\theta)$ | metric | no | SimHash; when a true metric is needed |
| dot product | unbounded similarity | yes | DPR, recommender systems |
| Euclidean | metric | yes, except between normalised vectors | tree indexes, k-means |
| Manhattan | metric: sum of absolute differences | yes | rarely with embeddings |
| Mahalanobis | metric that discounts the correlations between coordinates | yes | whitening embeddings |
| Pearson | similarity, from $-1$ to $1$ | no | the cosine after subtracting each vector's mean coordinate |
| Jaccard | $1 - J$ is a metric; compares sets | n/a | deduplicating corpora (with MinHash) |
| Hamming | metric: counts differing bits | n/a | binary codes, SimHash |
| Jensen–Shannon | divergence between distributions; its square root is a metric | n/a | comparing word or topic distributions |
| *cross-encoder* | learnt, no geometry | n/a | reranking results |

Three of them deserve a paragraph.

**The dot product** is the one that keeps the length, and it's the right choice when length is
information. In a recommender system, the dot product of a user's vector and an item's vector is the
prediction, and the length of an item's vector tends to capture how popular it is, which is a good
reason to recommend it. [DPR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906) (*Dense Passage Retrieval*, 2020), a
passage retriever for answering questions, was trained with the dot product, and in its own tests the
Euclidean distance performed similarly and both beat the cosine, though with settings tuned for the
dot product. It isn't even a well-behaved similarity, though: a vector can resemble another more
than it resembles itself. With $(1, 0)$ and $(2, 0)$, the product of the first with itself is $1$
and with the second is $2$.

**The Mahalanobis distance** is the Euclidean distance after *whitening* the data: transforming it so
that the coordinates are uncorrelated and all have the same variance. It's the link to the fix in the
next section.

**Cross-encoders** play a different game. All the measures above compare two vectors computed
separately, with each text encoded on its own. A cross-encoder reads both texts at once and returns a
score, so it sees every word of one against every word of the other, and it's usually more accurate.
The price is that nothing can be computed in advance: each pair requires a full pass through the
model. That's why the usual pattern is to use the cosine to pull a hundred candidates out of millions
and the cross-encoder to rerank those hundred. The cosine isn't the best similarity there is. It's
the best one that fits in an index.

## Where the cosine falls short

First, a distinction that saves arguments: a failure of the measure is not the same as a failure of
the representation. "Hot" and "cold" have a high cosine in many word models, and so do a sentence and
its negation. That isn't the cosine's fault. Those words appear in the same contexts, the model
placed them close together, and the cosine faithfully reports what the model learnt. No measure can
fix a representation that doesn't tell two things apart; that gets fixed in training.

Three failures do belong to the measure.

### When everything points the same way

In 2019, [Ethayarajh](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.00512) measured the average cosine between randomly
chosen words inside language models such as BERT (Google, 2018) and GPT-2 (OpenAI, 2019), using the
vector each layer of the model assigns to each word. In GPT-2 it was around $0.6$ in layers 2 to 8,
and in the last layer almost $1$: any two words had an almost perfect cosine. In BERT's last layer it
was around $0.46$. This is called *anisotropy*: the vectors don't fill the whole sphere but a narrow
cone, and everything resembles everything.

[Timkey and van Schijndel](https://aclanthology.org/2021.emnlp-main.372) found part of the cause in
2021: a few *rogue dimensions* with huge values. In BERT's layer 11, a single coordinate out of 768
contributes 88% of the expected cosine between two vectors; in XLNet, another 2019 model, 99.6%. In
those cases the cosine is measuring a single coordinate.

This happens above all when you take vectors from a language model that wasn't trained to compare
them. The fixes, from cheapest to most expensive: centring (subtracting the collection's mean from
every vector; [Mu and Viswanath](https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.01417) also remove the few dominant
directions), standardising each coordinate, which is Timkey and van Schijndel's fix,
[whitening](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.15316) (which amounts to comparing with the Mahalanobis
distance), or using a model trained with a contrastive loss, whose uniformity pushes directly against
the cone.

Even good models have their own scale. The model card for
[E5](https://huggingface.co/intfloat/e5-base-v2), a family of embedding models, warns that its
cosines usually fall between $0.7$ and $1$ because of its training temperature ($0.01$), and that
what matters is the relative order. A cosine of $0.8$ doesn't mean "80% similar", and a threshold
chosen for one model doesn't work for another: it has to be calibrated with labelled pairs from your
own data.

### When the length was the signal

In 2024, Steck, Ekanadham and Kallus, from Netflix, published a paper whose title is a question:
[*Is Cosine-Similarity of Embeddings Really About Similarity?*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.05440) In
a family of linear recommendation models, the prediction is the product of two matrices, $A B^\top$,
and you can multiply each column of $A$ by any factor as long as you divide the matching column of
$B$ by the same factor. With a diagonal matrix $D$:

$$
(A D)(B D^{-1})^\top = A D D^{-1} B^\top = A B^\top .
$$

The predictions don't change, but the cosines between the rows of $A D$ do. The model doesn't
determine them, and depending on the training they can come out arbitrary. The authors' recipe: if
you're going to compare with the cosine, train with the cosine inside the loss. Otherwise, the only
thing the model pinned down is the dot product. It's the principle from before, seen from the side
where it fails.

### When the relation has a direction

The cosine is symmetric: that's the third rule, the one it does satisfy. Many relations aren't. A
question and its answer aren't interchangeable. A dog is an animal, but not the other way round. One
sentence can imply another without the second implying the first.

There are three ways out. The first is to put the asymmetry in the model instead of the measure: E5
asks you to prefix questions with `query: ` and documents with `passage: `, so the same text is
encoded differently depending on its role. The second is to change the geometry:
[*order embeddings*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.06361) represent "is a" as an order between vectors,
and [hyperbolic embeddings](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08039) place hierarchies in a space where trees
fit without being squeezed. The third is to compare piece by piece: [ColBERT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12832),
a retriever from 2020, stores one normalised vector per word and scores by adding up, for each word
of the question, its best cosine with any word of the document. That sum runs over the question and
not the document, so it's asymmetric by construction. And there's always the cross-encoder.

## What to take away

If you remember only one thing, make it this: **the cosine is a similarity, and $1 - \cos\theta$ is a
distance that isn't a metric, because it's half the square of the Euclidean distance between the
normalised vectors.** For searching, it makes no difference, because the angle, the chord and the
cosine rank things in exactly the same way. For averaging, chaining or pruning an index it does, and
the metric to use is the angle or the Euclidean distance between normalised vectors.

And if you remember a second thing: **the cosine didn't win because it measures meaning better. It
won because it throws the length away**, which in almost all text embeddings is either noise or a
measure of something else, and because models are trained with it inside the loss.

The rest is practical.

**Use the measure the model was trained with.** It's usually on the model card. If it's the cosine
and the vectors already come normalised, use the dot product: same order, fewer operations.

**If the length means something, don't throw it away.** Popularity, frequency, importance: if the
model was trained with the dot product, compare with the dot product.

**Don't set thresholds by eye.** A cosine of $0.8$ means something different in each model, and two
similarities of $0.7$ don't guarantee a third. Calibrate with labelled pairs and, if you take vectors
from a model that wasn't trained for comparison, centre them first.

**For relations with a direction, put the asymmetry somewhere else.** Use different prefixes for
questions and documents, or a cross-encoder that reranks the top results.
