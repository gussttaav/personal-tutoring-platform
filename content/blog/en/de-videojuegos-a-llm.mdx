On 11 October 1999, NVIDIA, a graphics card maker, launched the GeForce 256 and billed it as
"the world's first GPU". By the definition NVIDIA wrote for the occasion, a **GPU** (*graphics
processing unit*) was a chip that could draw 3D scenes on its own. What set the GeForce 256 apart
was that it took over a calculation the computer's processor had always handled until then:
multiplying every vertex in the scene by a 4×4 matrix to put it in front of the camera.

Twenty-five years later, Meta, the company behind Facebook, trained Llama 3, a language model
with 405 billion parameters, on 16,384 NVIDIA H100 GPUs. The H100 is a direct descendant of that
card, yet it can barely draw anything any more: it has no video output, and only 4 of its 132
compute blocks can run the programs a video game uses to paint the screen.

How does a gaming chip end up training language models? Why not use the processor every computer
already has? And if artificial intelligence (AI) is worth so much money, why hasn't anyone built
a chip just for it?

**At heart, a video game and a language model do the same job: they multiply matrices, millions
of times over, against the clock. The processor is built for almost the opposite job, which is
why video games had to invent a chip of their own. Language models inherited that chip and, just
as video games did before them, are now building their own.**

The story runs twice, first for video games and then for language models, with the same steps
each time: what gets computed, why it has to be fast, why the processor isn't enough and which
chip was built to fill the gap. First, though, three properties of the operation that links the
two stories, matrix multiplication. Every figure comes from the manufacturers' published specs or
from the papers linked, and any figure derived from them comes with its working alongside.

## The operation this is all about

Multiplying an $m \times k$ matrix by a $k \times n$ one takes about $2mnk$ operations, half of
them multiplications and half additions. These are **floating-point** operations (floating point
is the format computers use to store numbers with decimals), which is why a chip's power is
measured in **FLOPS** (*floating-point operations per second*). A TFLOPS is a trillion
($10^{12}$) operations per second, and a PFLOPS is a thousand trillion.

Three properties of this operation explain almost everything that follows.

**Every number in the result is independent of the others.** Each dot product can be computed at
the same time as all the rest, on a different part of the chip and in any order.

**There are no decisions.** The recipe is always the same, multiply and add, with no "if this
happens, do that" that depends on the data.

**Every number is used many times.** When you multiply two $n \times n$ matrices, each number in
the first is multiplied by $n$ numbers from the second. That's $2n^3$ operations on $2n^2$ input
numbers, or $n$ operations per number: with thousand-by-thousand matrices, every number read from
memory can feed a thousand operations, as long as it stays close at hand in the meantime.

All three hold for a video game just as much as for a language model. Only the size of the
matrices changes.

## Video games

### What a video game computes

To a computer, a 3D world is a collection of triangles. A character, a wall or a tree is a mesh
of thousands of triangles, and each triangle is defined by its three **vertices**, each with three
coordinates: $x$, $y$ and $z$. To draw a **frame** (one of the still images that make up an
animation when shown in quick succession), the game has two big jobs to do.

**First, it places every vertex.** A character's coordinates are stored relative to its own
centre. To draw it, the game has to move it to its place in the world, rotate it, view it from
where the camera is and project it onto the flat screen. Each of those steps is a multiplication
by a matrix, and since multiplying by several matrices in a row is the same as multiplying by
their product, the game combines them into one and applies it to every vertex of the object:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} x' \\ y' \\ z' \\ w' \end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
m_{11} & m_{12} & m_{13} & m_{14} \\
m_{21} & m_{22} & m_{23} & m_{24} \\
m_{31} & m_{32} & m_{33} & m_{34} \\
m_{41} & m_{42} & m_{43} & m_{44}
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

The matrix is 4×4 rather than 3×3 because of a trick. Add a fourth number to each vertex, always
a 1, and moving an object becomes a multiplication too: the matrix's fourth column adds a fixed
amount to each coordinate. Perspective, which makes distant things look smaller, works the same
way: the fourth coordinate of the result, $w'$, grows with distance, and dividing $x'$ and $y'$
by it shrinks whatever is far away. That comes to 16 multiplications and 12 additions per vertex,
with the same matrix for every vertex of the object.

**Then, it colours every pixel.** Once projected, each triangle covers a handful of **pixels**,
the dots of colour that make up the image, and working out which ones is called
**rasterisation**. Each of those pixels then needs a colour, and that's algebra too. The
brightness of a matte surface, for example, depends on the angle between the direction it faces
(its **normal**, a vector perpendicular to it) and the direction the light comes from, and that
angle comes from a dot product. Textures (images stuck onto the triangles), shadows and
reflections go on top, but the pattern doesn't change: the same calculation, on different data,
for every pixel.

Both jobs have the shape of matrix multiplication: a small calculation, always the same, repeated
over millions of elements that don't depend on one another. A pixel's colour doesn't wait for its
neighbour's.

### Why it has to be fast

A game doesn't draw one image: it draws sixty a second, or more. At 60 frames per second, each
frame has 16.7 milliseconds to be ready, and if it's late the player sees a stutter. A Full HD
screen (1920 × 1080) has 2,073,600 pixels, and a 4K one (3840 × 2160) has 8,294,400. At 4K and
60 frames per second, that's almost 500 million pixels a second, each with its own colour
calculation, and that's without counting the ones that get drawn and then hidden behind something
closer.

Nor can any of the work be done in advance, because nobody knows what the next frame will hold
until the player moves the camera. Animated films can afford to wait: each frame of *Toy Story*
(1995) took Pixar [between 45 minutes and 30
hours](https://www.techradar.com/news/25-years-of-magic-a-look-at-how-the-vfx-industry-has-evolved-since-toy-story-debuted)
of computing on a cluster of 117 Sun workstations. A game has to do something similar in 16
milliseconds, and keep doing it.

Notice what actually has the time limit. No pixel is in a hurry on its own: the deadline applies
to the frame as a whole, and it doesn't matter if one pixel takes a long time as long as all two
million finish in time. What a game needs is **throughput**, how much work gets finished per
second, rather than low **latency**, how long each individual task takes. That distinction decides
which chip is up to the job.

### Why the CPU couldn't keep up

The **CPU** (*central processing unit*) is the computer's general-purpose processor: it runs the
operating system, the browser and the game's logic. It's designed to run a single **thread** of
instructions (a sequence in which each step usually depends on the one before) as fast as
possible, and to do that it devotes a good part of the chip to avoiding waits.

**Caches.** Reading a piece of data from main memory takes on the order of a hundred
nanoseconds, time in which a core could have done hundreds of operations. Caches are small, fast
memories on the chip that keep recently used data in case it's needed again.

**Branch prediction.** An ordinary program is full of decisions: if the player presses the
button, the character jumps. The CPU bets on which way each decision will go and carries on
working before it knows. If it guessed wrong, it throws that work away.

**Out-of-order execution.** If an instruction is waiting for data, the CPU looks further ahead
for others that don't depend on it and runs those first.

All of this makes a program full of decisions and unpredictable data run fast, which describes
nearly all software. None of it helps with painting pixels: there are no decisions to guess, the
data needed is known in advance, and millions of independent calculations are waiting their turn.
A **core** is the part of the CPU that runs one thread, and the CPU of 1996 had just one, which
spent most of its area making that thread fast rather than adding and multiplying.

Games really were drawn by the CPU for years. Quake, id Software's shooter from June 1996, did
everything on it. The box asked for a Pentium, Intel's processor of the day, running at 75 MHz or
more, and a 120 MHz Pentium managed somewhere around [30 frames per
second](https://fabiensanglard.net/quake_pc/). Every extra frame, or extra pixel, had to be paid
for with a more expensive processor.

Intel's response, in 1997, was MMX, its first set of **SIMD** instructions (*single instruction,
multiple data*), aimed at multimedia and games: a single instruction works on several numbers at
once, four additions in one go, say. SSE (*Streaming SIMD Extensions*) followed in 1999 and did
the same with decimal numbers, four at a time. It helped, but a core with SIMD still did only a
handful of operations per clock cycle, and the pixels ran into the millions. And the CPU had other
jobs to do as well: physics, sound, the enemies' behaviour.

What was needed was a chip that made the opposite bet: give up on making any single task fast in
exchange for doing a huge number of them at once.

### A chip of its own: the GPU

The first answer was the **3D accelerator card**, a board added to the computer purely for
drawing. Voodoo Graphics, from a company called 3dfx, arrived at the end of 1996 and did half the
job: it rasterised the triangles and applied their textures, but the CPU still placed the
vertices. In January 1997 id Software released GLQuake, a version of Quake that drew with these
cards, and the difference was obvious at a glance: higher resolution and smoother textures.

In 1999, the GeForce 256 took over the other half as well. NVIDIA described it as a single chip
with "integrated transform, lighting, triangle setup/clipping, and rendering engines", able to
process at least 10 million polygons a second. *Transform* means the multiplication by the 4×4
matrix: in 1999, the operation this post is about left the CPU for a chip of its own.

Those chips were **fixed-function**: each stage did exactly what its designers had hard-wired it
to do. In 2001 the GeForce 3 introduced **programmable shaders**, small programs written by the
game itself that the GPU runs once for every vertex or every pixel. From then on, the GPU would
run any calculation it was given, on one condition: that it be the same for millions of elements.
In 2006 the GeForce 8800 merged the two kinds of shader into a single kind of core, and it had
128 of them, serving vertices and pixels alike.

That's the origin of the design GPUs still have today, and it's the opposite of a CPU's.

**Many simple cores.** A desktop CPU has one or two dozen big cores, and the largest server CPUs
a few hundred. The [GeForce RTX
5090](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/geforce/graphics-cards/50-series/rtx-5090/), NVIDIA's most
powerful gaming card, from 2025, has 21,760 small ones, which together do 104.8 TFLOPS. At 4K and
60 frames per second, that's about 210,000 operations per pixel per frame.

**One instruction for many threads.** The cores are grouped into **multiprocessors** (the compute
blocks from the opening), and on NVIDIA GPUs every group of 32 threads, a ***warp***, runs the
same instruction at the same moment, each thread on its own data. NVIDIA calls this **SIMT**
(*single instruction, multiple threads*). Fetching an instruction and deciding what to do with it
costs the same for one thread as for 32, so almost the whole chip can be devoted to calculation.

**Waiting without stalling.** A GPU doesn't try to dodge memory waits with huge caches and clever
predictions. Instead it keeps thousands of threads in flight, and when one warp is waiting for
data, the scheduler switches to another whose data has already arrived, at no cost. That works
because graphics always has millions of threads to offer.

**Very wide memory.** The RTX 5090 reads 1,792 GB per second from its memory. A desktop with two
DDR5-5600 modules, the kind of memory most computers use today, manages 89.6 GB/s at most.

The price is that a GPU is bad at what a CPU does well. A single thread crawls along, and if the
threads in a warp go different ways at a decision, the warp runs both paths one after the other.
That's why a computer has both: the CPU for the program, the GPU for the millions of identical
calculations.

### From drawing to computing

As soon as shaders became programmable, some researchers realised that a GPU was, underneath, a
machine for doing the same operation on millions of numbers, faster than any CPU. All they had to
do was dress the problem up as drawing. In 2001, E. Scott Larsen and David McAllister, at the
University of North Carolina, published [*Fast matrix multiplies using graphics
hardware*](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/582034.582089): they stored the matrices as textures
and let the stage that combines textures do the multiplications and additions. In the words of
their abstract, they got the result by literally visualising the computation.

It worked, but every calculation had to be disguised as a picture. In 2007 NVIDIA released
[CUDA](https://developer.nvidia.com/cuda-toolkit) (*Compute Unified Device Architecture*), which
let programmers write for its GPUs in a variant of C, with no textures or pixels involved: you
write a function and ask for it to run on a million threads at once. The first GPU to support it
was the GeForce 8800, a gaming card.

**Neural networks**, programs that learn from examples by adjusting millions of numbers called
**parameters**, were among the first to benefit, because under the hood they are mostly matrix
multiplications. In 2009, Rajat Raina, Anand Madhavan and Andrew Ng, at Stanford, trained
networks on GPUs [up to 70 times faster](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1553374.1553486) than on
a dual-core processor: a model with 100 million parameters went from several weeks of training to
a day. And in 2012
[AlexNet](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2012/hash/c399862d3b9d6b76c8436e924a68c45b-Abstract.html),
the network built by Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton, won ImageNet, an image
recognition competition, by a wide margin. They trained it for five or six days on two GeForce
GTX 580s, gaming cards with 3 GB of memory each, and had to split the network between them
because it didn't fit on one. That's where modern **deep learning**, with its many-layered
networks, took off, and it took off on hardware bought at a games shop.

## Language models

### What a language model computes

A **language model** is a neural network that, given a piece of text, works out what is likely to
come next. An **LLM** (*large language model*) is one with billions of parameters, trained on a
huge share of the public text on the internet. The text goes in chopped into **tokens**, which are
pieces of words (a short word is usually a single token, a long one several), and the answer
comes out one token at a time: the model works out the next token, appends it to the text and
starts over.

Inside the model, each token is a vector: in Llama 3 405B, the model from the opening, a vector
of 16,384 numbers. Almost everything the model does with those vectors is multiply them by
matrices. Nearly every LLM today is a **transformer**, the architecture [published in
2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762), and a transformer is a stack of identical layers, 126 in
Llama 3 405B, each with two parts.

**Attention** lets each token look at the ones before it. Each token's vector is multiplied by
three matrices to produce three new vectors: a *query* (what it's looking for), a *key* (what it
offers) and a *value* (what it contributes if chosen). Comparing each token's query with the keys
of all the earlier tokens is another matrix multiplication; mixing their values according to that
comparison is one more; and a fourth matrix brings the result back to 16,384 numbers. In Llama 3
the key and value matrices are narrower, only 1,024 columns wide, thanks to an optimisation that
saves memory.

**The feed-forward network** handles each token separately: it multiplies its vector by a matrix
that widens it from 16,384 to 53,248 numbers, applies a simple function to each number and
multiplies the result by another matrix that brings it back to 16,384. Llama 3 uses a variant
with three matrices instead of two.

At the top of the stack, one last matrix turns the vector into a score for each of the 128,000
tokens in the vocabulary, and the next token is chosen from those scores.

The matrices are the parameters. Those in one layer of Llama 3 405B add up to about 3.2 billion
numbers, and the 126 layers, plus the vocabulary matrices, make the 405 billion in the name.
Every parameter takes part in one multiplication and one addition for each token that goes
through the model, so processing a token costs about $2N$ operations, where $N$ is the number of
parameters: about 810 billion for Llama 3 405B.

Training costs three times as much per token. During **training**, the model reads a text,
predicts each token from the ones before it, measures how wrong it was and adjusts its
parameters to do better next time. Besides the forward pass, which makes the prediction, there's
a backward pass, which works out which way to nudge each parameter and costs twice as much as the
first. The total is about $6N$ operations per token. Llama 3 405B was trained on 15.6 trillion
tokens:

$$
6 \times 405 \cdot 10^{9} \times 15.6 \cdot 10^{12} \approx 3.8 \cdot 10^{25}
$$

That's a 38 followed by 24 zeros, [matching the figure Meta publishes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783).

It's the same operation as in a video game, just on a different scale in two ways. The matrices
aren't 4×4 but thousands by thousands. And they aren't multiplied by one vector at a time but by
many: each of Llama 3's training steps processed 16 million tokens at once, so the tokens'
vectors are stacked into a matrix and every multiplication is matrix by matrix.

### Why it has to be fast

Meta trained Llama 3 405B on 16,384 H100 GPUs, and each one sustained about 400 TFLOPS, 41% of
its theoretical peak. At that rate, a single H100 would have needed about 3,000 years to do the
$3.8 \cdot 10^{25}$ operations. All 16,384 together needed about 67 days.

With that many components, something is always breaking. Meta reports 466 interruptions during a
54-day stretch of training, 419 of them unplanned, with more than half of those caused by
failures of the GPUs themselves: an interruption every three hours or so, on average. Each one
sends the run back to the last saved checkpoint.

And a model is trained once but used millions of times. Using it is called **inference**: the
trained model generating answers. Every generated token costs $2N$ operations, and there's a
deadline here too, just like the frame's: a chat that writes at 20 tokens per second has 50
milliseconds for each token. And that's not for one person, but for everyone using it at the same
time.

The parallel with video games is exact: the same calculation over millions of pieces of data,
against a deadline that comes round every few milliseconds.

### Why the GPU fits, and the CPU doesn't

The three properties from the start explain why the GPU is such a good fit.

**The results are independent**, like pixels, so thousands of cores can each work out their own
piece at the same time.

**There are no decisions**, again like pixels, so a CPU's branch prediction and out-of-order
execution have nothing to guess and nothing to reorder.

**Every number is reused many times.** When a 16,384 × 16,384 matrix is multiplied by another of
the same size, every number read from memory feeds 16,384 operations. If the chip can keep those
numbers close to the arithmetic units while it uses them, its speed depends on how much it can
compute rather than how much it can read. This is measured by the **arithmetic intensity** of a
calculation: how many operations it performs for each byte it reads from memory. It will come up
again shortly.

And what about the CPU? Today's isn't the one from 1996. Just as Intel added MMX for games, its
Xeon server processors have shipped since 2023 with **AMX** (*Advanced Matrix Extensions*), a
matrix multiplication unit inside every core. Intel [credits
it](https://www.intel.com/content/dam/www/central-libraries/us/en/documents/2025-02/xeon-6-granite-rapids-product-brief.pdf)
with a peak of 1,024 operations per cycle per core on 16-bit numbers. The [Xeon
6980P](https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/products/sku/240777/intel-xeon-6980p-processor-504m-cache-2-00-ghz/specifications.html),
from 2024, has 128 cores at 2 GHz, so on paper it reaches about 262 TFLOPS.

Comparing it with GPUs requires one more ingredient: precision. **BF16** is a 16-bit format for
decimal numbers, half the usual 32 bits; **FP8** and **FP4** use 8 and 4 bits; and **INT8** means
8-bit integers, whose speed is measured in **TOPS**, trillions of integer operations per second.
Fewer bits mean less precision, but more operations per second and fewer bytes to move. Neural
networks tolerate that loss well, which is why AI chips compete at these precisions. Here's the
Xeon next to NVIDIA's data-centre GPUs, the
[H100](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/h100/) and the
[B200](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/hgx/):

| | Xeon 6980P (CPU, 2024) | H100 (GPU, 2022) | B200 (GPU, 2024) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Matrices in BF16 | ≈ 262 TFLOPS | 989 TFLOPS | 2,250 TFLOPS |
| At its lowest precision | INT8: ≈ 524 TOPS | FP8: 1,979 TFLOPS | FP4: 9,000 TFLOPS |
| Memory bandwidth | 845 GB/s | 3,350 GB/s | 8,000 GB/s |
| Memory | up to 3 TB | 80 GB | 180 GB |
| Power | 500 W | 700 W | ≈ 1,000 W |

All three columns are theoretical peaks that nobody reaches in practice, but they're good enough
for a comparison.

The comparison makes three points. First, the gap is far smaller than it used to be. In 2009,
Raina and his co-authors measured a GPU up to 70 times faster than a dual-core CPU; today, on
paper, an H100 multiplies BF16 matrices about four times as fast as a 128-core Xeon, and a B200
about nine times as fast. At each chip's lowest precision the gap widens again: a B200 in FP4
does 17 times as many operations as the Xeon in INT8.

Second, the Xeon's memory lags by a similar margin, with four to ten times less bandwidth. And
per watt, the H100 computes almost three times as much as the Xeon.

The third point isn't in the table: how many of them you can link up. A server board takes two
Xeon 6980Ps at most. The servers Meta trained Llama 3 on had eight GPUs and two CPUs each, and
the eight GPUs talked to each other over **NVLink**, a direct link between NVIDIA GPUs that moves
900 GB/s on the H100. The CPUs are still there, but with a different job: preparing the data,
coordinating and handing out work to the GPUs.

There's a fourth reason, and it's less technical. For more than a decade, AI software, from the
matrix libraries up to PyTorch, the Python library almost every model is trained with, has been
written for NVIDIA GPUs first. By the time CPUs added AMX, the rest of the ecosystem had long
since moved elsewhere.

There's one thing the CPU does win at, though: capacity. A Xeon can take up to 3 TB of memory,
nearly forty times as much as an H100. That matters more than it seems, because training a model
and using it aren't the same problem.

### Training is not the same as inference

Training makes three demands at once.

**Compute.** With batches of millions of tokens, every multiplication is matrix by matrix and the
arithmetic intensity is very high: the chip spends nearly all its time computing. It's exactly
what GPUs are designed for.

**Memory.** Training needs more than the parameters. It also needs each one's **gradient** (which
way to move it) and the state of the **optimiser**, the algorithm that decides how far to move
it; the most widely used one, Adam, keeps two averages per parameter. With the usual recipe,
which computes in 16 bits but keeps a copy of the parameters in 32, that's [16 bytes per
parameter](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.02054): 6.5 TB for Llama 3 405B, enough to fill 81 H100s
all by itself, before counting each layer's intermediate results.

**Network.** Meta split the model three ways at once: each matrix sliced across the 8 GPUs in a
server, the layers divided into 16 groups that pass the work along like an assembly line, and 128
copies of all that processing different data ($8 \times 16 \times 128 = 16{,}384$). At every step
the GPUs exchange results, over NVLink inside a server and over the network between servers. A
large training run is as much a communications problem as a computing one, and Meta notes that
its complexity exceeds that of the much larger CPU clusters it had already run.

**Inference** has two phases, and they behave very differently.

**Prefill** reads the question. All its tokens are known from the start, so the model processes
them together, matrix by matrix, as in training, and compute is the limit.

**Decoding** writes the answer, and here everything changes. Each new token depends on the
previous one, so tokens are generated one at a time, and each one has to go through every matrix
in the model. It's matrix by vector: each parameter is read from memory for a single
multiplication and a single addition. With 16-bit parameters, that's two operations for every two
bytes, or one operation per byte.

An H100 performs 989 trillion operations per second and reads 3.35 trillion bytes. To keep its
arithmetic busy it needs about 295 operations for every byte it reads. Decoding for a single
person manages one, using 0.3% of the chip's compute. Memory sets the speed: Llama 3 8B, the
small version, takes up 16 GB in 16 bits, and an H100 can read that only about 200 times a
second, so it can't generate more than about 200 tokens per second for one person, whatever else
it does. Llama 3 405B, at 810 GB in 16 bits, doesn't even fit in a server with eight H100s, so
Meta spreads it over the 16 GPUs of two servers.

That chart is the [roofline
model](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~kubitron/cs252/handouts/papers/RooflineVyNoYellow.pdf),
named for its shape, and it's the most useful tool there is for reasoning about a chip's speed:
a calculation to the left of the knee is limited by memory, and one to the right by arithmetic.
The standard fixes for decoding all come down to moving to the right or reading less.

**Serving many users at once** (*batching*). If the server generates the next token for 64
conversations together, every parameter it reads serves 64 multiplications, so the intensity
rises to 64. It takes about 300 to saturate an H100.

**The KV cache.** To avoid recomputing the keys and values of all the previous tokens at every
step, the server keeps them in memory (KV stands for *key* and *value*). That saves compute but
takes up memory for every conversation, and it's what limits how many fit at once.

**Quantisation**: storing the parameters in 8 or 4 bits instead of 16. That leaves half or a
quarter as many bytes to read, so decoding runs nearly twice or four times as fast, at some cost
in precision.

And this is where the CPU comes back into the picture. Because decoding is limited by memory
rather than compute, a machine with fast memory does reasonably well at it. The Xeon 6980P, with
its 845 GB/s, can read Llama 3 8B in 16 bits about 50 times a second. A laptop with two DDR5-5600
modules, running the model quantised to 4 bits (about 4 GB), reaches about 20 tokens per second
on paper. That's why programs like [llama.cpp](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp), which runs
language models on just about any computer, work on laptops with no GPU. Training a model that
way is another story.

Training and inference need different things: training wants compute, memory for all its state
and a network linking thousands of chips; inference wants, above all, bandwidth and a low cost
per token. That difference explains much of what has been built since.

### A chip of its own, again

Video games had to invent a chip because the CPU wasn't up to the job. Language models found one
ready-made, and since then history has repeated itself twice over: the GPU has gradually turned
into an AI chip, and chips designed purely for AI have appeared.

**The GPU specialises.** In 2017, NVIDIA's V100 introduced **Tensor Cores**, units that multiply
not two numbers but two small matrices at once: each one [computes $D = A \times B + C$ on 4×4
matrices](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/programming-tensor-cores-cuda-9/) in a single step,
64 multiply-adds per cycle. The V100's 640 Tensor Cores delivered 125 TFLOPS, eight times what
the same card's ordinary cores could manage. This is the third property of matrix multiplication
built into the circuit itself: if every number is going to be used many times, let the hardware
reuse it without reading it again.

Lower precisions followed: FP8 on the H100 (2022) and FP4 on the B200 (2024), each doubling the
operations of the one before. Alongside them came **HBM** (*high bandwidth memory*), stacked
right next to the chip so it can be read faster, and NVLink, so that many GPUs behave a little
more like one. Along the way, the data-centre GPU left graphics behind: according to the [H100
whitepaper](https://resources.nvidia.com/en-us-tensor-core/gtc22-whitepaper-hopper), only 4 of
its 132 multiprocessors can run the shaders that draw a video game. It's a matrix multiplication
chip that kept its old name.

**Chips of their own.** The first to reach production was Google's. In 2013, an internal forecast
showed that if every user spoke to voice search, which ran on neural networks, for just three
minutes a day, Google would have to double its data centres. So it set about designing its own
chip for inference (for training, it bought GPUs) and had it running within 15 months. The
[TPU](https://cloud.google.com/tpu) (*tensor processing unit*) is an **ASIC**
(*application-specific integrated circuit*), a chip designed for a single task, and it has been
in Google's data centres since 2015.

Its heart is a
[**systolic array**](https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/~htk/publication/1982-kung-why-systolic-architecture.pdf),
an idea dating from the late 1970s: a grid of 256 × 256 cells (65,536 in all) that multiply and
add, with data flowing from cell to cell. The parameters are loaded into the cells and the inputs
enter from the left. Each cell multiplies the input arriving at it by its parameter, adds the
product to the partial result handed down from the cell above and passes the sum to the cell
below, while the input carries on to the right. A number read from memory is used 256 times
without going back to memory. According to the [paper in which Google described
it](https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04760), the TPU was on average 15 to 30 times faster than the CPU
and GPU of its day, and delivered 30 to 80 times as many operations per watt.

Google now trains its Gemini models on TPUs, and the seventh generation,
[Ironwood](https://cloud.google.com/tpu/docs/tpu7x) (2025), delivers 4,614 TFLOPS in FP8 with
192 GB of HBM at 7.4 TB/s, linked in groups of up to 9,216 chips. Google presented it as its
first TPU designed mainly for inference. And Google isn't the only one using them: in October
2025, Anthropic, the company that makes the Claude models,
[announced](https://www.anthropic.com/news/expanding-our-use-of-google-cloud-tpus-and-services)
plans to use up to a million.

The other tech giants have their own: Amazon has
[Trainium](https://aws.amazon.com/ai/machine-learning/trainium/) for training and
[Inferentia](https://aws.amazon.com/ai/machine-learning/inferentia/) for inference; Meta has
[MTIA](https://ai.meta.com/blog/next-generation-meta-training-inference-accelerator-AI-MTIA/)
(*Meta Training and Inference Accelerator*); Microsoft has
[Maia](https://azure.microsoft.com/en-us/blog/azure-maia-for-the-era-of-ai-from-silicon-to-software-to-systems/).
And several start-ups have made riskier bets.

- [Cerebras](https://www.cerebras.ai/chip) makes a chip the size of an entire silicon wafer, with
  900,000 cores and 44 GB of **SRAM** (the fast kind of memory that caches are made of) on the
  chip itself.
- [Groq](https://groq.com/) designed an inference chip that keeps the parameters in SRAM instead
  of HBM, so decoding never has to wait on memory. In December 2025, NVIDIA [took a non-exclusive
  licence](https://groq.com/newsroom/groq-and-nvidia-enter-non-exclusive-inference-technology-licensing-agreement-to-accelerate-ai-inference-at-global-scale)
  to its technology and hired its founder, in a deal the press valued at around \$20 billion.
- [Etched](https://www.etched.com/) announced a chip in 2024 that [can only run
  transformers](https://techcrunch.com/2024/06/25/etched-is-building-an-ai-chip-that-only-runs-transformer-models/),
  trading away everything else to do that one thing faster.

In phones and laptops, **NPUs** (*neural processing units*), small matrix units inside the
processor, run models without draining the battery on the GPU. Many of these chips started with
inference, and not by chance: it's the problem from the previous section, the one that depends on
bandwidth and on the cost of each token, and where a general-purpose GPU leaves the most room for
improvement.

**Why the GPU is still in charge.** Despite all these specialised chips, NVIDIA's GPUs still
account for [more than 60% of all the AI computing capacity sold since
2022](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/ai-chip-production), according to estimates by Epoch AI, an
independent research group that tracks trends in the field, with Google and Amazon making up much
of the rest. There are three reasons.

**Software.** CUDA has been around since 2007, and everything written on top of it (matrix
libraries, PyTorch, each lab's own tricks) works there first. A new chip needs its own compiler,
its own libraries and programmers who know how to use it, and Google had to build all of that for
the TPU.

**Time.** Designing a chip and getting it into production takes years, and models change faster
than that. The first TPU was designed for the networks Google was running at the time, three
kinds that made up 95% of its inference workload, and none of them was a transformer, which
arrived two years later. The TPU survived the change because it had bet on matrix multiplication
rather than on any particular network. A chip that runs only transformers, like Etched's, is the
opposite bet: if the architecture changes, the chip is left behind. The GPU, being programmable,
can adapt to whatever comes next.

**Scale.** For twenty years video games paid for the GPU's development; now data centres pay for
it. And what sells by the million gets cheaper and better faster than what sells by the thousand.

That's the big difference from 1999. Back then the CPU couldn't do the job; today the GPU does it
well. A new chip isn't up against a processor that can't cope, but one that improves every two
years and absorbs its rivals' ideas along the way: tensor cores are, in miniature, the same idea
as the TPU's systolic array, and NVIDIA already holds a licence to Groq's technology.

## What to take away

If you remember only one thing, make it this: **the GPU didn't end up in AI by accident. A video
game and a language model do the same kind of work: the same small operation on millions of
independent pieces of data, with no decisions and every number reused many times. The CPU is
designed for the opposite: a single fast thread of instructions, full of decisions.** Video games
had to invent the GPU, and AI inherited it.

The same steps, side by side:

| | Video games | Language models |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is computed | Every vertex times a 4×4 matrix, and the colour of every pixel | Every token times matrices thousands of rows by thousands of columns, layer after layer |
| How much | Almost 500 million pixels a second, at 4K and 60 frames per second | $3.8 \cdot 10^{25}$ operations to train Llama 3 405B; $2N$ per generated token |
| Time limit | 16.7 ms per frame | Tens of milliseconds per token |
| Why not the CPU | One core, built for a thread full of decisions | Four to ten times less compute and bandwidth, and only two per server |
| A chip of its own | The GPU (1999) | Tensor cores, the TPU and the rest (from 2015) |

And if you have room for three more, make them practical ones.

**Always ask what's holding you back: compute or memory.** Count how many operations a
calculation performs for each byte it reads, and compare that with how much the chip can compute
for each byte its memory delivers. If the calculation falls short of that ratio, more arithmetic
won't make it any faster.

**When generating text, count bytes, not operations.** Decoding speed for one person is at most
the memory bandwidth divided by the size of the model. That's why quantisation speeds things up,
and why a laptop with good memory can run small models.

**The name "GPU" is a historical leftover.** What matters in an AI chip is how many matrix
multiplications it can do per second and at what precision, how many bytes per second it can be
fed, and how many other chips it can be linked to. And behind all that, the software that knows
how to use it.

In 1999, a card for playing games took a multiplication by a 4×4 matrix off the CPU's hands.
Twenty-five years later, that same operation, on matrices thousands of rows tall, is what trains
language models. The chips have changed in size, in name and even in maker; the operation is the
same.
