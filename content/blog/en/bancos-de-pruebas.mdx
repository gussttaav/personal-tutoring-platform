In June 2023, Hugging Face's public leaderboard of open models gave LLaMA 65B a score of 48.8% on
[MMLU](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.03300) (*Massive Multitask Language Understanding*), a
14,042-question multiple-choice exam covering 57 subjects. Hugging Face is the biggest platform for
sharing language models, and LLaMA 65B was the large model Meta had released that February (the
number is its size: 65 billion parameters). Meta's paper reported a good deal more, and the gap was
too big to put down to chance. Hugging Face [looked into
why](https://huggingface.co/blog/open-llm-leaderboard-mmlu) and found three programs that all
claimed to grade the same exam. With the same model and the same questions, MMLU's original code
gave 63.6%; Stanford's HELM benchmark (*Holistic Evaluation of Language Models*) gave 63.7%; and the
*LM Evaluation Harness*, the language model (LM) evaluation tool from EleutherAI, an open research
collective, which the leaderboard used, gave 48.8%.

It wasn't only the score that changed. The ranking did too. Under the Harness, Falcon 40B, the model
from the Technology Innovation Institute in Abu Dhabi, came out ahead of LLaMA 65B, at 52.7% against
48.8%. Under the original code it came third, behind LLaMA 30B as well. And the weakest models,
which barely cleared the 25% you get by answering at random under the original code, gained almost
ten points under the Harness.

A multiple-choice exam looks like the one case where evaluating a language model is a solved
problem. The hard part of evaluating what a model writes is deciding whether an answer is right,
because there's almost never a single correct answer, and that calls for other judges: a program
that checks, a person, another model. In a multiple-choice exam, though, every question has a key, a
letter, and grading means comparing letters. That's why fixed-answer **benchmarks** (fixed
collections of questions used to compare models) are the most common way to compare models, and why
their figures head the model card of every new release.

Even so, the score depends on four decisions that aren't published alongside it: how the answer is
read, how the question is asked, which questions are used (and whether the model had already seen
them) and how many there are. Any one of them moves the result by more than the gap between the
models being compared. This article goes through them one at a time, with real MMLU questions and
two small models measured on a laptop, and ends with what to ask before trusting the number on a
model card. It's the second in a series on how language models are evaluated; the
[first](/blog/evaluar-texto-generado) introduces the different judges, but you don't need to have
read it.

## What's inside a benchmark

A fixed-answer benchmark has four parts. The **questions**. The **answer key**, the correct answer
to each one. A **scoring rule**, which says how the model's answer is extracted and when it matches
the key. And an **averaging rule**, which turns thousands of hits and misses into one number. The
first two are the exam, and they get published. The last two are the measuring instrument, and they
almost never appear next to the score.

[MMLU](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.03300) is the textbook example. A group led by Dan Hendrycks at
the University of California, Berkeley published it in 2020: 14,042 test questions with four options
each, spread over 57 subjects ranging from abstract algebra to virology, with between 100 and 1,534
questions per subject. Even the average comes in two versions: over all 14,042 questions, where
professional law, with its 1,534, weighs fifteen times as much as abstract algebra, or over the 57
subjects, where they all weigh the same. Each subject also comes with five solved questions that
don't count towards the score and serve as examples. Here's how the model sees a question, with the
five examples in front of it:

```text
The following are multiple choice questions (with answers) about high school geography.

The rate of natural increase of a population is found by subtracting the
A. crude death rate from the crude birth date.
B. crude birth rate from the crude death rate.
C. doubling time from the crude birth rate.
D. fertility rate from the crude death rate.
Answer: A

[… four more solved questions in the same format …]

What family do most European languages belong to?
A. Ural-Altaic
B. Basque
C. Indo-European
D. Phoenician
Answer:
```

The five solved examples don't teach the subject; they teach the format. This is [in-context
learning](/cursos/llm-agents/aprendizaje-en-contexto): the model sees the pattern of question,
options, `Answer:` and a letter five times, and carries it on. That's why MMLU is said to be
evaluated with five examples (*5-shot*); without them it would be *0-shot*.

The text ends at `Answer:`, and that's where what the exam fixes comes to an end. The model hasn't
answered anything yet, because a language model doesn't answer: it continues text. For the next
position it gives a probability to every piece of its vocabulary, the **tokens** (words, pieces of
words or single symbols). Reading the answer means deciding what to do with that list of
probabilities, and there's more than one way to do it.

To see this with numbers anyone can reproduce, I measured two small open models:
[SmolLM2-360M](https://huggingface.co/HuggingFaceTB/SmolLM2-360M), from Hugging Face, with 360
million parameters and a vocabulary of 49,152 tokens, and
[Qwen2.5-0.5B](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B), from the Chinese company Alibaba, with 500
million parameters and 151,665 tokens. They're base models, not tuned for chat, which is what MMLU
had in mind. The measurements use 570 MMLU questions, ten from each subject picked at random with a
fixed seed, always the same ones and always with the same five examples. [The
program](/blog/bancos-de-pruebas/ejemplos/en/medir_mmlu.py) downloads the models and the questions,
takes every measurement in the article and saves every probability, so anyone can repeat or check
them.

## Three ways to read the answer

The three programs Hugging Face compared didn't differ in the exam but in what they did with the
model's probabilities after `Answer:`. With the same model, the same prompt and the same question,
the three readings can give three different answers, and the figure shows it with the geography
question from the example and SmolLM2.

### The most likely letter

MMLU's original code looks at just four of those probabilities, the ones for the letters A, B, C and
D, and takes the highest. The model never writes anything: one pass through the model per question
is enough, and you always get one of the four options.

Both models put almost all of the next token's probability on those four letters, 99.4% on average
for SmolLM2 and 99.8% for Qwen2.5: the five examples have taught them that a letter comes after
`Answer:`. But saying a letter doesn't mean knowing which one. On the geography question, SmolLM2
gives the four letters between 23% and 27% each, and over the whole exam it scores 24.7%, the same
as answering at random: it picks A on 245 of the 570 questions and B on 216, whatever the question.
Qwen2.5, on the other hand, scores 52.3%, its best score across all the readings in this article, and
on the geography question it gives C 92%.

Even this reading has a trap. A token includes the space in front of it, and in `Answer: A` the
token after the colon isn't "A" but " A", with the space (how text is split into tokens is covered in
the lesson on [BPE](/cursos/llm-agents/bpe-de-verdad), *byte pair encoding*, the algorithm that
builds models' vocabularies). A program that compares the probabilities of "A", "B", "C" and "D"
without the space is looking at tokens the text never puts there, and the score changes. Qwen2.5
drops from the 52.3% it gets when the letter is read properly, with the space, to 48.9%: 19 fewer
questions out of 570, a small difference but bigger than its margin. SmolLM2 goes from its 24.7% to
27.2%, which is noise. The fact that the mistake costs so little is exactly what makes it dangerous:
the figure looks reasonable, and nobody checks it.

### The most likely whole option

The 2023 Harness did something else: it worked out the probability of each complete option as a
continuation of the text, " C. Indo-European" in full, and took the most likely one. The probability
of a sequence is the product of its tokens' probabilities, each less than 1, so a long option starts
at a disadvantage just for being long. That's why it's usually divided by the number of tokens, or
of characters. Even so, that probability also measures how ordinary the phrase is, not just whether
it's the right answer.

There's a variant that doesn't show the options. The prompt is just the question and `Answer:`, and
each option is scored as a possible continuation, like a fill-in-the-blank exercise. It's called the
*cloze* formulation, after that kind of exercise, and it's the classic way to evaluate a model that
can't answer with a letter.

With the options shown, SmolLM2 stays at chance: 24.0%, or 24.9% when dividing by tokens. Without
them, and dividing by characters, it climbs to 38.2%, and on the example question it gives
"Indo-European" 99.6% of the probability shared by the four. The model knows more than the letter
reading lets on; what it can't do is tie each answer to its letter. Qwen2.5 goes the opposite way:
from 52.3% when the letter is read, it drops to 47.2% with the whole option, to 39.6% dividing by
tokens and to 35.4% without the options.

### What the model writes

HELM let the model generate text and compared it with the correct letter. If the most likely token
isn't a letter but a line break or a "The", the answer counts as wrong even if the correct letter
had more probability than the other three. With base models and five examples in front of them this
hardly happens: for both models the most likely token was a letter on all 570 questions, and this
reading agrees exactly with the most likely letter.

Even so, it's the most widely used reading today, for two reasons. Many models can only be used
through an API (*application programming interface*, the interface one program uses to call
another's service) that doesn't return probabilities, or only those of a few tokens. And chat and
reasoning models don't answer with a letter: they explain, reason and finally give an answer that
has to be found in the text, usually by asking for a fixed format such as "Answer: B" and searching
for it with a pattern. An answer in any other format counts as wrong, so this reading also measures
whether the model sticks to the format.

### Which reading is fair

The two models respond in opposite ways. Qwen2.5 does better the more the reading looks like a
multiple-choice exam: 52.3% when the letter is read and 35.4% without the options, almost 17 points
less. SmolLM2 only beats chance when it doesn't see the options. Reading the letter, Qwen2.5 is 27.5
points ahead on the same questions; without the options, SmolLM2 even comes out ahead, by 2.8, a
difference that fits inside its ±3.4 margin. Depending on how the answer is read, Qwen2.5 is either
far better than SmolLM2 or indistinguishable from it. That's what happened to LLaMA 65B and Falcon
40B in Hugging Face's table, on a small scale and with two models anyone can run.

Joshua Robinson and colleagues gave it a name in 2022: reading the letter only works if the model
can tie each option to its symbol, what they call [*multiple choice symbol
binding*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.12353), and that ability varies a lot from one model to
another. Models that have it do much better when the letter is read, because they see the four
options at once and can compare them. Models that don't only give a signal without the options.
[OLMES](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.08446) (*Open Language Model Evaluation Standard*), the
evaluation standard proposed in 2024 by the Allen Institute for AI (AI2), a non-profit research
institute in Seattle, settles the dilemma by evaluating every model both ways and keeping the better
score. The differences aren't small. Llama 3 70B, Meta's 2024 model, gets 93.7% on ARC-Challenge
(*AI2 Reasoning Challenge*), the institute's own benchmark of school science questions, when the
letter is read, and 69.0% without the options. And OLMo 7B (*Open Language Model*), one of the
institute's open models, answered at random with letters during the first part of its training: the
ability appeared at around 400 billion tokens.

That explains the Hugging Face table from the beginning. The 2023 Harness read the whole option,
which gives some signal to models that can't tie answers to letters, and that's why the weakest ones
rose from 25% to over 33%. LLaMA 65B, which can, was judged on how likely each full phrase was, and
lost fifteen points. No reading is correct in the abstract: each measures a slightly different
ability, and the minimum requirement is that it's the same for every model being compared.

## How the question is asked

Reading the answer is half the instrument. The other half is the text put in front of the model,
which moves the score even though nothing about what's being asked changes.

The simplest example is the order of the options. The same four options can be presented in any
order, and the correct answer should stay the same. To check, I ran the 570 questions four more
times, rotating the options so that the correct answer always sat at A, then always at B, and so on
to D, and read the most likely letter.

SmolLM2 scores 44.2% when the correct answer is at A and 8.4% when it's at C. It doesn't know more
with A: it says A or B on four questions out of five, and gets them right when the key happens to
land where it was going to answer anyway. Qwen2.5, which does tie letters to options, depends much
less on the order: it scores between 46.0% and 54.6% depending on where the key is, with a slight
preference for C, which it picks in 29.6% of its answers. Even so, 8.6 points between its best
letter and its worst is more than the margin. [Chujie Zheng and
colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.03882) measured this bias across 20 models in 2023. With
every correct answer at A, LLaMA 30B scored 68.2% on MMLU; with every one at D, 41.2%: a 27-point
gap on the same questions. The main cause, by their analysis, is that the model gives certain
letters more probability from the start, whatever the options say. The fix is cheap: shuffle the
options several times and average, or estimate that preference and subtract it.

Formatting matters even more. [Melanie Sclar and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11324)
(2023) changed details that leave the prompt's meaning alone, such as the separator between question
and answer, spacing or capitalisation, and the accuracy of LLaMA-2-13B, a Meta model, on a single
task varied by as much as 76 points from one format to another. Another study, by [Moran Mizrahi and
colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.00595) (2024), evaluated 20 models on 39 tasks with hundreds
of different wordings of the same instructions, and the ranking of the models changed with the
wording: on one task, the same model came first with one wording and ninth with another. That's why
a standard like OLMES fixes each task's exact prompt and examples, and why it's worth reporting the
score across several prompts, or at the very least with the same one for every model.

## If the model has already seen the exam

Benchmarks are public and live on the web, and models are trained on the web. MMLU's questions sit
in the paper's repository, and in copies, translations and forums where someone asks about one of
them. If the test questions, or their answers, made it into the training data, part of the score
measures memory, and that's **contamination**.

Whoever has the training data can look for it. In 2020, the authors of GPT-3, OpenAI's language
model from that year, searched for runs of thirteen consecutive words that appeared both in the
benchmarks and in the data they were about to train on, and removed the matches. A programming bug
left some of them in, and [they explained in their paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165) that
retraining the model was too expensive, so they also scored each benchmark without the suspect
questions. Searching for exact runs has a further limit: a 2023 study on [rephrased
questions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04850) trained a 13-billion-parameter model on rewritten or
translated versions of the questions from MMLU and other exams, and the model matched GPT-4 without
the match search catching anything.

Whoever doesn't have the data, which is nearly everyone, can only question the model. There are two
ideas. The first looks at **order**. A benchmark's questions are published in a particular order,
but any other order would be just as valid, so a model that hasn't seen them has no reason to prefer
one. A model that memorised them does: it gives more probability to the published order than to
shuffled ones. [The test by Oren and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17623) (2023) compares
the two probabilities, and it works on models from 1.4 billion parameters up with around a thousand
examples. Applied to five public models, it found little evidence of widespread contamination. The
second idea looks at **unlikely words**: text the model hasn't seen tends to contain a few words it
gives very low probability to, and text it saw in training hardly has any. [Min-K%
Prob](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.16789) (2023) scores a text by the average probability of its
lowest-probability tokens. Neither is conclusive for a single question; across hundreds, they give a
signal.

The most direct check is to write new questions. GSM8K (*Grade School Math 8K*) is a widely used
benchmark of about 8,500 primary-school maths problems. In 2024 the data company Scale AI wrote
[GSM1k](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.00332), 1,205 new problems in its style and at its difficulty,
and didn't publish them. Some model families lost up to 8 points going from one to the other.

There are three habits for preventing it. The first is to mark the files:
[BIG-bench](https://github.com/google/BIG-bench) (*Beyond the Imitation Game*), a collaborative 2022
benchmark with more than two hundred tasks, puts a **canary string** in every file, a unique
identifier that anyone preparing training data can search for to remove it. The second is to keep
the test questions unpublished and grade on a server. The third is to use questions newer than the
models' training data: [LiveBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19314) (2024) is refreshed every month
with problems from recent maths competitions, papers and news, and all of its answers are checked
without judges. An evaluation of your own, built from questions that have never been published, is
safe by construction, until the day someone pushes it to a public repository.

## How many questions you need

A score over $n$ questions is an estimate: the share of correct answers the model would get on all
questions like these, measured with a sample. If it gets a share $p$ right, the 95% margin of error
is roughly

$$
\pm 1.96 \sqrt{\frac{p\,(1 - p)}{n}}.
$$

With the 570 questions in these measurements, every score has a margin of about ±4 points: ±3.5 for
25% and ±4.0 for 40%. The 13.5 points SmolLM2 gains when it doesn't see the options are a real
difference; the 0.7 separating the letter reading from the whole-option reading aren't. Comparing
the two models on the same questions, the 27.5 points Qwen2.5 gains over SmolLM2 when the letter is
read have a margin of ±5.5, and the 2.8 SmolLM2 gains without the options, a margin of ±3.4.

### Comparing on the same questions

What usually matters isn't one score but the difference between two, and there the margin depends on
how the comparison is made. A small example shows it. A company that makes invoicing software, which
we'll call Albarán, has an assistant that answers its support team, and it tests two new versions on
200 real closed questions: each is answered with a yes or a no and a date, and a program checks them
against the key. Version 1 gets 70% right, 140 questions, and version 2 gets 74%, 148.

If the two versions had each answered 200 different questions, the margin on the difference would be
about ±8.8 points, and a 4-point lead would tell you nothing. But they answered the same ones, and
then what counts is how many questions they disagree on. For each question, write down $+1$ if only
version 2 gets it right, $-1$ if only version 1 does, and $0$ if both get it right or both get it
wrong. The lead is the average of those numbers, and its margin comes from the same idea as before,
applied to those 200 numbers. The questions where the two agree contribute zeros and add no noise:
each question's difficulty affects both versions equally and cancels out.

If version 2 fixes 10 answers and breaks 2, the two versions disagree on 12 questions and the margin
is ±3.4 points: the lead is real. If it fixes 14 and breaks 6, the lead is still 8 questions, but
they disagree on 20 and the margin rises to ±4.4: you can no longer tell. Up to 16 disagreements,
the 4 points beat their margin. If the two versions failed independently, without sharing which
questions they find hard, they'd disagree on about 81 and the margin would be back to ±8.8. Evan
Miller works this out, along with the rest of this section, in [*Adding Error Bars to
Evals*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00640) (2024), and recommends always comparing on the same
questions when you can.

### Questions that come in groups

The formula assumes every question is independent of the others, and that isn't always true. In a
reading-comprehension benchmark several questions share the same passage, and a model that
understands the passage tends to get all of them right, while one that doesn't tends to get all of
them wrong. In practice there's less information than it seems, and the margin has to be worked out
by group. Miller finds that the grouped margin can be more than three times the naive one: 3.05
times on DROP (*Discrete Reasoning Over Paragraphs*), a benchmark of questions about Wikipedia
paragraphs.

### Answers that change from one run to the next

If the model generates its answer by sampling among the most likely tokens, the same question can
get a different answer in two runs, and the score moves for that reason too. There are two remedies:
answer each question several times and average, or, when you have them, use the probabilities
instead of sampling, which removes that part of the noise. What Miller advises against is lowering
the sampling temperature just so the score moves less, because then you're measuring a different
model, one nobody uses that way.

### How many questions

All of this can be turned around for planning: decide what difference you want to be able to see and
work out how many questions it takes. Under the assumptions of a typical example of Miller's, seeing
a 3-point difference between two models with a reasonable probability (80%) takes about a thousand
questions, hence his recommendation that a new benchmark should have at least that many.

## When the exam runs out of road

A score has a floor and a ceiling, and both sit further from 0 and 100 than they seem.

The floor is chance. With four options, answering at random gets you 25%, and always answering D
gets you 26.9% on MMLU, because D is the key for 26.9% of its questions. A score of 30% isn't "30%
knowledge": it's barely more than nothing. That's why the 2024 version of Hugging Face's
leaderboard, when it changed exams, [rescales the
scores](https://huggingface.co/docs/leaderboards/en/open_llm_leaderboard/normalization) so that
chance is worth 0 and a perfect score 100: on a four-option exam, a raw 60% becomes 46.7.

The ceiling is the exam's own mistakes. A 2024 review, [*Are We Done with
MMLU?*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04127), estimated that 6.5% of MMLU's questions have some error
(a wrong key, an ambiguous question, more than one correct option), and that in virology the figure
is 57%. Not even the solved examples are spared: the first one in the geography prompt above says
"crude birth date" where it should say "crude birth rate". A perfect model wouldn't reach 100%, and
near the ceiling part of what separates two models is which one agrees more with the key's mistakes.
In June 2024, Anthropic and OpenAI published the same 88.7% for their flagship models, Claude 3.5
Sonnet and GPT-4o.

When a benchmark saturates, a harder one replaces it. [MMLU-Pro](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01574)
(2024) goes from four options to ten, which brings chance down to 10%, and swaps recall questions
for questions that require reasoning: models lose between 16 and 33 points going from MMLU to
MMLU-Pro. Along the way, the score depends less on the prompt: across 24 different prompt styles,
the variation drops from 4 or 5 points to 2. Hugging Face's leaderboard made that switch in 2024,
when it replaced its six exams, MMLU among them, with six harder ones, MMLU-Pro among them, and it
explained why: the best models were close to human level, some had probably been trained on the
questions, and some exams contained errors.

## How to read the number on a model card

In December 2023 Google launched Gemini Ultra, its most capable model, with 90.0% on MMLU, above the
89.8% estimated for a human expert. The table in the [technical
report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.11805) put it next to GPT-4's 86.4%. The two figures came from
different procedures. GPT-4's 86.4% was the one OpenAI had published, with five examples and a
single answer. Gemini's 90.0% used what the report calls CoT@32: the model reasons before answering
(*chain of thought*), does so 32 times and, if enough of the answers agree, takes the majority one;
if not, it answers directly without reasoning. In the appendix, the same report compares the two
models under the same procedure, measuring GPT-4 through OpenAI's API. With a single answer, picking
the most likely token at every step, Gemini Ultra scored 84.0% and GPT-4 84.2%. With the 32-answer
procedure for both, 90.0% and 87.3%. Same exam, same two models, and who wins depends on how the
question is asked.

Nothing in the table is false, and the procedure is written into it. But the figure that travels is
the headline one, and what you have to do with any benchmark score is ask how it was obtained. The
questions are always the same.

- **Which program, and which version?** The 2023 LM Evaluation Harness and today's don't give the
same results.
- **How was the answer read?** The most likely letter, the whole option or the generated text.
- **With how many examples, which prompt, and with or without reasoning?**
- **One answer or several?** And if several, how they're combined.
- **On which questions?** The whole exam or part of it, and how it was averaged.
- **With what margin?** And if it's compared with another model, whether it was on the same
questions.
- **Could the model have seen the exam?** If nobody checked, nobody knows.

Two figures can only be compared if they come from the same program with the same configuration. The
ones on a model card were measured by whoever built the model, with their program and their
configuration, and the ones on another card by another team with theirs.

## Takeaways

If you take away one thing, make it this: **a benchmark score is a measurement, and a measurement
depends on the instrument.** The exam fixes the questions and the key, but not how the question is
asked, how the answer is read, which questions count or how many there are, and any of those four
decisions moves the score by more than the gap between two models.

| before trusting the score, ask… | why |
|---|---|
| how the answer was read | LLaMA 65B scores 63.6% or 48.8% on MMLU; Qwen2.5 beats SmolLM2 by 27.5 points reading the letter, and by nothing without the options |
| which prompt, how many examples and in what order | moving the correct answer from A to D shifts LLaMA 30B's score by 27 points; a change of format, by up to 76 |
| whether the model could have seen the questions | some families lose up to 8 points on new problems (GSM1k) |
| on how many questions, and whether the comparison used the same ones | on 200 questions, 4 points can be noise or a real difference |
| where the floor and the ceiling are | with four options, chance gives 25%; 6.5% of MMLU's questions have errors |

And if you take away anything more, make it practical.

**Fix the instrument before comparing.** The same program, the same version, the same prompt, the
same examples and the same way of reading the answer for every model, and write it down next to the
score.

**Read the answer in a way the model can give.** A model that can't tie answers to letters scores
the same as chance if you read the letter, and one that can loses points if you read the whole
phrase. If you're comparing very different models, measure both ways, as OLMES does, and say which
one you used.

**Shuffle the options.** If the score changes when you move the correct answer, you're measuring the
model's taste for a letter.

**Compare on the same questions and put a margin on the difference.** What matters is how many
questions the two models disagree on.

**Be wary of an exam everyone passes.** Near the ceiling, the score measures the key's mistakes;
near the floor, little more than chance.
