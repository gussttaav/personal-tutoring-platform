I've been tutoring for years. In a good hour, something happens that no syllabus accounts for: a student asks *why* something works, I sketch three shapes on the whiteboard, and the idea clicks. By the next day the whiteboard has been wiped, and I have to redraw those same three shapes for the next person.

This blog exists so the drawing gets written down.

## What you'll find here

It isn't a journal. It's the material I teach from, arranged so you can read it without me in the room:

- **One concept at a time.** A single idea per article, with the mathematics included rather than tucked out of sight.
- **Specific mistakes.** The errors I see over and over in lessons, what causes them, and how to spot them.
- **Reading notes.** Papers and books worth your time, and a warning about the ones that aren't.

What you won't find is coverage of the latest trends. Other people do that far better than I would, and the field moves faster than anything careful can be written.

## How I plan to write it

The rule I follow when teaching applies here too: never skip the uncomfortable step. If a neuron computes its preactivation as $z = \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b$, the article will show where that dot product comes from, not just what it's called.

And wherever there's code, it will be code you can run and code you can doubt:

```python
import numpy as np

def preactivation(w, x, b):
    """A neuron's weighted sum, with no library standing in between."""
    return float(np.dot(w, x) + b)

w = np.array([2.0, -1.0, 3.0])
x = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 1.0])
print(preactivation(w, x, b=-6.0))  # -1.0
```

That's three lines of arithmetic, and the only library call is `np.dot`, which almost everyone would rather take for granted. That's exactly where people get lost.
