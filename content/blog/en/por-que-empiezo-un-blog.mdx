I have been tutoring for years. In a good hour something happens that no syllabus contains: the student asks *why* a thing works, I draw three shapes on the whiteboard, and the idea clicks. The next day that whiteboard is wiped, and the same three shapes have to be rebuilt for the next person.

This blog exists so that the drawing stays written down.

## What you will find here

It is not a journal. It is the material I teach from, arranged so it can be read without me in the room:

- **One concept at a time.** A single idea per article, with the mathematics included rather than tucked out of sight.
- **Specific mistakes.** The failures I watch repeat in lessons, what causes them, and how to spot them.
- **Reading notes.** Papers and books worth the hours, and a warning about the ones that are not.

What you will not find is trend coverage. Other people do it far better than I would, and the subject moves faster than anything careful can be written.

## How I plan to write it

The rule I follow when teaching holds here too: never skip the uncomfortable step. If a neuron computes its preactivation as $z = \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b$, then the article shows where that dot product comes from, not just what it is called.

And wherever there is code, it will be code you can run and code you can distrust:

```python
import numpy as np

def preactivation(w, x, b):
    """A neuron's weighted sum, with no library standing in between."""
    return float(np.dot(w, x) + b)

w = np.array([2.0, -1.0, 3.0])
x = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 1.0])
print(preactivation(w, x, b=-6.0))  # -1.0
```

Three lines of arithmetic, and the only library call is `np.dot`, which nearly everyone would rather take as read. That is exactly where people get lost.
