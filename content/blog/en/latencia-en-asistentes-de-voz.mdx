Arce Dental, an invented dental practice, answers its phone with a voice assistant. A patient calls
and asks:

> Have you got anything on Thursday afternoon for a clean?

And nothing happens. Two seconds go by, then three. The patient, unsure whether the line has dropped,
says "hello?" at the very moment the assistant starts to answer. They talk over each other, the
assistant hears the "hello?", stops to listen and starts again from the top. The answer was right:
there was a slot at five. The conversation was not.

In a text chat, that wait would have gone unnoticed. While the model checks the diary, the screen
shows three bouncing dots, and whoever is typing knows the answer is on its way. On the phone,
silence has no dots. All the patient knows about the assistant is what she hears, and what she hears
is three seconds of nothing, in a kind of exchange where people normally reply within a fraction of
a second.

This post is about those seconds: where they come from, how much each step takes between the moment
the patient stops talking and the moment she starts hearing the answer, why checking the diary,
searching documents or calling an external server multiplies them, and what can be done to win them
back. Every figure comes from a published source, linked where it appears. None of them was measured
here.

## How long a person takes to answer

In 2009, a team of linguists recorded spontaneous conversations in
[ten languages](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0903616106) across five continents, from
Japanese and Danish to Tzeltal in Mexico and Yélî Dnye in Papua New Guinea. In each one they looked
for the same moment: a yes/no question and its answer. They timed the interval from the end of the
question to the start of the answer, counting a gesture such as a nod as an answer too. The mean
across the whole set was 208 milliseconds. The most common value in each language lay between 0 and
200 ms, and the most common value overall was exactly 0: the answer begins the instant the question
ends.

There are differences between cultures, but they are small. The Japanese speakers in the sample
answered after 7 ms on average; the Danes, after 469. Every language's mean fell within about 250 ms
of the overall mean, roughly the length of one syllable.

Stivers' study looked only at yes/no questions, and Spanish was not among its ten languages. In 2022,
Mark Dingemanse and Andreas Liesenfeld [repeated the measurement](https://aclanthology.org/2022.acl-long.385/)
over every change of speaker in two-person conversations in 24 languages, Spanish and Catalan
included. The Spanish data were phone calls. Across 674,223 transitions the mean was 59 ms, and
almost half of all turns (46%) began slightly before the previous one had finished. A recent
measurement of Peninsular Spanish, based on [four recordings](https://doi.org/10.58859/resla.869),
points the same way: in the two everyday conversations, the next speaker started a median of 37 to
55 ms before the other had finished; in two TV panel shows, between 258 and 380 ms before.

Gaps this short are a puzzle for anyone who studies how speech is produced, because speaking is slow.
Naming a picture, a single word, takes about 600 ms from seeing it to starting to say it; describing
a simple scene, such as "the boy kicks the ball", around 1.5 seconds. Levinson and Torreira
[sum it up](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00731/full) like this: if the gap
between turns is 200 ms and preparing a reply takes between 600 and 1,500, the reply has to be
prepared while the other person is still talking. And that is what the experiments they cite show:
listeners start planning what to say as soon as they can, sometimes a second or more before the
other person finishes, and they predict when that will happen from what is being said and how.

So a person does not answer quickly because they think quickly, but because they do not wait. They
listen and prepare at the same time, and when the other person stops, the reply is almost ready.
That is the bar conversation sets, and almost nothing in a voice assistant works that way by
default: each step waits for the previous one to finish. Callers have never heard of these numbers,
but they have lived by them all their lives, and a one-second silence sounds to them like
hesitation, like not having been understood, or like a dropped call.

## What happens between speaking and hearing the answer

The most common way to build a voice assistant is a chain of three models, usually called a
*cascade*: one turns speech into text, a language model writes the reply, and a third turns that
reply back into speech. Between them sit the network, buffers, and a component that decides when
the user has finished speaking. The alternative, a single model that both listens and speaks, gets
its own section further down.

To put numbers on each step, the most useful reference I know of is
[Twilio's guide](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/blog/developers/best-practices/guide-core-latency-ai-voice-agents)
to latency in voice assistants, from November 2025. Twilio sells the telephony infrastructure many
of these assistants run on, and it publishes a budget by component with two figures for each: a
target (the median a newly launched assistant should reach without any special tricks) and an upper
limit. The overall target, from the moment the user stops speaking to the moment the reply reaches
their ear, is 1,115 ms, with a limit of 1,400. That is more than five times the human gap, and it is
a sensible starting point, not a poor result.

### The network and the audio

Speech leaves the microphone in packets. The codec most used for voice over the internet,
[Opus](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc6716), cuts it into frames of between 2.5 and 60 ms,
and its specification says 20 ms is a good choice for most applications. Each frame has to fill up
before it is sent, and the codec also needs a little audio ahead of it to compress well. Then comes
the journey: in Twilio's guide, 40 ms from the device to the first server, 30 in the jitter buffer
(packets do not arrive at a steady pace, so the buffer holds them for a moment to play them back
evenly) and 25 to decode. That is 95 ms before the server has even heard the end of the sentence.

And that journey is not made once. Twilio counts at least ten network trips in a turn: the voice on
the way in, the voice on the way back, and eight hops between services, because speech recognition,
the language model and speech synthesis are usually three providers in three places. Each hop adds
little if the services are close together and quite a lot if they are not. Light in optical fibre
covers about 200 km per millisecond, and Madrid is some 6,000 km from the US east coast as the crow
flies: 30 ms one way and 60 there and back as a physical minimum, and cables never run in a straight
line.

### Knowing you have finished

The clock starts when the user stops speaking, but the system does not know that at that instant.
People pause mid-sentence: to think, to breathe, to find a word. If the assistant answers at the
first pause, it interrupts; if it waits too long, it seems slow. Deciding when the turn is over is
called *endpointing* or *turn detection*, and Twilio's guide is blunt about it: it is usually the
slowest part of the whole chain, because it is tuned not to cut anyone off.

The simplest approach is a silence timer. A voice activity detector (VAD), a small model that tells
speech from background noise, marks when the speech stops, and once the silence lasts longer than a
threshold the turn is declared over. According to Twilio, most defaults sit around 500 ms. OpenAI's
voice API uses that same value in its
[example configuration](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/realtime-vad), and some
systems wait 700 or 800. Lowering the threshold is risky, the guide warns: an assistant that cuts
people off mid-sentence is worse than a slow one.

Half a second of silence is more than twice the entire human gap, and the timer only starts once
the speech has stopped. The obvious improvement is to stop merely counting silence and pay attention
to what was said and how. "Thursday afternoon, for a clean" sounds finished; "Thursday afternoon,
or else..." does not. That is what end-of-turn models do, and today they come in two kinds:

- Those that read the transcript. LiveKit, an open platform for building voice assistants, has one
  built on a small language model (Qwen2.5 with 500 million parameters) that estimates the
  probability that the sentence is complete, and the version it released in December 2025 cuts
  people off 39% less often than the one before.
- Those that listen to the audio. [Smart Turn](https://github.com/pipecat-ai/smart-turn), from
  Pipecat, another open platform, starts from the Whisper Tiny encoder, has about 8 million
  parameters, takes up 8 MB and runs in about 10 ms on some CPUs. Working on the audio means it can
  use intonation and rhythm, which say a great deal about whether someone has finished and which
  the transcript loses.

LiveKit's documentation shows nicely what one of these models buys you. Without it, the assistant
waits between 0.5 and 3 seconds of silence; with its detector, between 0.3 and 2.5, and the model
decides where to land within that range. In the [latest version](https://livekit.com/blog/turn-detector-v1-1),
from November 2026, which listens to the audio and also reads what the assistant said last,
detecting the end of a turn in English takes 462 ms if you accept cutting the user off in 5% of
turns. The trade-off is in that sentence: neither number means anything without the other. The
sooner you decide, the more often you cut someone off.

### Turning speech into text

While the user is speaking, automatic speech recognition (ASR) transcribes as it goes. If it works
in streaming mode, emitting text as the audio arrives rather than waiting for the whole recording,
nearly all of its work happens during the sentence, and at the end it only has to confirm the last
few words. That is why Twilio puts recognition and end-of-turn detection on the same line of the
budget, a 350 ms target and a 500 ms limit: they happen together, and what you are mostly waiting
for is the decision that the turn is over.

Not every recognition model works this way. Whisper, OpenAI's, was trained on chunks of up to 30
seconds, each containing a complete sentence, and it is not designed to transcribe on the fly. It
can be forced to, as [Whisper-Streaming](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.14743) does: re-transcribe the
accumulated audio every so often and accept only the part on which two consecutive passes agree.
But the result, in the paper that introduces it, is an average latency of 3.3 seconds in English.
For a conversation, recognition has to be built for streaming from the start.

### The model: the first token

With the turn closed and the transcript complete, the language model receives the text and starts
writing. What matters here is not how long it takes to write the whole reply, but how long it takes
to write the first word, because that word can already go to speech. This is measured as time to
first token (TTFT), and Twilio's target is 375 ms, with a limit of 750.

That time has two parts. First, the model reads everything it receives in one go: the system
instructions, the descriptions of the tools it can use, the conversation so far and the latest
sentence. That reading step is called *prefill*, and it grows with the length of the input. Then it
generates the first token, and from there the rest one at a time. A long conversation with long
instructions slows down the first token of every turn, however short the user's sentence.

The most effective tool against prefill is prompt caching: the provider keeps the result of having
read the start of a text, and the next request that begins the same way skips it. In the table
Anthropic used to [launch its own](https://claude.com/blog/prompt-caching), chatting with a
100,000-token book went from 11.5 to 2.4 seconds to the first token, and a 10,000-token prompt from
1.6 to 1.1. In voice, where the instructions and the tools are the same on every turn, caching is
close to mandatory, with one condition: the fixed part goes first and whatever changes goes last,
because the cache only helps with an identical beginning.

The other part of the first token is reasoning. Models that think before they answer generate
reasoning tokens first, which the user never hears, and the first token of the answer only arrives
after all of them. Artificial Analysis, which measures the speed of models from the main providers,
makes the distinction explicit: its "time to first answer token" includes the reasoning time. In
text, a model that thinks for ten seconds before answering can be worth the wait. In voice, those
ten seconds are silence. That is why voice assistants use fast models with little or no reasoning,
and would rather have a small one that answers now than a large one that answers better in four
seconds.

### From text to speech

Speech synthesis, or text-to-speech (TTS), takes text and returns audio. What counts is the time to
the first chunk of audio, because the rest plays while it is being generated: Twilio's target is
100 ms, with a limit of 250. It is the cheapest step in the chain, as long as it does not wait for
the whole reply.

The usual approach is not to feed it individual tokens, because the intonation of a sentence depends
on the whole sentence, but to collect what the model writes and send each sentence as soon as it is
complete. A Salesforce team that built a complete assistant from scratch and
[documented every step](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05413) calls this sentence buffer the critical
piece of the system: it is what lets the first sentence play while the model writes the second.

### Adding it up

With Twilio's targets, the sum goes like this: 95 ms of network on the way in, 350 to close the
turn and the transcript, 375 to the first token, 100 to the first audio, and 195 for the way back
and the hops between services, which is what remains to reach the total of 1,115. A real assistant
looks much the same: Salesforce's, with cloud services, gave medians of 402 ms for recognition, 457
for the first token and 221 for synthesis.

What stands out is where the time goes. The language model, which anyone would point to as the slow
part, takes a third. Almost another third is waiting for the user to finish, and the rest is network
and synthesis. No single step is expensive on its own; what is expensive is that they stand in a
queue.

## Overlapping instead of waiting

If each step waited for the previous one to finish completely, none of this would work.
Recognition would wait for the whole recording, the model would write the complete reply before
passing it to speech, and the speech would be generated in full before it started to play. Each of
those steps would take as long as its whole job, and the total would run to several seconds.

What lets a cascade fit into a second is the same thing that lets a person fit into 200 ms: each
step starts before the previous one ends. Recognition transcribes while the user talks. The model
starts writing as soon as it has the text, and its first sentence leaves for synthesis before it
writes the second. Synthesis starts sending audio before it finishes the sentence. What adds up is
not the durations but how long each step takes to start producing something.

Beyond that, the techniques that save time are well known, and OpenAI's guide to
[latency](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/latency-optimization) arranges them into seven
principles, which in voice translate like this: a faster model, shorter replies, less input (or the
same input, cached), fewer calls in a row, the remaining ones in parallel, making the wait feel
shorter, and not using a language model for what does not need one, such as saying "you're
welcome". There is one more that belongs to voice: put the three services close together, in the
same region or on the same machine, because every hop between them is a round trip.

## When the user interrupts

People interrupt each other in conversation. If the assistant is halfway through explaining the
different kinds of clean and the patient says "OK, OK, Thursday at five", the assistant has to stop
talking there and then. This is called *barge-in*, and it has more consequences than it seems.

Stopping is not just a matter of no longer playing audio. Twilio's guide lists four things to do:
flush the audio that has been sent but not yet played, stop the model that is still writing, cancel
any tools in progress, and clean up the conversation history. The last one is the least obvious. The
model writes faster than anyone speaks, so when the patient interrupts, the text of the reply is
usually well ahead of the audio. If the history keeps the whole reply, on the next turn the model
will believe it said things the patient never heard. OpenAI's voice API has a message just for
this, one that [truncates a reply](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/api-reference/realtime-client-events/conversation/item/truncate)
at the millisecond it was interrupted and deletes the text that never got spoken, so that nothing the
user has not heard stays in the context.

Interruptions also test end-of-turn detection, from the other side. An "uh-huh" while the assistant
is speaking is not an interruption; it is how people show they are still listening. A system that
falls silent at every "uh-huh" is as awkward as one that never stops.

## When the model has to go and look something up

So far, the assistant answers with what it already knows. But the Arce patient is not asking
anything the model knows: she is asking about Thursday's diary. To answer, the model has to check
it, and that is where the sums above stop holding. Twilio's guide admits as much from the outset:
its numbers cover the core latency of the chain, and it deliberately leaves out the spikes that come
from tools and reasoning.

### A tool is another full round

When a model uses a tool, it does not run it itself. It writes a request in a fixed format, for
example "check the diary for Thursday from 15:00 to 20:00", and the program around it runs the
request and hands back the result. With the result, the model makes a second call and writes the
reply. That brings three costs a cascade without tools never pays:

1. Writing the request. The model cannot run half of it: the tool needs the complete request, with
   all its arguments. You have to wait for the first token and for every other token of the request
   as well.
2. Running it. However long the tool takes: a query against a local database, a few milliseconds; a
   document search with re-ranking, seconds.
3. Starting again. The model receives the result and has to read it, along with everything before
   it, before writing the reply: another first token, with a longer input.

The Meta and Carnegie Mellon University team behind [Stream RAG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.02044)
measured those parts in a spoken dialogue system built on Qwen2.5 with 7 billion parameters, with a
search tool. The median time to write the query was 0.59 s; for the search, including re-ranking the
results, 2.78 s; and for starting to generate the answer afterwards, 2.52. Without the tool, the
model took 1.34 s to start answering; with it, 5.90. The tool improved the answers a great deal,
which went from getting 11.1% of the questions right to 26.3%, because the model stopped making
things up, but it more than quadrupled the wait.

In complete systems the difference looks the same. [EVA-Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.13841),
an evaluation of twelve voice assistants in whole conversations with tools, measured each one's mean
latency on turns with a tool call and on turns without. Every one was slower with a tool. The
difference ranged from 0.15 s, for a cascade with a fast open model, to 5.6 s; OpenAI's
GPT-Realtime-1.5 went from 1.0 to 2.4 s. The scale the authors use to score speed gives those turns
more leeway, to absorb the time the tool takes.

### RAG: searching before asking

Document search, known as RAG (retrieval-augmented generation, covered in depth in
[another post](/en/blog/de-rag-a-rag-agentico)), can be set up in two ways, and in voice the
difference matters.

In the classic form, the search is not one of the model's tools. The program takes the user's
transcript, uses it to find the most similar passages, and passes them to the model along with the
question. There is no first call to write a query: the query is the user's own sentence. The only
cost is the search, and it can be very small. A
[voice assistant for telecoms](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.04721) that searched a local vector index
(an index that finds the most similar texts without comparing against all of them, as
[this post](/en/blog/indices-vectoriales) explains) took 8 ms on average for the search, less than
a single network hop.

In the agentic form, the model decides whether to search, what to search for, and whether to search
again with what it found. It is more flexible, and every search is one more tool, with its three
costs. An agentic RAG that runs three searches in a row pays for three rounds.

Both forms carry a cost that does not show up in the search itself: the passages found are added to
the input, and the model has to read them before the first token. Five passages of five hundred
tokens are 2,500 more tokens of prefill on every turn, and since they change with every question,
they get no help from the prompt cache. When what you look up is small and fixed (the clinic's
opening hours, its prices, its policies), it is sometimes better not to search at all and put
everything in the instructions, where it does get cached; that is the idea behind
[cache-augmented generation](/en/blog/cuando-no-hace-falta-buscar).

### MCP: the tool is on another machine

MCP (Model Context Protocol) is the open protocol through which a model connects to tools offered
by another program, an MCP server, instead of having them built into the assistant itself. The
[specification](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-11-25/basic/transports) defines
two ways of talking to that server. With stdio, the assistant launches it as just another process on
the same machine and they talk over standard input and output, with no network involved. With
Streamable HTTP, the server lives somewhere else and every message is an HTTP request.

The convenience of MCP is that the clinic can connect its assistant to the server of the booking
software it already uses, without writing the integration. The cost is that every call to a remote
tool is at least one network round trip on top of whatever the tool takes. If the server is on
another continent, that is at least 60 ms per call from physics alone. If the connection is not
open yet, it has to be set up first: one trip for the TCP connection and another for TLS encryption
before the request. And up to the November 2025 revision of the protocol, a new session also began
with a handshake, the `initialize` message, which the July 2026 revision
[removed](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2026-07-28/changelog) when it made the
protocol stateless.

There is a second, less visible cost. The assistant asks the server for its list of tools and passes
it to the model on every turn, with each tool's name, description and parameters. A server with
forty tools means a lot of prefill tokens on every sentence the user says, even if only one is used.
The same 2026 revision asks servers to return the list in a fixed order, precisely so that the
model's prompt cache can reuse it.

### Several tools in a row

The worst case is not one tool but several that depend on each other. "Have you got anything on
Thursday afternoon? And if so, can you book it for me?" is at least two: check the diary and then,
with the result, make the booking. The second cannot start until the first has finished, and each
one is a full round. Agents that chain five or six calls in text without anyone noticing are, in
voice, fifteen seconds of silence.

## How to hide the wait

There are two ways to make an unavoidable wait feel shorter: say something in the meantime, and
start earlier. Both are in the figure above.

### The filler phrase

The oldest is to do what a receptionist would: "one moment, let me check". The model first writes a
short phrase, which goes off to speech synthesis, and then the request to the tool, which runs while
the phrase is playing. Twilio's guide mentions this as the way to mask delays from the model and the
tools, and the figure shows what it does: the first sound goes back to the 1,115 ms of the turn
without tools, even though the answer with the data still arrives just as late.

It works because the patient now knows she has been heard and that something is happening, which is
exactly what was missing in the opening example. But it has a clear limit. A filler phrase buys a
few seconds, not ten, and if it comes on every turn the assistant sounds like someone who cannot
answer anything without looking it up. There is also a less obvious risk: if the model speaks before
it knows what it is going to do, it may commit to something it then fails to deliver. In the
evaluation in [Full-Duplex-Bench-v3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.04847), a benchmark for voice
assistants with tools, Ultravox, an open voice model, was the fastest to produce a first sound (3.88 s
on average) but the slowest to reach the tool call (6.01). 88% of its replies opened with a filler
phrase, and in almost half of the turns it started speaking before the user had finished.

### Starting before they finish

The other way is the one people use: do not wait for the end of the sentence. If, halfway through
"Have you got anything on Thursday afternoon for...", it is already clear that Thursday's diary needs
checking, the lookup can go out at that point, on the half-finished transcript, and its result can be
ready when the patient stops. If the sentence turns out to ask for something else, the result is
thrown away. This is called *speculative execution*, and since 2025 several studies have measured it.

- Stream RAG launches searches on the partial transcript at regular intervals, or lets the model
  itself decide when to launch a new one, and cuts the time spent waiting for the search by around
  20%. Its authors point out that, with synthetic audio, they did not count end-of-turn detection,
  and that with human speech, which leaves more silence at the end, the saving is larger.
- A Qualcomm team did the same [on a phone](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07641), with a 3-billion
  parameter model, web searches, and the results kept in a cache that the model draws on when the
  transcript closes. The median fell from 5.79 to 4.60 s and the 99th percentile (the time exceeded
  by only one answer in a hundred) from 19.8 to 15.1. With a search of about ten seconds, the answer
  went from 17 s to 8.
- [Heartbeat Thinking](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26233) takes the idea further: a model reviews
  what it has heard every half second and fires off whatever tools it already can while the user is
  still talking. The right call arrived on average 4.4 seconds before the user finished, and the
  answer 4.4 s after the end, against 8.1 for the best cascade without speculation.

In the figure, the box for starting early assumes the request is clear one second before the user
finishes, which by these studies' standards is cautious. With a 2.78 s tool, that wins back almost a
second and a half: the one second of head start plus the time the turn used to take to close.

### What should not be hidden

Speculation has a price, and it is not only the searches that get thrown away. People correct
themselves mid-sentence ("Thursday, no, sorry, Friday"), and a tool launched too early can lock in
the wrong value. Full-Duplex-Bench-v3 studied this with recordings of real people who hesitate and
correct themselves. In one of its examples, an assistant searched for trips to the destination the
user had just corrected, because the call had gone out 2.3 seconds before they finished speaking.
Even the best system evaluated handled no more than 59% of the self-correction cases well, and the
cascade, 18%. In Heartbeat Thinking, 4% of the recordings ended up using outdated results.

Hence the rule all of these studies follow: only speculate with tools that read. Checking Thursday's
diary just in case does no harm; booking Thursday at five just in case does. Tools that change
something wait until the user has finished, and often until they have confirmed.

## Models that listen and speak

The cascade has a problem that is not about time: turning speech into text throws away everything
that is not text. A questioning tone, hesitation, irony, background noise, whether the speaker is a
child. And the language model cannot express any of that in its reply either, because it only writes
words. The alternative is a single model that takes audio in and gives audio out, usually called
*speech-to-speech*.

The clearest example is [Moshi](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.00037), from Kyutai, a French lab,
published in 2024. As well as being a single model, it is *full-duplex*: it listens and speaks at the
same time, continuously, as on a phone call, instead of taking turns. It is always generating audio,
which is sometimes speech and sometimes silence, and it decides for itself when to speak, without a
separate end-of-turn detector. Its theoretical latency is 160 ms, 80 for the audio frame it works
with and 80 more of margin, and in practice 200: the human gap. The voice APIs from OpenAI and Google
are also a single model that takes and returns audio, although they do not publish the details, and
OpenAI's, for one, still decides the turn with a separate detector.

Does that solve the problem? For conversation without tools, largely yes: in EVA-Bench, the
speech-to-speech systems answered the most turns on time, with mean latencies of 1 to 1.3 seconds
without tools, against 2.2 to 4.3 for the cascades evaluated. But with tools, the tool still takes
just as long, and the model still has to write the request and read the result. In
Full-Duplex-Bench-v3, where the tools were simulated and answered instantly, the speech-to-speech
models took between 4.3 and 8.4 seconds on average to deliver the data, and the cascade 10.1. Faster,
but a long way from 200 ms.

There are costs of another kind too. In a cascade you can choose each piece (the best recogniser
for the caller's language, the language model that is best with tools, the voice the clinic prefers),
and read what the model wrote before it is spoken and check it. A speech-to-speech model is more of
a black box, harder to evaluate, and usually offers less choice. The price of that freedom shows in
EVA-Bench as well: the three most accurate cascades averaged more than 5 seconds on turns with tools.

## How to measure it

If there is one practical recommendation to take from all the sources, it is to measure each step
separately and with percentiles. The mean latency hides what the user remembers, which is the time
they waited six seconds. What you look at is the median (what happens to the typical user) and a
high percentile, the 95th or 99th (what happens once in twenty or once in a hundred), and in a chain
of steps in a row, the high percentiles of each one add up at the worst possible moment. Salesforce's
assistant shows it: its model had a median of 337 ms to the first token, but the first request, with
the server still cold, took 4.3 seconds.

Measuring it means putting a timestamp on every boundary: when the user stopped speaking according
to the audio, when the turn was closed, when the first token arrived, when the first audio left,
when it started to play. The first two are not the same thing, and the difference between them is
the price of end-of-turn detection, the number people most often forget. And all of it is measured
with real speech over the real network: the studies above that use synthetic audio warn that human
speech leaves more silence at the end of a sentence, and that this changes the sums.

The figures in this post cannot be compared with one another without care: each source starts and
stops the clock in a different place, some include the network and some do not, some measure the
first sound and others the answer with the data. What they all share is the structure: where the
time goes and what multiplies it.

## Takeaways

- **The bar is below 200 ms**: that is the average gap on yes/no questions, and counting every
  change of speaker the mean drops to 59 ms, with almost half of all turns overlapping. No current
  assistant comes close, least of all with tools. A well-built cascade lands around one second
  without them; a sensible starting target, according to Twilio, is 1,115 ms.
- **The slowest step is usually deciding that the user has finished.** A half-second silence timer
  eats almost half the budget; an end-of-turn model that hears intonation and reads the words cuts
  it down, at the cost of interrupting someone now and then.
- **It all fits because the steps overlap**: streaming recognition, a model that starts writing as
  soon as it has the full text, sentences that go to speech as soon as they are complete. A step that
  waits to finish before producing anything breaks the whole budget.
- **Every tool is another round**: write the request, run it and read again. With one, the answer
  moves to several seconds; with several in a row, to more than ten. A RAG that searches with the
  user's sentence before calling the model saves the first part; an agentic one pays it on every
  search.
- **A remote MCP server adds network and tokens**: a round trip per call, more if the connection has
  to be opened, and the tool list read on every turn. A nearby server and a short list, in a fixed
  order and cached, bring that down to almost nothing.
- **The wait that is left can be hidden in two ways**: a filler phrase so the first sound arrives on
  time, and starting the search on the half-finished sentence so the data arrives sooner. The second
  only with tools that read, because people correct themselves.
- **Speech-to-speech models** get close to 200 ms in conversation, but with tools they still pay for
  every round, and they are harder to choose and to evaluate than a cascade.

Had the Arce patient heard "one moment, let me check" a second after she stopped talking, and had
the diary lookup gone out the moment she said "Thursday afternoon", the answer would have come a
couple of seconds later, with no silence in between and no "hello?". It is not a conversation
between two people, but it is close enough that nobody hangs up.
