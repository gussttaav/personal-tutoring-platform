A company that makes invoicing software (call it Albarán) has an assistant that answers questions
from its support team. Two new versions are being trialled, and the company wants to know which one
answers better before it replaces the one it uses. To find out, someone on the team puts together a
list of real questions, each with the answer a person who knows the product well would give. Albarán
works in Spanish, so the questions and answers are in Spanish; translations follow each one. One of
the questions is this:

> ¿El cliente de la incidencia 4812 tiene derecho a reembolso?
>
> *(Is the customer in ticket 4812 entitled to a refund?)*

The customer is Ortega Hardware, which is on the Team plan, paid its annual renewal on 14 August and
asked for a refund on 2 September. Albarán's FAQ says that "as a rule" refunds can be requested within
14 days, but the Team plan's terms give 30. The reference answer the team wrote reads:

> Sí. El plan Equipo da 30 días desde el cobro anual, que se hizo el 14 de agosto, así que el plazo
> acaba el 13 de septiembre.
>
> *(Yes. The Team plan gives 30 days from the annual charge, which was made on 14 August, so the
> window closes on 13 September.)*

Across the versions being compared, the assistant gives four different answers to that question:

> **A.** Puede pedirlo: con su plan dispone de treinta días desde que se le cobró la renovación, es
> decir, hasta el 13 de septiembre.
>
> *(They can claim it: their plan gives them thirty days from when the renewal was charged, that is,
> until 13 September.)*
>
> **B.** No. El plan Equipo da 14 días desde el cobro anual, que se hizo el 14 de agosto, así que el
> plazo acabó el 28 de agosto.
>
> *(No. The Team plan gives 14 days from the annual charge, which was made on 14 August, so the window
> closed on 28 August.)*
>
> **C.** Sí, hasta el 13 de septiembre.
>
> *(Yes, until 13 September.)*
>
> **D.** ¡Buena pregunta! Sí, el cliente tiene derecho al reembolso. Ferretería Ortega tiene
> contratado el plan Equipo, cuyas condiciones dan 30 días desde el cobro a las suscripciones
> anuales. El cobro se hizo el 14 de agosto, así que el plazo acaba el 13 de septiembre y la solicitud
> del 2 de septiembre llegó a tiempo. Te recomiendo tramitarlo cuanto antes. ¡Espero que te sirva de
> ayuda!
>
> *(Great question! Yes, the customer is entitled to the refund. Ortega Hardware is on the Team plan,
> whose terms give annual subscriptions 30 days from the charge. The charge was made on 14 August, so
> the window closes on 13 September and the request of 2 September arrived in time. I'd recommend
> processing it as soon as possible. I hope this helps!)*

Three are true. B is false: it applies the FAQ's general deadline to a plan that has its own. To
compare hundreds of answers like these without reading them one by one, the classic approach is to
measure how closely each one resembles the reference, and two of the most widely used measures for
that give this result.

Neither measure is broken. They do what they promise, which is to count the words an answer shares
with the reference. B shares 22 of its 27 words because it's a copy with five words changed, and
those five words are exactly the ones that make it false. Answer A says the same thing as the
reference in other words, and pays for it.

The case is invented. Albarán and ticket 4812 already appeared in the [article on
RAG](/blog/de-rag-a-rag-agentico) (retrieval-augmented generation, the pattern of searching
documents before answering), where the assistant made the same mistake as B, but you don't need to
have read it. What matters is the problem it exposes. Evaluating a classifier means counting correct
answers, because every input has one correct label. A model that writes doesn't have one correct
answer but thousands, and it also has wrong answers that look a lot like the right ones. Someone has
to decide, answer by answer, whether it's right.

There are four candidates for that job: a reference answer to compare against, a program that checks,
a person, and another model. Each sees different things, costs a different amount and fails in a
different way, and picking the wrong judge gives you very precise numbers about something other than
what you wanted to know. This article goes through all four, separates the two questions an
evaluation can answer, and looks at when to trust the number that comes out. It's the first in a
series: the ones that follow take each judge in turn.

## Grading text is not counting matches

A spam filter is a classifier: for each email it picks one of two labels, spam or not spam. To
evaluate it, a thousand hand-labelled emails and a tally are enough: **accuracy** is the share of
emails where the filter's label matches the person's. There are two possible answers and both are
known in advance, so comparing them is trivial.

A language model that answers questions doesn't pick between labels. It writes. Albarán's question
can be answered well in thousands of ways: with or without the date, with or without the reason,
formally or casually, in one sentence or five. Nobody can write down in advance the list of every
correct answer. At most, an example of one, and that's what the reference is.

There are two further complications. The first is that an answer can be partly right, and "right" has
several dimensions: an answer can be correct but confusing, complete but far too long, clear but in
the wrong tone for a customer. The second is that the model doesn't always say the same thing. When it
generates text, each word (or piece of a word) is drawn at random from among the most likely ones, so
the same question can produce different answers on two runs, and a single run is a sample.

The measure that language models have been compared with since the beginning,
[perplexity](/cursos/llm-agents/perplejidad), doesn't solve this. It measures how much probability the
model assigns to a text someone else wrote: how well it predicts, word by word, what comes next. It's
useful for comparing models that predict the same text, but it says nothing about what the model
writes when you ask it for something. A model can predict text very well and still not do what it's
asked, because [predicting isn't obeying](/cursos/llm-agents/predecir-no-es-obedecer). To evaluate
what a model writes you have to look at what it writes, and someone has to judge it.

## Four judges

The four judges can be ordered by what you need to know before the answer arrives. A reference needs
the answer to have been written already. A program needs you to know what to check. A person or a
model only needs to read, which is why they can judge any answer, including ones nobody foresaw. What
you gain in reach you lose in certainty: the reference and the program always give the same verdict
for the same answer, and the person and the model don't.

## First judge: a reference answer

### When the answer can be closed

If the answer is a number, a letter or a name, the comparison is exact: it either matches or it
doesn't. That's why many **benchmarks** (fixed sets of questions used to compare models) are built
this way, so they can mark themselves. [MMLU](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.03300) (Massive Multitask
Language Understanding, 2020) has 14,042 test questions with four options each, across 57 subjects
ranging from algebra to law, and it's marked by comparing a letter.
[GSM8K](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.14168) (Grade School Math, 2021) has about 8,500 primary-school maths
problems, 1,319 of them in the test set, and it's marked by comparing the final number.

The trick is to **close the question**: instead of asking for an explanation, ask for an option or a
number. At Albarán you could ask the assistant, just for the evaluation, to start with "sí" or "no"
(yes or no) and give the deadline in a fixed format. From then on, checking means comparing two
strings.

Even closed, the answer still has to be read, and how it's read changes the number. In 2023, the
public leaderboard of open models run by Hugging Face, the largest platform for sharing models, gave
LLaMA 65B, Meta's large model, a good deal less on MMLU than its own paper reported, and Hugging Face
[looked into why](https://huggingface.co/blog/open-llm-leaderboard-mmlu). There were three
implementations of the same benchmark. The original one looks at the probability the model gives each
of the four letters and takes the highest. The one in HELM (Holistic Evaluation of Language Models,
Stanford's benchmark) lets the model generate text and compares it with the expected answer. The one
in the tool the leaderboard used, the *LM Evaluation Harness* from the open research group EleutherAI,
compared the probability of each full option, letter and text. With the same questions and the same
model, the first gave 63.6%, the second 63.7% and the third 48.8%. Fifteen points apart without
touching either the model or the test.

### When it can't: counting words

A translation, a summary or an explanation can't be closed. For those, the tradition is **overlap**
measures, which count how much text the answer shares with one or more references. The one that set
the habit was [BLEU](https://aclanthology.org/P02-1040/) (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy), published
by IBM researchers in 2002 for machine translation. It counts how many sequences of one, two, three
and four words in the translation appear in human translations of the same sentence, and it penalises
translations that are too short. [ROUGE](https://aclanthology.org/W04-1013/) (Recall-Oriented Understudy
for Gisting Evaluation, 2004) did the same for summaries, from the other side: how much of the
reference appears in the summary.

The two measures in the opening figure belong to this family. The first is **word F1**. If the answer
has $n_r$ words, the reference has $n_{\text{ref}}$ and the two share $c$, then *precision* $P$ is the
part of the answer that's in the reference and *recall* $R$ is the part of the reference that's in
the answer. F1 is their harmonic mean, which is only high when both are:

$$
P = \frac{c}{n_r}, \qquad R = \frac{c}{n_{\text{ref}}}, \qquad F_1 = \frac{2PR}{P + R}.
$$

Words are counted ignoring capitals and punctuation, each one as many times as it appears in both. B
has 27 words, the reference another 27, and they share 22, so $P = R = 22/27$ and $F_1 = 0.81$. C
has 6 words and shares 5: its precision is very high, $5/6 = 0.83$, but it only covers $5/27 = 0.19$
of the reference, and F1 stays at $0.30$. For this measure, being brief is a flaw. Answer A shares
10 of its 23 words and scores $0.40$.

The second is **ROUGE-L**, the variant of ROUGE that also requires order. Instead of shared words it
counts the *longest common subsequence*: the largest number of words that appear in both in the same
order, even if they aren't next to each other. The formula is the same, with that length in place of
$c$. B keeps the reference's order across all 22 shared words and scores $0.81$ again. Answer A has
only 9 in order and drops to $0.36$.

In translation, counting words worked reasonably well. A sentence has few good translations and they
share most of their words, and BLEU was designed to average over thousands of sentences, where the
errors on any one sentence cancel out. With open-ended answers, both conditions fail. In 2016,
[a study of dialogue systems](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.08023) compared these measures with human
judgement, and the correlation was very weak on Twitter conversations and absent on Ubuntu technical
support. One word can flip the meaning ("no" for "sí", 14 for 30) and barely count, while a correct
paraphrase loses points for not repeating the exact words.

There are versions that compare meanings instead of exact words.
[BERTScore](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.09675) (2019) pairs each word in the answer with the most
similar word in the reference, using the vectors a language model (BERT, the 2018 Google model it's
named after) gives each word in context, so "treinta" (thirty) and "30" can count as the same. That
helps answer A, but it doesn't fix answer B: a similarity measure still measures similarity, and B
is extremely similar to the reference. Its error is in two numbers and one word.

Overlap still has its place: when the answer can be closed (and then exact match is enough), in
translation at corpus scale, and as a cheap alarm when something changes: if the same system's score
on the same questions suddenly drops, something has changed. For deciding whether a particular answer
is correct, it's no use.

## Second judge: a program that checks

Instead of comparing against an example of a correct answer, you can write a check that defines what
correct means. With code, that's the obvious thing to do: run the tests.
[HumanEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.03374), which OpenAI released in 2021 alongside Codex, its model
for writing code, is 164 hand-written programming problems, each with its unit tests; a solution counts
if it passes all of them, however it's written. Because the model can generate several different
solutions, the result is reported as *pass@k*: the probability that at least one of $k$ solutions
passes. Codex solved 28.8% of the problems with one attempt and 70.2% with a hundred.

With maths, you extract the final result and compare it as a number, which is what GSM8K does. With
format, you validate: a JSON object (JavaScript Object Notation, the most common data format between
programs) against its schema, a date against a pattern. [IFEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.07911)
(Instruction-Following Eval, Google, 2023) took this to instructions: around 500 prompts containing
instructions a program can check, of 25 kinds, such as "write more than 400 words" or "mention the word
AI at least three times".

At Albarán, a program that checks whether the answer says "sí" or "no" and which date it gives
passes answers A, C and D and fails B. It doesn't care about wording, length or tone, and it's exact
about what it checks. It has two limits.

The first is that **it only checks what it was told to check.** The answer «No: el plazo vencía el 13
de septiembre y el cliente lo pidió tarde» ("No: the window closed on 13 September and the customer
asked too late") is false and has the right date, so a program that only looked for the date would
pass it. And the more you ask a program to understand, the more it turns into a fragile natural
language parser. The fix is the same as with the reference: close whatever can be closed. If, for the
evaluation, the assistant returns a field with the decision and another with the deadline alongside
its text, the program compares fields and is always right. Whatever stays in the free text (whether
the explanation is clear, whether it makes something up) goes unjudged.

The second is that **what can be checked can also be optimised.** Models that reason before answering
are trained with reinforcement learning, and checks like these are one of their cheapest reward
signals. [DeepSeek-R1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948), the reasoning model the Chinese company
DeepSeek released in January 2025, learnt to reason mostly from rule-based rewards: whether the final
answer was correct and whether it followed the requested format. That has two consequences for anyone
evaluating. A check a model was trained against no longer measures the same thing on the questions it
was trained on, so evaluation questions have to be kept apart. And if the check can be satisfied
without doing the task (tests that miss the hard case, a correct format with empty content), a model
optimised against it will find the way.

## Third judge: a person

When what matters can't be written as a check (whether the explanation makes sense, whether the tone
suits a customer, whether the summary captures what's important), what's left is reading. A person
can judge in two ways. They can **score** each answer on a scale, say 1 to 5 for clarity, or they can
**compare** two answers and say which they prefer. Comparing is easier and more stable, because a 4
means different things to different people, while "this one is better than that one" much less so.
It's the same reason chat models are tuned on pairwise preferences rather than scores, and the
[Bradley–Terry model](/cursos/llm-agents/modelo-de-recompensa) turns many comparisons into one score
per answer: the probability that one beats another depends only on the difference between their
scores, as in the Elo rating system used in chess.

The best-known application is [Chatbot Arena](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.04132), now LMArena,
launched in 2023 by a group at the University of California, Berkeley. Anyone types a question, gets
two answers from two anonymous models and votes for the one they prefer. By March 2024 it had
collected over 240,000 votes, and its ranking, computed with Bradley–Terry, is the most cited ranking
of chat models.

As a judge, a person has three limits.

**They judge what they know.** Someone voting in an arena doesn't know the terms of the Team plan.
To them, B is a firm, specific, coherent answer, and nothing in it gives it away. To judge whether
an answer is correct you need people who know the facts, which at Albarán means the support staff
and in medicine means doctors. What you get from everyone else is preference, not correctness.

**They also prefer on style.** LMArena measured this in 2024 with its [style
control](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2024-08-28-style-control): once the effect of length and
formatting (headings, bold, lists) was discounted, the ranking changed, and length was the factor
that weighed most. D would stand a good chance in front of almost anyone: it's long, friendly and
complete, and it's long partly because of sentences that add nothing.

**Whatever gets voted on ends up being optimised.** In April 2025, Meta entered an experimental
version of Llama 4 Maverick in LMArena, "optimized for conversationality", and it reached second place.
The version Meta actually released, evaluated later in the same arena,
[came 32nd](https://techcrunch.com/2025/04/11/metas-vanilla-maverick-ai-model-ranks-below-rivals-on-a-popular-chat-benchmark/),
and LMArena changed its rules. A study from the same year,
[*The Leaderboard Illusion*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.20879), counted 27 private variants Meta had
tested in the arena before launching Llama 4: testing many and publishing the best is, in part,
keeping the one that got luckiest.

On top of that, people don't fully agree with each other, and they're slow and expensive. In the study
cited in the next section, two experts comparing the same two answers picked the same one 81% of the
time when ties were left out. And the experts who know the facts are precisely the most expensive.

## Fourth judge: another model

The fourth judge is a language model that's given the question, the answer (or two, to compare) and
instructions on what to look for, and asked for a verdict: what's known as *LLM-as-a-judge*. Its
standard validation was published in 2023 alongside [MT-Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685), a set
of conversation questions over several turns (hence the MT, for multi-turn). GPT-4 as a judge agreed
with human experts on 85% of comparisons without ties, more than the experts agreed with each other,
at 81%. It's cheap, fast, can judge any answer and explains its verdict. That's why it's now the most
widely used judge for open-ended answers.

But it has biases, and the same paper measured the main ones.

**Position.** When the order in which two answers were shown was swapped, GPT-4 kept its verdict only
65% of the time, and in 30% of cases it preferred whichever answer came first. The remedy is cheap:
judge each pair twice, in both orders, and count it as a tie when the two verdicts disagree.

**Length.** Judges prefer long answers. In the same paper, padding answers by repeating their
content as a list fooled the judge 91% of the time with Claude-v1 and GPT-3.5, and 9% of the time
with GPT-4. AlpacaEval, a benchmark that uses a model as its judge, added a [length
correction](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.04475) in 2024, and its correlation with the Chatbot Arena
ranking went from 0.94 to 0.98. At Albarán, D is the obvious candidate to win on length.

**Self-preference.** A judge tends to score text written by itself, or by its own family of models,
more highly. A 2024 study of
[judges' preference for their own text](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076) found that the better a
model recognises what it wrote, the more it prefers it.

And, like a person, **it only knows what it's told.** Without the Team plan's terms, B is a
plausible answer and the judge has nothing to contradict it with. With the reference or the terms in
its instructions, it can check it. The practical rule is to give the judge what a human expert would
need to judge.

Before trusting a judge, measure it. Take a sample of answers, between fifty and a hundred, judge them
by hand, judge them with the model, and compare. The agreement rate tells you how far to trust it, and
the disagreements tell you what the judge can't see: they're almost always fixed by changing its
instructions or giving it more information. Once validated, the judge can mark the thousands of
answers that remain, and the hand-marked sample is repeated whenever the judge or the answers change.

## Two different questions

An evaluation can answer two questions that tend to get mixed up.

**Which model is better?** This is the question benchmarks and public leaderboards answer: fixed
questions, the same for every model, which let you compare models with each other and over time. They
serve researchers, and anyone choosing which models to try.

**Does my system work?** This is Albarán's question, and no public benchmark answers it, because none
of them contains the Team plan. A system is a model plus everything around it: the instructions, the
documents it searches, the output format, its users' questions. The top model on a leaderboard may
not be the top one on Albarán's questions, and a change to the instructions can move the result more
than switching models.

The second question can only be answered with your own questions. Thirty or fifty real ones are
enough to start, each annotated with what a correct answer must contain: the decision, the date, the
source. Every failure you later see with real users becomes another question. And this is where the
four judges combine: a program for whatever can be closed (the decision, the date, the format), a
model validated against people for whatever can't (whether the explanation is clear, whether it makes
up terms), and people to review a sample from time to time.

## When to trust a number

Once the judge is chosen, you're left with a number, and there are five reasons not to trust it
blindly.

### Noise

A score on $n$ questions is an estimate: the share of correct answers the model would get on all
questions like those, measured with a sample. If a model gets a proportion $p$ of $n$ questions
right, the 95% margin of error is roughly

$$
\pm 1.96 \sqrt{\frac{p\,(1 - p)}{n}}.
$$

With 70% correct on 200 questions, $1.96 \sqrt{0.7 \cdot 0.3 / 200} \approx 0.064$: the result is 70%
give or take 6.4 points. If another model scores 74% on a different 200 questions, the margin on the
difference between the two is about 8.8 points, and a 4-point lead says nothing about which is better.
Comparing them on the same questions helps, because each question's difficulty affects both equally
and cancels out. Miller works through this, with the formulas for comparing models and for deciding
how many questions you need, in
[*Adding Error Bars to Evals*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00640) (2024).

The margin shrinks with the square root of $n$: four times the questions only halves it. With MMLU's
14,042 questions, a 70% score has a margin of 0.76 points, and a one-point gap can be real. With the
fifty questions an evaluation of your own starts with, the margin is 12.7. The formula is an
approximation that breaks down with few questions or with proportions near 0 or 100%, where better
intervals exist, but it's the order of magnitude that matters. And if the model generates by drawing
words at random, running the same test again gives different numbers: either fix the randomness or
average several runs.

### The instrument

This is what happened to LLaMA 65B on MMLU: the same model on the same questions gives results fifteen
points apart depending on how the answer is read. The same goes for the text around each question,
the worked examples shown to the model beforehand, and how many of them there are. Two results can
only be compared if they come from the same tool with the same settings, and the number on a model's
spec sheet was measured by whoever built the model, with their own tool.

### Contamination

Benchmarks are public, they're on the web, and models are trained on the web. If the test questions,
or their answers, were in the training data, the score partly measures memory. In 2024 the data
company Scale AI wrote [GSM1k](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.00332): 1,205 new problems with the style
and difficulty of GSM8K that were never published. Some families of models lost up to 8 points moving
from GSM8K to GSM1k (Yi; Phi and Mistral, around 6), while frontier models such as GPT, Claude and
Gemini barely changed. What's more, the models that found it easiest to reproduce GSM8K problems
tended to lose the most. The defence is to have questions nobody has seen, and an evaluation of your
own already is one.

### Saturation

When the best models get close to the ceiling, a benchmark stops telling them apart. In June 2024,
Anthropic and OpenAI reported the same 88.7% on MMLU for Claude 3.5 Sonnet and GPT-4o, their leading
models. And near the ceiling, what's left also measures the test's own mistakes: a 2024 review,
[*Are We Done with MMLU?*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04127), estimated that 6.5% of its questions
contain errors (a wrong answer key, an ambiguous question, more than one correct option), rising to
57% in virology. The response has been harder benchmarks. [GPQA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.12022)
(Graduate-Level Google-Proof Q&A, 2023) is 448 biology, physics and chemistry questions written by
experts so that they can't be answered by searching Google: experts in the field get 65% right, and
skilled people from other fields 34%, even after spending more than half an hour searching the web.
[*Humanity's Last Exam*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.14249) (2025) is 2,500 questions across dozens of
subjects, written by specialists so that they can't be answered by looking things up. Each new
benchmark takes less time than the last to saturate.

### Goodhart

"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." That's Goodhart's law, in the
wording of the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern, and it runs through everything above: the version of
Llama 4 built for the arena, the long answers that win over judges, the checks turned into training
rewards. The more a number decides (which model gets released, which one gets bought), the more it's
optimised against and the less it measures. The defence is the usual one: measure with several judges
and keep some questions to yourself.

## What comes next

Three things are changing how evaluation is done.

**Rubrics.** Between a check's number and a judge's opinion there's room for a list of concrete
criteria for each question, written by experts and checked one by one by a model.
[HealthBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.08775), which OpenAI released in May 2025, has 5,000 medical
conversations with 48,562 criteria written by 262 physicians, and a model that checks each criterion.
At Albarán, the rubric for ticket 4812 would say "answers yes", "gives 13 September", "relies on the
Team plan's terms" and "doesn't invent deadlines". It's the program's closed check and the judge's
reading combined, and in 2026 it's one of the most common ways to evaluate open-ended answers in
specialised fields.

**Long tasks.** An agent, a model that acts over several steps with tools, doesn't give an answer but
follows a path, and what gets evaluated is the state it leaves things in at the end. METR (Model
Evaluation & Threat Research), an independent organisation that evaluates models, measures their
[*time horizon*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14499): the length of tasks, measured by how long a human
expert takes to do them, that a model completes half the time. Between 2019 and 2025 it doubled every
seven months, and METR's [January 2026 update](https://metr.org/blog/2026-1-29-time-horizon-1-1/)
estimates that since 2023 it has doubled every 131 days. A task that takes hours can't be marked by
counting words or by a quick read: it needs an environment that checks the result at the end.

**Models that notice the test.** The most capable models are getting better at recognising when
they're being evaluated. In September 2025 Anthropic
[reported](https://transformernews.ai/p/claude-sonnet-4-5-evaluation-situational-awareness) that
Claude Sonnet 4.5 said it suspected it was being tested in around 13% of the conversations in its
automated evaluations. An instrument that changes what it measures forces evaluations to look more and
more like real use.

## What to take away

If you take away one thing, let it be this: **evaluating what a model writes means deciding who has
the authority to say an answer is right.** A reference only works if the answer can be closed. A
program is right about what it checks and blind to everything else. A person and a model can judge
anything, but they only see what they know, and each has its own biases. The more open the answers a
judge can handle, the less certain its verdict.

| if the answer… | judge | example |
|---|---|---|
| is a number, an option or a name | exact match | the deadline; MMLU |
| can be run or validated | a program | code tests; a JSON object against its schema; "under 100 words" |
| is open-ended, but you know what it must contain | a model with a rubric, validated against people | that it says yes, relies on the plan and doesn't invent deadlines |
| is a matter of taste | people, comparing pairs | which answer your users prefer |
| is a translation, and there are thousands | overlap, at corpus scale | BLEU |

And if you take away anything else, let it be practical.

**Close whatever can be closed.** If part of the answer has a correct value, ask for it in a field and
check it with a program. Save judgement for the rest.

**Don't use word overlap to decide whether an answer is correct.** It measures resemblance to an
example. Albarán's answer B is the proof.

**Validate the judge before you trust it.** A sample marked by hand, the agreement rate and a read
through the disagreements. In comparisons, judge in both orders, watch for length and give the judge
the facts an expert would need.

**Put a margin on every number and compare on the same questions.** With 200 questions, a 4-point gap
tells you nothing.

**Use public leaderboards to pick candidates, and your own questions to decide.**
