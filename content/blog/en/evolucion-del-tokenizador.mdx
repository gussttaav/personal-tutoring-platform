"El niño juega en el jardín." Six Spanish words and a full stop. The tokeniser GPT-2 was trained
with in 2019 needed twelve tokens to write it down, while its English translation, "The boy plays in
the garden.", got by on seven. In 2022, with the tokeniser used by GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, the Spanish
came down to eleven. In 2024, with GPT-4o's, it came down to seven: exactly as many as English, and
this time with the words left whole.

English stood still: seven tokens across all three generations, cut in the same places each time.
What moved was Spanish, and not because anyone fixed it; the language never entered into the
equation. What changed was the size of a list, and who was on it.

This article is about how that list gets built. I'll go through it in the order it happened, because
that order is what makes it make sense: how text was cut before any of this existed and why that way
of cutting stopped working, where BPE came from (a compression algorithm from 1994 that was given a
different job), what really separates the three methods in use today, why a floor made of bytes
ended up underneath all of them, and what the choice still costs you when the text you write isn't
English.

Not one of those decisions was made with Spanish in mind.

## The age of the word, and why it ended

A language model doesn't read text; it reads numbers. Before any neural network gets involved,
someone has to break the string of characters into units and give each unit an index. Those units are
called **tokens**, the closed list of the ones the model recognises is the **vocabulary**, and the
program that decides where to cut is the **tokeniser**.

The index on its own says nothing: "casa" being entry 412 and "perro" entry 413 doesn't make them
alike, any more than two consecutive phone numbers belong to two neighbours. What the model learns is
a vector of $d$ numbers for every vocabulary entry, its **embedding**, with the same $d$ for all of
them: a few hundred in small models, a few thousand in large ones. Stacked, those vectors form a
matrix of $\lvert V \rvert \times d$ that is trained along with the rest of the network, and at the
end of the model, in the layer that decides which token comes next, there's another matrix of the
same size.

That's why the vocabulary is fixed before training and never touched again: every entry costs two
rows of $d$ numbers, and the number of rows has to be known before you start. It's finite, closed
and expensive. Language is none of those things, and everything that follows comes out of that
collision.

For decades, deciding where to cut meant writing rules. A tokeniser of that era was a list of cases:
cut on spaces, separate the final punctuation from the word but not the full stops in "EE. UU.", keep
opening and closing quotes together, decide what to do about hyphens. The two most used in research,
Penn Treebank's and Moses', were exactly that, and they came with some small print: the rules are
per language. The one that knows "don't" is two words knows nothing about "dámelo", and neither is
any use for Chinese, which doesn't separate words with spaces.

The underlying problem wasn't the rules, which can be written. It was the size of the list they
produced.

To see it in numbers rather than adjectives, I put together three public-domain Spanish books, all
from Project Gutenberg and all downloadable as plain text:
[*Don Quijote*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2000), by Cervantes;
[*La Regenta*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/17073), by Leopoldo Alas;
and [*Amar es vencer*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/24925), by Pauline Caro.
That comes to 746,368 words in total, counting as a word any sequence of symbols separated by a
space and lowercasing everything.

The vocabulary that comes out has 38,792 distinct words, distributed the way it always is in any
language: a word's frequency is inversely proportional to its rank,

$$
f(r) \;\propto\; r^{-\alpha},
$$

with an $\alpha$ of $1.09$ in this corpus. Ten words ("de", "que", "y", "la", "a"…) take up an
enormous share of the text, and behind them comes a tail that never ends. To put a number on it: of
those 38,792 distinct words, **17,536 appear exactly once**. Nearly half the vocabulary exists to be
used a single time in 746,368 words.

That's already uncomfortable, because every vocabulary entry costs one row of numbers to represent
it and another in the output layer, so you end up paying two rows for a single appearance. But the
serious problem is the other one: the list never stops growing. Having read half the corpus, you'd
seen 22,643 distinct words; reading the other half brings 16,149 more. The text doubles and the
vocabulary grows by 71%.

That curve has a name and a closed form. It's known as Heaps' law, and it says the vocabulary grows
as a power of the amount of text read:

$$
\lvert V \rvert \;\approx\; K\,N^{\beta}.
$$

Here $N$ is how many words you've read (746,368 at the end of the corpus), $\lvert V \rvert$ is how
many distinct ones have appeared by that point, and $K$ and $\beta$ are two constants fitted to the
corpus: $K$ sets the height of the curve and $\beta$, the one that matters, sets its slope on the
logarithmic axes of the figure. In any language, $\beta$ falls between $0.4$ and $0.8$; in this
corpus it's $0.73$, with $K = 2.4$. That $\beta$ is less than $1$ means each new word is a little
harder to find than the last; that it's greater than $0$ means you'll never stop finding them.
There's no corpus size beyond which the vocabulary is complete, and that's because Spanish isn't a
list: it's a machine for generating words, with its conjugation, its diminutives and its knack for
gluing two clitics onto an imperative.

What the first generation of neural machine translation systems did was the only thing it could: cut
the list wherever it could be afforded. The cut wasn't aesthetic; it was arithmetic. The layer that
decides the next word computes a score for every vocabulary entry, which costs on the order of
$\lvert V \rvert \cdot d$ operations at every step of every sentence, with the same $d$ as the
embeddings. With $\lvert V \rvert$ in the hundreds of thousands, that layer alone eats the budget.
Hence the vocabularies of 30,000 to 80,000 words in those systems, and hence the
[research devoted purely to making a larger vocabulary usable](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.2007).

And the words left outside? They were replaced by a single symbol, `UNK`, and on you went. Measure
what that means on the corpus above: train the vocabulary on 90% of the text and read the remaining
10%. **4.8% of the words you read aren't in the vocabulary**, and they aren't oddities: that's one
in every twenty ordinary words. All of them reach the model as the same symbol, indistinguishable
from one another: the proper noun, the technical term and the typo share a single representation.
And since translation has to produce text, `UNK` came out the other side as well, so people had to
[invent patches](https://arxiv.org/abs/1410.8206) that recorded where each `UNK` came from, in order
to copy the original word or look it up in a dictionary afterwards.

A tokeniser that needs a patching system for the words it couldn't represent is saying, under its
breath, that the chosen unit is the wrong one.

## The flight to the character, and what it costs

If the problem is that words run out before the language does, there's an obvious way out: don't use
words. Use letters. The vocabulary becomes the alphabet, a few dozen symbols, and in the figure above
it's the flat line: with a thousand words read, you've already seen almost every letter Spanish
uses, and reading a million more adds practically none.

Everything that hurt is fixed at a stroke. No `UNK` is possible, because any word that arrives is
written with letters already in the vocabulary. The output layer stops being the bottleneck, because
deciding between forty symbols is free compared with deciding between fifty thousand. And related
words stop being strangers to each other: "niño" and "niña" share three of their four letters,
something a vocabulary of words can't even express. There were serious character-level models, and
they worked.

The price shows up the moment you count steps. Call $C$ the number of characters in a text, $T$ the
number of tokens a tokeniser breaks it into and $\bar{\ell}$ the mean length of those tokens, in
characters. Tokens are pieces of the text itself, so their lengths add up to the whole text, and the
three quantities are tied together:

$$
T = \frac{C}{\bar{\ell}}.
$$

The text fixes $C$, so $T$ and $\bar{\ell}$ can't move independently: the shorter the pieces, the
longer the sequence, and you can't have both. Cutting by characters gives $\bar{\ell} = 1$ and
therefore $T = C$. On the corpus above, that means that where the word tokeniser saw 746,368 units,
the character one sees 4,194,315: nearly six times as many steps for the same text.

And the cost isn't multiplied by six but by quite a bit more, because the parts that compare each
position with all the others (attention, which is what today's models are built from) grow as
$T^{2}$:

$$
\frac{T_{\text{char}}^{2}}{T_{\text{word}}^{2}} \;=\; \bar{\ell}_{\text{word}}^{2} \;\approx\; 32.
$$

Thirty-two times the work, for the same text, and with the model having to learn how to spell along
the way. There's a second cost, less visible and worse: the distance between two related words in a
sentence is multiplied by six too, so the model has to hold much longer dependencies to discover the
same thing.

With the two obvious ways out ruled out, the trade-off is laid out in full:

| cut | $\bar{\ell}$ | $T$ | $\lvert V \rvert$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| characters | $1$ | $C$ | dozens, and it saturates |
| subwords | $3$–$4$ | $\approx C/3.5$ | 30,000–200,000, *chosen in advance* |
| words | $\approx 5.6$ | $\approx C/5.6$ | tens of thousands, and growing |

The middle row is the one that won, and its right-hand cell says what it consists of: instead of
letting the size of the vocabulary be a consequence of how you cut, you fix it up front and leave an
algorithm to decide which pieces make it up. Frequent words end up as a single piece and rare ones
get written with several, without anyone having to decide which are which.

So the flight to the character worked, and nobody stayed to live there. The answer turned out to be
that nobody should choose the unit.

## BPE: a compression algorithm repurposed

The algorithm that ended up solving this is called *byte-pair encoding*, BPE from here on, and the
name says exactly what it does: it encodes pairs of bytes.
[It was published in 1994](https://web.archive.org/web/20210329111703/http://www.pennelynn.com/Documents/CUJ/HTML/94HTML/19940045.HTM)
in a magazine for C programmers, and its goal was to compress files. Find the most repeated pair of
bytes, replace it throughout the file with a byte that doesn't appear in it, and repeat. Every one of
those substitutions has to be recorded so it can be undone later, and the place where they're
recorded is the **substitution table**: a list of lines saying "the byte 0xFE means the pair AB".

Twenty-two years later, [a paper on machine translation](https://arxiv.org/abs/1508.07909) took that
algorithm out of compression and gave it a different job. The authors didn't change the algorithm.
They changed when it stops.

The difference lies in what you do with that table. In compression, it's a necessary evil that
travels with the file, because without it nobody can recover the original. In tokenising, nothing
needs to be recovered: you stop at 30,000 substitutions because that's the vocabulary size you
wanted, and **the table is the tokeniser**. Everything a BPE tokeniser knows fits in that ordered
list of pairs.

### Training

The corpus is first counted by words, with their frequencies, and each word is split into characters
(characters, yes, despite the algorithm's name; the actual bytes come back later, and not by
accident). An end marker, `</w>`, is glued to the end of each word, which stops a piece from crossing
from one word into the next and, above all, records where a space used to be. That second part is a
requirement, not a flourish: concatenating the tokens has to give back the text you started with,
spaces included, because a tokeniser that loses information along the way is no longer dividing the
text, it's altering it, and what the model is denied it will never see.

Let $f(w)$ be the frequency of the word $w$ in the corpus and $c_w(a,b)$ the number of times the
pieces $a$ and $b$ appear next to each other inside $w$. Summing over all words, weighting each by
how often it occurs, gives how many times that pair appears in the whole corpus:

$$
n(a,b) \;=\; \sum_{w} f(w)\, c_w(a,b).
$$

On each round, the pair with the highest count is merged,

$$
(a,b)^{\star} \;=\; \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{(a,b)} \; n(a,b),
$$

and this is repeated as many times as you want pieces added to the vocabulary. That's all there is to
it. There's no grammar, no morphology and no notion of a word beyond the end marker.

```python
from collections import Counter

CORPUS = """
el niño juega en el jardín la niña juega en el jardín
el niño aprende programación la niña aprende programación
la programación se aprende programando y programando se aprende
el profesor enseña programación la profesora enseña programación
enseñar no es lo mismo que aprender el niño enseña a la niña
la niña enseña al niño programación y el niño aprende
"""

def pairs(vocab):
    """Count every adjacent pair of pieces, weighted by the word's frequency."""
    counts = Counter()
    for pieces, f in vocab.items():
        for pair in zip(pieces, pieces[1:]):
            counts[pair] += f
    return counts

def merge(vocab, pair):
    """Replace every occurrence of `pair` with the piece that results from gluing it."""
    a, b = pair
    updated = {}
    for pieces, f in vocab.items():
        out, i = [], 0
        while i < len(pieces):
            if i < len(pieces) - 1 and pieces[i] == a and pieces[i + 1] == b:
                out.append(a + b)
                i += 2
            else:
                out.append(pieces[i])
                i += 1
        updated[tuple(out)] = f
    return updated

def train(corpus, n_merges):
    # Every word starts split into characters. `</w>` marks where it ends.
    vocab = {tuple(w) + ("</w>",): f for w, f in Counter(corpus.split()).items()}
    merges = []
    for _ in range(n_merges):
        counts = pairs(vocab)
        if not counts:
            break
        pair = max(counts.items(), key=lambda kv: kv[1])[0]   # tie: the first one seen wins
        merges.append((pair, counts[pair]))
        vocab = merge(vocab, pair)
    return merges

for i, (pair, n) in enumerate(train(CORPUS, 15), 1):
    print(f"{i:2}. {pair[0]!r} + {pair[1]!r} -> {(pair[0] + pair[1])!r}   (x{n})")
```

On that six-line corpus, the first fifteen merges are these. The number in parentheses on each line
is $n(a,b)$, the count that won that round:

```text
 1. 'a' + '</w>' -> 'a</w>'   (x18)
 2. 'p' + 'r' -> 'pr'   (x16)
 3. 'e' + 'n' -> 'en'   (x13)
 4. 'o' + '</w>' -> 'o</w>'   (x10)
 5. 'pr' + 'o' -> 'pro'   (x10)
 6. 'n' + 'i' -> 'ni'   (x9)
 7. 'ni' + 'ñ' -> 'niñ'   (x9)
 8. 'l' + '</w>' -> 'l</w>'   (x8)
 9. 'n' + '</w>' -> 'n</w>'   (x8)
10. 'e' + '</w>' -> 'e</w>'   (x8)
11. 'pro' + 'g' -> 'prog'   (x8)
12. 'prog' + 'r' -> 'progr'   (x8)
13. 'progr' + 'a' -> 'progra'   (x8)
14. 'progra' + 'm' -> 'program'   (x8)
15. 'program' + 'a' -> 'programa'   (x8)
```

I generated the corpus with AI so the merges would show, and it shows: six lines of Spanish with a
lot of children and a lot of programming. A real corpus has billions of words, and its first merges
are every bit as dull as these.

That list is worth reading slowly, because it contains three things that aren't obvious.

The first merge is an `a` glued to a word ending. The algorithm doesn't know that in Spanish a final
`a` marks the feminine in "niña" and "profesora", nor that the infinitive ends in `r`, nor what a
morpheme is. It has simply counted. It turns out that in Spanish, counting and knowing grammar look
very much alike, because endings are precisely what repeats most, which is why BPE's pieces end up
resembling morphemes without anyone asking them to.

The second is the staircase from 11 to 15. The piece `pro` eats one letter per round until it
reaches `programa`, and every step is one more vocabulary entry. BPE doesn't recognise the root
"program": it builds it, letter by letter, because that's what repeats most in a corpus where
everybody programs. With a frequent root, that's a bargain, because afterwards "programación",
"programando" and "programa" share their first piece. With a rare one, it's pure waste.

The third is that the resemblance to morphology is a statistical coincidence, and it breaks the
moment the statistics stop cooperating. GPT-4's tokeniser splits "dámelo" into `d`, `ám`, `elo`,
which is nobody's segmentation: the morphological one would be "dá me lo", the imperative and its two
clitics. BPE's pieces look like morphemes when the morphemes are frequent, and like nothing
recognisable when they aren't.

### Encoding is replaying the list

Once the list is learned, tokenising a new word means applying the merges in the order they were
learned: no more than those, and in no other order.

```python
def encode(word, merges):
    """Replay the list, in order. This is everything a BPE tokeniser does."""
    vocab = {tuple(word) + ("</w>",): 1}
    for pair, _ in merges:
        vocab = merge(vocab, pair)
    return next(iter(vocab))
```

And the result, which is what the model ends up seeing:

```text
>>> encode("programación", merges)
('programa', 'c', 'i', 'ó', 'n</w>')
```

Five pieces, and only the first means anything on its own. Behind it are the loose letters of the
ending, unmerged, because fifteen merges only stretch so far: the list ran out of budget before it
got to them. Keep training and that changes. Nine merges later, number 24 glues `programació` to
`n</w>` and the whole word becomes **a single token**, down from five to one.

There, in miniature, is the trade-off the rest of the article is about. Every merge you add shortens
the sequences and costs a vocabulary entry, with its two rows of $d$ numbers. Nobody writes a rule
for where to stop: you pick a number of merges, and the algorithm uses it to decide which words of
the language fit whole and which reach the model in pieces.

The fact that order matters has two consequences worth looking at together. The good one is
**determinism**, the other condition a tokeniser has to meet: the same word always produces the same
cut, because the list never changes and is always walked the same way. If the cut changed from one
day to the next, what the model learned would stop matching what it receives. The awkward one is that
the list is the tokeniser right down to its silliest details. In my code, when two pairs tie on
frequency, the one seen first wins, and that line is as much a part of the tokeniser as the corpus
is: two implementations that break ties differently, trained on the same text, produce different
vocabularies and cut differently forever.

### The word the corpus didn't contain

That leaves the question the word tokeniser couldn't answer: what to do when a word arrives that the
corpus didn't contain. Take "El niño programa una criptomoneda", an ordinary sentence, and cut it
with a word tokeniser trained on the corpus above: three of its five words aren't in the vocabulary.
"criptomoneda" is the worst of the three, because it isn't an oddity or a proper noun but a perfectly
normal word that this corpus happened not to contain, and a word tokeniser has absolutely nothing to
offer for it.

Here is the same list of fifteen merges applied to that word, next to two words the corpus did
contain:

```text
niño           -> niñ o</w>
enseñar        -> en s e ñ a r </w>
criptomoneda   -> c r i p t o m o n e d a</w>
```

There's no `UNK` anywhere. "criptomoneda" didn't appear in the corpus, none of its merges were
learned, and even so the tokeniser writes it out whole, letter by letter, because single letters are
always in the vocabulary. That's the property that let BPE take over the field: the vocabulary is
closed and finite, and yet every string has a representation.

And now the price, which in 2016 didn't look serious and today is half the problem. "criptomoneda"
has cost twelve tokens instead of two or three. The word hasn't been lost, it's been made expensive,
and on top of that it reaches the model chopped into pieces that mean nothing separately. The
question is no longer whether a word is in $V$. It's how much the tokeniser charges you to write it,
and that question has a different answer for every language.

## Three ways of choosing the pieces

BPE is the one everybody names, but it isn't the only one in use. There are three algorithms in
production, and the difference between them fits in one sentence: what changes is the criterion by
which a piece is admitted to the vocabulary. One of the three also changes the direction it
approaches the problem from.

### WordPiece: merge what is sought, not what is plentiful

WordPiece runs the same greedy loop as BPE, starting from characters and merging one pair per round,
and changes only the score used to pick the pair. Where BPE keeps the highest $n(a,b)$, WordPiece
divides that same count by how often each piece appears on its own:

$$
\text{score}(a,b) \;=\; \frac{n(a,b)}{n(a)\,n(b)},
$$

where $n(a)$ is the number of times the piece $a$ appears in the corpus, alone or accompanied, and
likewise for $n(b)$.

Taking logarithms, the score is $\log n(a,b) - \log n(a) - \log n(b)$, which is the pair's pointwise
mutual information up to a constant that is the same for every pair and therefore doesn't change
which one wins. And that quantity measures something different: not how many times the two pieces
appear together, but how many times more than you'd expect if each went its own way.

The difference shows in the trace from the previous section. The third merge glued `e` to `n` because
the pair came up thirteen times, but `e` and `n` are two of the most frequent letters in Spanish, so
the fact that they coincide a lot says nothing about them. WordPiece divides by precisely that, and
prefers pairs whose pieces, taken separately, are rarer than their union suggests. The vocabulary
that comes out has less filler and more pieces that correspond to something.

It comes from a [2012 paper on voice search in Japanese and Korean](https://research.google/pubs/japanese-and-korean-voice-search/),
two languages where it isn't even clear where a word ends, and it became famous six years later as
[BERT](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)'s tokeniser. It marks continuations with `##`: in
`c ##ript ##omo ##ned ##a`, the `##` says that the piece doesn't start a word, and removing it
reconstructs the text.

There's a second difference that almost nobody mentions. WordPiece **keeps no merge list**. BPE
encodes by replaying its own list in order; WordPiece, which no longer has one, encodes another way:
at each position, it takes the longest piece in the vocabulary that fits there and carries on from
where that piece ends. Two nearly identical training algorithms produce two different encoders.

### Unigram: start at the top and prune

The third one reverses the whole direction. Instead of growing from the alphabet, it starts with an
enormous vocabulary of candidates (every reasonably frequent substring of the corpus) and removes the
ones that are least needed, until it comes down to the fixed size.

Deciding which are least needed forces you to measure that, and measuring it forces you to have a
model. That's the underlying difference from the other two. A segmentation
$\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_M)$ of a sentence has probability

$$
P(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{M} p(x_i),
$$

that is, each piece has its own probability, and the pieces are assumed not to depend on one another:
hence *unigram*. With that, the best segmentation of a sentence is the one with the highest
$P(\mathbf{x})$.

Finding it is cheaper than it looks. A string of $n$ characters can be split into contiguous pieces
in $2^{n-1}$ ways, so enumerating them is out of the question for any real sentence, but nothing
needs enumerating. Taking logarithms, $\log P(\mathbf{x})$ is a sum of terms that don't affect one
another, so the best way of cutting up to position $j$ depends only on the best way of reaching each
earlier position $i$, plus the score of the piece running from $i$ to $j$. You walk the sentence left
to right keeping one number per position, the best accumulated score up to that point, and at the end
you retrace the path backwards. That walk has a name: it's the **Viterbi algorithm**, the same one
used to decode hidden Markov models, and it brings the cost down from exponential to $O(n \cdot L)$,
where $L$ is the length of the longest piece in the vocabulary. It's exactly the code a few
paragraphs below, where $L$ is called `maxlen`.

Training, for its part, now has something to optimise: the likelihood of the corpus, summing over all
possible segmentations $S(s)$ of each sentence $s$,

$$
\mathcal{L} \;=\; \sum_{s} \log \!\!\sum_{\mathbf{x} \in S(s)} \prod_{i} p(x_i).
$$

The algorithm alternates two steps. The first adjusts the $p(x)$, and has a chicken-and-egg problem:
to know how much each piece is used, you'd need to know where each sentence is cut, and to cut the
sentences you need the $p(x)$. The way out is **expectation-maximisation** (EM), which goes back and
forth between the two halves: with the $p(x)$ from the previous round, it computes how many times
each piece is expected to appear, counting every possible segmentation weighted by its probability,
and sets the new $p(x)$ proportional to those expected counts. The second step computes, piece by
piece, how far $\mathcal{L}$ would fall if that piece didn't exist, and throws out the ones that cost
least. Then another round, down to the fixed size.

It comes [from a 2018 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.10959) whose main subject is, in fact,
something else. Because the model gives probabilities, there's no single good segmentation but a
ranking of them, and you can **sample**: the same sentence goes in cut one way today and another way
tomorrow, which works as data augmentation and makes the model less brittle in the face of an odd
cut. Neither BPE nor WordPiece can do that, because for them only one answer exists.

### The file gives it away

None of this has to be taken on faith. Any open model's tokeniser is a file you can download and
open, and what's inside gives the algorithm away:

| algorithm | what the file carries | example |
|---|---|---|
| BPE | vocabulary **and an ordered merge list** | GPT-2: 50,257 entries, 50,000 merges |
| WordPiece | a bare vocabulary, no merges, with the `##` prefix declared | BERT: 30,522 entries |
| Unigram | a vocabulary where **each piece carries its $\log p(x)$** | XLM-R: 250,002 scored entries |

The arithmetic in the first row adds up and is worth a look: $256 + 50\,000 + 1 = 50\,257$. That's
two hundred and fifty-six starting pieces, fifty thousand learned merges and one special end-of-text
token. Where those 256 come from is exactly the subject of the next section.

The scores in the third row are literal: in XLM-R's vocabulary, `▁de` is worth $-5.31$, `ción`
$-9.59$ and `▁niño` $-13.22$, where `▁` marks a piece that starts a word. With those numbers and
fifteen lines of Python, you can reproduce the entire tokeniser.

```python
def segment(text, pieces, maxlen=16):
    # Viterbi: of all the ways of splitting the text into vocabulary pieces,
    # the one maximising the sum of log p(x). Cost O(n * maxlen), not exponential.
    s = "▁" + text.replace(" ", "▁")
    n = len(s)
    best = [-math.inf] * (n + 1)      # best log-probability up to position j
    back = [0] * (n + 1)              # where that optimum came from, to rebuild it
    best[0] = 0.0
    for j in range(1, n + 1):
        for i in range(max(0, j - maxlen), j):
            p = pieces.get(s[i:j])    # None if that substring is not a piece
            if p is not None and best[i] + p > best[j]:
                best[j], back[j] = best[i] + p, i
    out, j = [], n
    while j > 0:
        out.append(s[back[j]:j])
        j = back[j]
    return out[::-1]
```

Load XLM-R's published vocabulary into `pieces` and this function returns exactly what the official
tokeniser returns, token for token. It doesn't resemble the algorithm: it is the algorithm.

### The three on the same words

Here are the three real tokenisers cutting the same six Spanish words:

| word | BPE (GPT-4o) | WordPiece (mBERT) | Unigram (XLM-R) |
|---|---|---|---|
| niño | `ni` `ño` | `niño` | `▁niño` |
| jardín | `j` `ard` `ín` | `jardín` | `▁jardí` `n` |
| enseñar | `ense` `ñar` | `ens` `##eña` `##r` | `▁enseñar` |
| programación | `program` `ación` | `programación` | `▁programación` |
| dámelo | `d` `ám` `elo` | `dá` `##mel` `##o` | `▁dám` `elo` |
| criptomoneda | `cript` `omon` `eda` | `c` `##ript` `##omo` `##ned` `##a` | `▁cripto` `mone` `da` |

The comparison isn't clean, and it's worth saying so: the three vocabularies are of very different
sizes (200,019, 119,547 and 250,002) and were trained on different corpora, so what you see isn't the
algorithm alone. Even so, two things stand out without effort.

No two of them agree on any of the words they break into pieces. And none of the three finds the
segmentation a speaker would give for "dámelo", which is "dá me lo", the imperative with its two
clitics: out come `d ám elo`, `dá ##mel ##o` and `▁dám elo`. Three algorithms, three cuts, and all
three equally far from morphology. Pieces look like morphemes when the morphemes are frequent, and
like nothing recognisable when they aren't; an earlier section already flagged this with a single
tokeniser, and here you can see it wasn't that tokeniser's fault.

### SentencePiece is not a fourth algorithm

This is the most repeated confusion in the field, and it comes from the name appearing on the spec
sheet of half a dozen models. [SentencePiece](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.06226) is an
**implementation**: a library that trains and applies BPE or Unigram, not a third way of choosing
pieces. Saying a model "uses SentencePiece" tells you as little as saying a program "uses gcc".

What it did contribute, and what made it take over, is of a different order. Earlier tokenisers
assumed the text arrived already split into words by a separate program, with its per-language rules,
the same ones from the first section. SentencePiece skips that step: it treats the sentence as a
stream of characters, spaces included, and turns the space into one more symbol, the `▁` in the table
above. With that, there are no per-language rules to maintain, Japanese and Spanish come in through
the same door, and reconstruction is exact again with no conventions to remember: glue the pieces
together, swap `▁` back for a space and you have the text you started with.

Even so, the three algorithms share an assumption we haven't touched yet. All three build their
pieces out of characters, and which characters exist is decided by looking at the corpus. If a text
turns up tomorrow containing one the corpus didn't have (an emoji, an ideogram, a letter from an
alphabet nobody considered), the vocabulary comes up short again. It's the same hole as before, one
floor down.


## The byte floor

Unicode has on the order of 150,000 characters assigned, spread across more than 150 writing systems,
and more are added every year. No corpus contains them all. So the alphabet any of the three
algorithms starts from, however large the corpus, is always an incomplete list, and the problem from
the first section reappears one floor down: along comes an ideogram, an emoji invented last year or a
letter from an alphabet nobody looked at, and the tokeniser has nothing to write it with.

The solution that won out, and that arrived [with GPT-2 in 2019](https://cdn.openai.com/better-language-models/language_models_are_unsupervised_multitask_learners.pdf),
is uncomfortably simple: stop building the pieces out of characters and build them out of **bytes**.
Any text, in any script, is stored on disk as a sequence of UTF-8 bytes, and a byte can take only 256
values. If the starting alphabet is those 256 values, there is no text that can't be written with the
vocabulary. None. Ever.

That's where the arithmetic from the previous section fits: $256 + 50\,000 + 1 = 50\,257$. The 256
bytes, the 50,000 merges learned on top of them and one end-of-text token. GPT-2's vocabulary **has
no unknown token**, and that's no oversight: it doesn't need one, because nothing can be left out.
The `UNK` that had forced people to invent patch-up dictionaries disappears from the problem by
construction.

### What going down to the byte costs

A floor made of bytes has an odd consequence: a token can be a piece of a character. Not an
incomplete letter in some figurative sense, but a sequence of bytes that on its own means nothing.

```text
'🙂'   its UTF-8 bytes are  b'\xf0\x9f\x99\x82'
  gpt2          2 tokens:  [b'\xf0\x9f', b'\x99\x82']
  cl100k_base   2 tokens:  [b'\xf0\x9f', b'\x99\x82']
  o200k_base    1 token:   [b'\xf0\x9f\x99\x82']

'漢字'  its UTF-8 bytes are  b'\xe6\xbc\xa2\xe5\xad\x97'
  gpt2          5 tokens:  [b'\xe6', b'\xbc', b'\xa2', b'\xe5\xad', b'\x97']
  cl100k_base   3 tokens:  [b'\xe6\xbc', b'\xa2', b'\xe5\xad\x97']
  o200k_base    2 tokens:  [b'\xe6\xbc\xa2', b'\xe5\xad\x97']
```

With GPT-2, the smiley costs two tokens, and neither of them is a character: `b'\xf0\x9f'` is the
first half of an emoji, and on its own it can't even be printed. The two ideograms cost five tokens,
three of them loose bytes. The model doesn't see two characters; it sees five pieces whose
relationship to what was written exists only if it learns to reconstruct it.

That's the currency the guarantee is paid in. Nothing falls outside the vocabulary, but whatever is
far from the training corpus comes in chopped into meaningless fragments. Compare that with `ñ`,
which in all three tokenisers is a whole token despite taking two bytes: Spanish is represented well
enough in the corpus for that merge to have been learned. The difference between a cheap character
and an expensive one isn't Unicode; it's how often the character turned up in the corpus.

### The regex almost nobody looks at

Before BPE touches the text, there's a step almost nobody talks about, and it decides more than it
seems. The text is first split with a regular expression, and merges can never cross those cuts. The
regex, therefore, speeds nothing up: it delimits what the algorithm is allowed to learn.

The part governing numbers changed between generations, and you can see the change at a glance:

| tokeniser | digits in the regex | `1234567` | `2026` |
|---|---|---|---|
| gpt2 | `' ?\p{N}++'`, runs of any length | `123` `45` `67` | `20` `26` |
| cl100k, o200k | `'\p{N}{1,3}'`, groups of three at most | `123` `456` `7` | `202` `6` |

In GPT-2, the regex handed over `1234567` whole and BPE merged inside it according to what it had
seen, so the cuts landed wherever they happened to land. From cl100k on, the cut comes first and is
fixed: groups of three, **left to right**. Look at what that does to the year I'm writing this in.
`2026` isn't split into `20` and `26`, nor left whole: out come `202` and `6`. The same digit ends up
in a different token depending on the length of the number around it, and that decision, made in a
regular expression, is where half of language models' arithmetic problems come from.

### Two roads to the same floor

Not everyone went down to the byte through GPT-2's door. SentencePiece, which trains on characters,
added an option called `byte_fallback`: the vocabulary stays a vocabulary of characters, and only
when a character turns up that isn't in it does the tokeniser fall back on writing it out with its
bytes. The guarantee is the same; the route isn't.

Llama's two tokenisers show both routes, one per version. Llama 2's, with 32,000 pieces, declares
`byte_fallback: true`: it's a character BPE with a safety net. Llama 3's, with 128,256, declares it
`false`, not because the net was removed but because it's no longer needed: they switched to byte
BPE, where the floor is the alphabet itself. In two versions of the same family, you can see the
whole migration.

### The vocabulary race

With the floor settled, the question from earlier remains: how many pieces? And here the numbers
moved a great deal in a few years, from Llama 2's 32,000 to GPT-4o's 200,019.

What a bigger vocabulary buys is shorter sequences. What it costs is parameters: the model stores the
embedding matrix and the output one, $2\lvert V \rvert d$ numbers in total, and the final layer does
on the order of $\lvert V \rvert d$ operations **for every token it generates**. With
$\lvert V \rvert = 128\,256$ and $d = 4096$, those two matrices come to about 1,050 million
parameters, which in an eight-billion-parameter model is more than an eighth of the total, spent
getting in and out of the vocabulary. Raising $\lvert V \rvert$ divides the number of steps and
multiplies the cost of each one, so there's an optimum, and it depends on the size of the model;
[recent work is devoted to locating exactly that](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.13623).

While we're looking at numbers, the interesting question is what that size buys **for Spanish**.
Here are seven real tokenisers on the same text, 400,000 characters of Spanish literature, 72,786
words:

| tokeniser | algorithm | $\lvert V \rvert$ | tokens per word |
|---|---|---|---|
| Llama 2 (2023) | BPE + `byte_fallback` | 32,000 | 1.750 |
| GPT-2 (2019) | byte BPE | 50,257 | 2.029 |
| GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 (2022) | byte BPE | 100,277 | 1.653 |
| multilingual BERT (2018) | WordPiece | 119,547 | 1.542 |
| Llama 3 (2024) | byte BPE | 128,256 | 1.650 |
| GPT-4o (2024) | byte BPE | 200,019 | 1.460 |
| XLM-R (2019) | Unigram | 250,002 | 1.519 |

Within one family, the trend is clean: OpenAI's three tokenisers bring Spanish down from 2.03 to 1.65
and then to 1.46 tokens per word as the vocabulary doubles, with clear diminishing returns (the first
doubling takes off 18%, the second 12%).

Across families, the table says something different and more useful: size doesn't rule. Llama 2,
with 32,000 pieces, cuts Spanish better than GPT-2 with 50,257. A multilingual BERT from 2018 cuts it
better than a Llama 3 from 2024, at almost the same vocabulary size. And XLM-R, with 250,002 pieces,
the largest vocabulary in the table, loses to GPT-4o, which has fifty thousand fewer.

What decides it isn't how many pieces there are but how many of them were spent on your language.
XLM-R spreads a quarter of a million pieces across a hundred languages; GPT-4o concentrates two
hundred thousand on rather fewer. A vocabulary is a budget, and dividing it up is a product decision
nobody consults you about and that you end up paying for by the token.


## What the choice costs you

Everything above is machinery. This section is the bill, and the point of it is that none of what
follows is a mystery: each item can be traced back to a concrete decision you've watched being made
in the earlier sections.

### The tax on writing in Spanish

Take the *Quijote* in Spanish and [Ormsby's English translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/996),
both on Project Gutenberg, and run OpenAI's three tokenisers over each. The Spanish original takes
2,105,831 characters; the English translation, 2,292,068. The Spanish text is **shorter**.

| tokeniser | tokens in Spanish | tokens in English | Spanish / English | characters per token, es / en |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| gpt2 (2019) | 778,102 | 565,416 | 1.38 | 2.71 / 4.05 |
| cl100k (2022) | 632,915 | 541,068 | 1.17 | 3.33 / 4.24 |
| o200k (2024) | 558,227 | 536,900 | 1.04 | 3.77 / 4.27 |

With GPT-2, the shorter book costs 38% more. The right-hand column says why: the tokeniser gets 4.05
characters out of every token in English and 2.71 in Spanish, so the same reading effort yields a
third less. And that difference isn't merely cosmetic, because tokens are the unit everything else is
counted in: what fits in the context window, how long a reply takes and what the API bill comes to.
Writing in Spanish was, quite literally, more expensive.

The last row is the good news this article opened with: with GPT-4o's tokeniser the ratio drops to
1.04, near parity. Not because Spanish changed, but because doubling the vocabulary left room to put
whole Spanish words inside it. But don't generalise from here to every language: Spanish shares an
alphabet with English and turns up in training corpora by the bucketload. For languages that also
change script, the difference [can still be several-fold](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15425), and the
byte-floor section explains why: when one character costs three tokens of loose bytes, no vocabulary
fixes that without devoting space to it.

### Why it can't count the r's in a word

This is the example that circulates everywhere, and it's almost always explained wrongly. The
question is how many r's there are in "strawberry", and the model gets it wrong. The cause isn't that
it can't count:

```text
strawberry    ->  str · aw · berry
ferrocarril   ->  fer · roc · arr · il
```

The model never sees the word. It sees three pieces, and not one of the three is a letter. To answer,
it would have to know by heart how each piece is spelled, because the information it needs was
destroyed before it ever arrived, at the moment of the cut. In Spanish the same thing happens, only
worse: "ferrocarril" comes in four pieces with four r's scattered among them, and one of the pieces,
`arr`, carries two inside it. Counting over that is counting over something that isn't there.

It's the condition from the first section seen from the other side. The tokeniser can preserve the
whole text and still destroy its structure, because preserving something and making it accessible are
not the same thing.

### Arithmetic, cut in the wrong places

The regex we saw earlier splits digits into groups of three from left to right. Look at what that
does to numbers that closely resemble each other:

```text
1000    ->  100 · 0
10000   ->  100 · 00
100000  ->  100 · 000
1999    ->  199 · 9
19999   ->  199 · 99
```

In `1000`, the first digit shares a token with the second and third, and the last one goes alone. In
`10000`, that same leading `100` is now followed by `00`. The tokens don't correspond to units, tens
and hundreds, and they fail to correspond in different ways depending on the length of the number. A
model doing addition has to work over pieces whose position within the number changes from one case
to the next. Why arithmetic on long numbers is fragile stops being a mystery the moment you see the
cuts: the model isn't adding digits, it's predicting fragments.

### The space at the end of the prompt

This one bites anyone programming against an API, and it's completely invisible:

```text
'El niño juega en el'    ->  El · ni · ño · jue · ga · en · el
'El niño juega en el '   ->  El · ni · ño · jue · ga · en · el · ␣
```

One extra space at the end of the prompt adds a token that stands on its own. And here's the problem:
in all the text the model was trained on, the space is **glued to the following word** (` jardín`,
with its leading space, is one token). A loose space at the end is a situation the model has hardly
ever seen, so instead of continuing with ` jardín`, it has to continue with something that starts
without a space, which is a completely different probability distribution. One extra keystroke, and
quality drops. Some libraries fix this internally with what's called *token healing*: they remove the
last token of the prompt and merge it again with whatever the model generates. Almost none does it by
default.

### Indentation, and why code got cheaper

The same mechanism explains a change that people did notice at the time. Here is one indented line of
Python, under GPT-2 and under cl100k:

```text
'    return x'      gpt2   5 tokens:  ␣ · ␣ · ␣ · ' return' · ' x'
                    cl100k 3 tokens:  '   ' · ' return' · ' x'
'        return x'  gpt2   9 tokens
                    cl100k 3 tokens
```

GPT-2 spent one token per space, so a function with eight levels of nothing ate the context window in
indentation. cl100k put pieces made of several consecutive spaces into the vocabulary, and the same
code came to cost half as much or less. Nobody changed the model; they changed what was on the list.

### The ghost tokens

The strangest of the lot, and the one that best shows the tokeniser is a separate component. GPT-2's
vocabulary contains entries like ` SolidGoldMagikarp`, ` petertodd`, ` RandomRedditor` and
` externalToEVAOnly`, each **a single token**. They're Reddit usernames and code fragments that
appeared so relentlessly in the material the tokeniser was trained on that they earned their own
merge.

The catch is that this material isn't the same as the material the model was trained on. When those
texts were filtered out of the training corpus, their tokens stayed in the vocabulary, each with an
embedding row initialised at random and never adjusted. Asking GPT-2 to repeat one of them
[produced absurd or evasive replies](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/aPeJE8bSo6rAFoLqg/solidgoldmagikarp-plus-prompt-generation),
because the model was reading a vector that meant nothing. Most of them disappeared from later
vocabularies, though not all: ` davidjl` is still a single token in GPT-4's.

Not one of these six things is a programming error. Each is the consequence of a reasonable
decision: compress the corpus you had, cut numbers in a fixed way, glue spaces onto the following
word, use whatever text was available to train the tokeniser. What they have in common is that none
of those decisions was made with any thought for what you'd later do with the model.


## What comes after the tokeniser

After seven sections of problems caused by choosing a unit, the question practically asks itself: why
not take the tokeniser out of the way and let the model read bytes?

It has been tried, and it works. [ByT5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.13626) is a 2021 model that reads
raw bytes and holds its own against its tokenised counterpart, besides being far more robust to typos,
which is exactly what you'd expect from something that sees the letters.
[CANINE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06874), from the same year, does something similar by compressing
the sequence internally. Both run into the wall from the third section: without long units the
sequence is multiplied by six and the cost by thirty, and no amount of elegance pays for that.

What has changed in recent years is the shape of the question. Instead of choosing between a large
unit and a small one, the idea is not to fix the unit at all: let the model read bytes and decide on
the fly where to group them. [MEGABYTE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07185) did it with fixed-size
patches. The [Byte Latent Transformer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09871), from late 2024, does it with
variable-size patches, and its criterion is the best idea in the whole business: a small model
estimates the entropy of the next byte, and a new patch is opened wherever that entropy spikes,

$$
H(x_t) \;=\; -\!\!\sum_{b \in \text{bytes}} p(b \mid x_{<t}) \log p(b \mid x_{<t}) \;>\; \theta .
$$

Read it slowly, because it inverts the entire premise of the article. BPE spends pieces where the
corpus repeated things, years ago and once and for all. This approach spends compute where the text
in front of you is hard to predict, right now. The end of "programación" is predictable and doesn't
deserve a cut; the proper noun you've never seen deserves several. It's a tokeniser that adapts to
the text instead of to a frozen corpus.

At the other extreme, there are people pushing the opposite way, and doing well out of it:
[SuperBPE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13423) keeps the vocabulary fixed but lets pieces cross spaces,
so that whole expressions become a single token. And [H-Net](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.07955) learns
the chunking end to end, inside the network, with no separate module at all.

As of today, none of this is the norm. The models you reach when you open an API have a byte BPE
inside them, with a vocabulary of between one and two hundred thousand pieces, trained once and
frozen. What would have to happen for that to change is measurable: the overhead of reading longer
sequences would have to fall below what the fixed vocabulary costs today, counting in that cost the
billion parameters of the two matrices, the per-language tax and the list of defects from the
previous section. It's closer than it was three years ago. I'm writing this in 2026, and it's the
part of the article that will age worst.

## What you take away

If you take away one thing, let it be this: **the tokeniser is the only part of the system that's
decided before anything is learned and never revisited.** Everything else in a language model is
adjusted from the data. The cut is not. It's fixed at the start, with whatever corpus existed then,
and everything it discards or scrambles stays discarded and scrambled forever.

And if you take away three more, let them be practical.

**Measure your own text before choosing a model.** The table in the byte-floor section is built from
seventeenth-century literature; your text looks nothing like that. It takes six lines:

```python
import tiktoken

enc = tiktoken.get_encoding("o200k_base")     # or "cl100k_base", to compare
text = open("what_you_write.txt", encoding="utf8").read()
tokens, words = len(enc.encode(text)), len(text.split())
print(f"{tokens / words:.2f} tokens per word")
```

Between 1.46 and 2.03 tokens per word, the range this article gets for the same Spanish text,
there's a 40% difference in what fits in the context, in how long it takes and in what it costs.
It's worth knowing where in that range you sit before signing anything.

**Treat the prompt as what it is: a sequence of tokens.** Don't leave a loose space at the end. Don't
expect reliable arithmetic on long numbers. Don't ask the model to count letters, because it doesn't
have them in front of it. None of those three is a flaw that a better version of the model will fix:
they're consequences of the cut, and they only go away by changing the cut.

**And if you ever train something of your own, start there.** A tokeniser trained on your own domain,
however small, cuts better than a general-purpose one with ten times as many pieces, for the same
reason a multilingual BERT from 2018 cuts Spanish better than a Llama 3 from 2024: what decides it
isn't how many pieces there are but how many were spent on what you write.

Back to the beginning. "El niño juega en el jardín." It cost twelve tokens in 2019 and costs seven
now, the same as in English. Nobody fixed Spanish. What changed was who got onto a list.
