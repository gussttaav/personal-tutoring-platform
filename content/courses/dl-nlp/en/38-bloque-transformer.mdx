summary: 'The attention layer is complete, and the paper puts six of them one on top of another. It is two things, and both fit in one line. The sum (the sublayer returns what it computed plus what came into it) opens a path from the output to the input that crosses no matrix, and along the way forces d_model to be a single number across the whole model, which is what the multi-head output projection was for.       def layer_norm(X, gamma, beta, eps=1e-5):
          mu = X.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)          # (T, 1): one mean per row
          s = X.std(axis=1, keepdims=True)            # (T, 1): one deviation per row
          return gamma * (X - mu) / np.sqrt(s ** 2 + eps) + beta

Attention is now missing nothing: it has its three projections, its divisor, its heads and, since
the previous lesson on positional encoding, rows that know where they are. What doesn't exist yet is
a layer that can be put *on top of another*. And the paper puts six, with what leaves each one
serving as the input to the next.

Repeating an operation that already works six times sounds like doing nothing, and it is exactly
where Block 3 came to grief. There, what was repeated was a step of the recurrence, and composing a
function with itself multiplies derivatives: a product of many similar factors goes to zero or blows
up, and what came in at the bottom doesn't reach the top. A Transformer's depth is set not by the
sentence but by the layers, and there are six however long the text grows. But six products are
still six products. This lesson is what has to be put around a sublayer for that calculation to come
out differently, and it is two things.

Go back to the diagram from <Leccion slug="adios-recurrencia">the lesson that said goodbye to
recurrence</Leccion> and look at the boxes it left unexplained. Five of the fifteen are called
<W>Add and layer norm</W>, and from each one a line runs down the outside of its column and comes
back in below the sublayer that box closes. Click one: that line is half of what the box adds.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
A sublayer doesn't replace what it receives: it adds to it, and that untouched term is the path by
which what is at the bottom reaches the top, however many layers there are in between.
</Callout>

## No sublayer replaces what it receives

The residual connection is the line in the drawing written in symbols, and there is nothing more to
it than this:

$$
\mathbf{X} + \text{Sublayer}(\mathbf{X}),
$$

where $\text{Sublayer}$ is a slot (like Block 2's $\varphi$ or the previous block's $a$) filled by
multi-head attention in the first sublayer and by the position-wise perceptron in the second. The
interesting part is in its derivative. Stack the $T$ rows of $\mathbf{X}$ one below another, as in
<Leccion slug="por-que-falla-el-mlp">the lesson on why the MLP (multilayer perceptron) fails on
sequences</Leccion>, and call that column $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \cdot d_{\text{model}}}$.
Differentiating a sum is differentiating each term:

$$
\frac{\partial\left(\mathbf{x} + \text{Sublayer}(\mathbf{x})\right)}{\partial\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{I} + \frac{\partial\,\text{Sublayer}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{x}}.
$$

That is everything. The sublayer's Jacobian depends on its parameters and can be worth anything,
even zero; the other term is the identity and depends on nothing. And since the chain rule from
<Leccion slug="regla-de-la-cadena">its lesson</Leccion> turns a composition into a product of
Jacobians, stacking $N$ blocks multiplies $N$ brackets like this one, each with its identity inside.

<Details summary="Two blocks, multiplied">

With two blocks, and subscripts to tell each one's sublayer apart, the product opens up like this:

$$
\left(\mathbf{I} + \frac{\partial\,\text{Sublayer}_2}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\right)\left(\mathbf{I} + \frac{\partial\,\text{Sublayer}_1}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\right) = \mathbf{I} + \frac{\partial\,\text{Sublayer}_1}{\partial\mathbf{x}} + \frac{\partial\,\text{Sublayer}_2}{\partial\mathbf{x}} + \frac{\partial\,\text{Sublayer}_2}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\frac{\partial\,\text{Sublayer}_1}{\partial\mathbf{x}}.
$$

Four terms: one that crosses no sublayer, two that cross one and one that crosses both. With $N$
blocks there are $2^{N}$, one for each way of choosing in each bracket between the identity and the
sublayer, and among them there is always exactly one that crosses nothing.

</Details>

In <Leccion slug="gradiente-desvanecido">the lesson on the vanishing gradient</Leccion> there were no
brackets: each factor was a bare product and the only path between step $1$ and step $T$ crossed
them all. The LSTM answered by opening an additive path alongside. The residual connection is that
same idea with depth in the place of time: what gets stacked here is not positions, it is layers.

That settles the previous lesson's debt. Positional encoding is added *once*, at the very bottom,
and no box afterwards recalls where each token was: the term equal to $\mathbf{I}$ is what carries
it to the top.

### One width from the bottom to the top

The sum brings a hidden constraint: $\mathbf{X}$ and $\text{Sublayer}(\mathbf{X})$ are added entry
by entry, so the sublayer is forbidden from returning any shape other than $T \times d_{\text{model}}$.
Neither of the two respects it inside, and both make the return trip: multi-head attention drops to
$d_v$ per head and $\mathbf{W}^O$ brings it back (the job
<Leccion slug="multi-head">the lesson on multi-head attention</Leccion> flagged it for), and the
position-wise perceptron rises to $d_{\text{ff}}$ and comes back down.

That turns $d_{\text{model}}$ into the only number to be chosen for the whole model. In Block 2's
multilayer perceptron each layer had its width $d_l$ and could narrow at will; here, if one layer
changed width, the next would have nothing to add it to. The paper's $512$ are the same in the first
layer and in the sixth, and that rigidity is the price of stacking.

## Each row is normalised with its own coordinates

The sum creates a problem of its own: if every sublayer adds without taking anything away, what
flows grows layer by layer, and <Leccion slug="scaled-dot-product">the lesson on the *scaled
dot-product*</Leccion> has already measured what happens to a softmax when large numbers reach it.
The scale has to go back to where it was after each sum, and that is the layer norm.

Let's fix the row at position $t$, with its $d_{\text{model}}$ coordinates $x_{t1}, \dots,
x_{t\,d_{\text{model}}}$. Its mean and its standard deviation are those of these numbers and of no
others:

$$
\mu_t = \frac{1}{d_{\text{model}}}\sum_{j} x_{tj}, \qquad s_t = \sqrt{\frac{1}{d_{\text{model}}}\sum_{j}\left(x_{tj} - \mu_t\right)^{2}}.
$$

With them the row is centred and scaled, and then two learned vectors come in, $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$
and $\boldsymbol{\beta}$, both of $d_{\text{model}}$ coordinates:

$$
\hat{x}_{tj} = \frac{x_{tj} - \mu_t}{\sqrt{s_t^{2} + \varepsilon}}, \qquad \text{LayerNorm}(\mathbf{x}_t) = \boldsymbol{\gamma} \odot \hat{\mathbf{x}}_t + \boldsymbol{\beta}.
$$

$\varepsilon$ is a small constant ($10^{-5}$ in almost every implementation) that avoids dividing by
zero if a row has all its coordinates equal. The other two seem to undo the normalisation, and in
part they do: leaving every row with mean $0$ and deviation $1$ is a condition nobody asked for, and
$\boldsymbol{\gamma}$ and $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ give that freedom back as parameters. They can't undo
it completely: $\mu_t$ and $s_t$ change from row to row, and these two vectors are the same in all of
them.

### Why the row and not the batch

Averaging across the row is a decision, and the alternative is the opposite one: normalising each
coordinate down the column, which is batch normalisation. On sequences it fails in three places.
What came out at position $t$ would depend on which other sentences had landed in the same batch; it
would depend on $T$ and on what was at the other positions, including the padding positions, which say
nothing; and you would have to store statistics from training to answer a single sentence later.

With the row none of the three appears, because $\mu_t$ and $s_t$ come from row $t$ and nothing
else. One consequence is worth keeping: the <W>Add and layer norm</W> box *doesn't mix positions*, and that is
why the claim of <Leccion slug="adios-recurrencia">the lesson that said goodbye to
recurrence</Leccion> (three of the fifteen boxes look beyond their own row) still stands now that we
know what the other twelve do.

## The sublayer that looks at no other position

The second sublayer is still missing: the one the paper calls FFN (feed-forward network) and this
course calls the position-wise perceptron (the two names are the same box). Inside, it is Block 2's
multilayer perceptron with two layers and a ReLU in between, applied to one row at a time:

$$
\text{FFN}(\mathbf{X}) = \text{ReLU}\left(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_1 + \mathbf{1}_T\mathbf{b}_1^{\top}\right)\mathbf{W}_2 + \mathbf{1}_T\mathbf{b}_2^{\top},
$$

with $\mathbf{W}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{ff}}}$ and $\mathbf{W}_2 \in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{ff}} \times d_{\text{model}}}$, and with the subscript naming which of the two
layers it is. $\mathbf{1}_T$ is Block 2's column of ones, which here repeats the bias once per
position instead of once per example, and the ReLU is the paper's $\max(0, \cdot)$.

The two matrices are *the same for every position*: row $3$ and row $300$ go through the same
parameters, so the sublayer has no way of finding out which one it is transforming. That is what
<W>position-wise</W> means, and what keeps it out of the boxes that mix.

### What attention cannot compute

A box that doesn't look at the other positions needs a reason to be there, and the reason lies in
what attention does *not* do. Let's write $\alpha^{(i)}_{ts}$ for the weight head $i$ gives position $s$
when computing row $t$, and $\mathbf{W}^O_i$ for the $d_v$ rows of $\mathbf{W}^O$ that multiply what
that head returns. Opening up the $\text{Concat}$ of <Leccion slug="multi-head">the lesson on
multi-head attention</Leccion>, row $t$ of $\text{MultiHead}(\mathbf{X})$ is

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{h}\sum_{s=1}^{T}\alpha^{(i)}_{ts}\,\mathbf{x}_s\mathbf{W}^V_i\mathbf{W}^O_i.
$$

The softmax lives entirely inside the $\alpha$, and what the $\alpha$ decide is *where* to read from.
What is done with what was read is another matter: a weighted average of rows, each passed through
two matrices. With the weights already decided, row $t$ is a linear function of the rows of
$\mathbf{X}$. So is the sum of the first section. The layer norm is non-linear, but it has no
parameters with which to learn a combination: it centres the row, divides all of it by one and the
same number, and $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$ and $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ work coordinate by coordinate. None
of its parameters looks at two coordinates at once.

Go back to the four phrases of <Leccion slug="xor-y-capas-ocultas">the lesson on XOR and hidden
layers</Leccion>: <W>it's good</W>, <W>it's bad</W>, <W>not good</W> and <W>not bad</W>. Here
<W>not</W> and <W>good</W> occupy different rows, and bringing to the row of <W>good</W> the news that
there is a <W>not</W> in front is attention's job. But once it has brought it, that row holds the two
coordinates of that lesson's square, and that lesson showed that no single neuron separates its
diagonals: <W>not</W> brings no sign of its own, it flips that of the word after it. Composing
linear functions gives another linear function, so stacking more attention doesn't fix it. The two
facts are together, and attention has no piece made for combining them.

The position-wise perceptron is the hidden layer that square asked for, put in every block and
applied to every row. Its ReLU lets the output depend on *whether* a combination of coordinates is
positive, which is what no linear function does, and its $d_{\text{ff}}$ units are new coordinates,
in which the square stops being an XOR.

Attention gathers; the position-wise perceptron computes.

The concession: I have argued with the weights already decided, and they don't come decided. The
$\alpha$ come from $\mathbf{X}$, so attention as a whole is non-linear in what it receives, and
nothing stops a trained network from making use of that. But that non-linearity is there to choose
where to read from, and using it to combine the coordinates of one row is asking one piece to do
another's job.

That it is the piece that computes shows in how the parameters are split, which is not the way the
drawing suggests. With $d_{\text{model}} = 512$ and $d_{\text{ff}} = 2\,048$:

$$
2 d_{\text{model}} d_{\text{ff}} = 2\,097\,152 \quad\text{against}\quad 4 d_{\text{model}}^{2} = 1\,048\,576.
$$

On the left the two matrices of the position-wise perceptron, on the right the four of the
attention added together: the box that looks like plumbing carries twice the parameters of the one
that gives the architecture its name. Neither number mentions $T$.

### The whole block, in two lines

With the three pieces in place, an encoder block is the same line twice:

$$
\text{LayerNorm}\left(\mathbf{X} + \text{Sublayer}(\mathbf{X})\right),
$$

with $\text{MultiHead}$ in the slot the first time and $\text{FFN}$ the second, and with the output
of the first serving as the $\mathbf{X}$ of the second. Biases and normalisation vectors included,
that is $3\,150\,336$ parameters per block, and the paper's encoder stacks $N = 6$: some $18.9$
million, none of them dependent on the length of the text.

And now the concession. The paper normalises *after* adding, and that leaves the layer norm on top
of the identity path: the term equal to $\mathbf{I}$ is inside the bracket, but what leaves the box
has also been through a normalisation, so the clean path of the first section isn't quite so clean.
Almost every implementation today writes
$\mathbf{X} + \text{Sublayer}\left(\text{LayerNorm}(\mathbf{X})\right)$ (normalise what *goes into*
the sublayer and leave the sum untouched), which is what is known as *pre-norm*. Which one trains
better is outside this course; that they are two different operations is not.

## The three pieces in NumPy

The first cell is the layer norm over five positions. Look at three things: the mean and standard
deviation of each row, what $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$ and $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ give back, and what
happens to row $0$ when row $4$ is touched.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
X = rng.normal(loc=3.0, scale=2.0, size=(5, 8))   # 5 positions, d_model = 8


def layer_norm(X, gamma, beta, eps=1e-5):
    mu = X.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)            # one mean per row
    s = X.std(axis=1, keepdims=True)              # one deviation per row
    return gamma * (X - mu) / np.sqrt(s**2 + eps) + beta


gamma, beta = np.ones(8), np.zeros(8)
Y = layer_norm(X, gamma, beta)

print("in   mean per row:", np.round(X.mean(axis=1), 3))
print("     deviation   :", np.round(X.std(axis=1), 3))
print("out  mean per row:", np.round(Y.mean(axis=1), 3))
print("     deviation   :", np.round(Y.std(axis=1), 3))

Z = layer_norm(X, np.full(8, 4.0), np.full(8, -1.0))
print("\\nwith gamma = 4 and beta = -1:", np.round(Z.mean(axis=1), 3), np.round(Z.std(axis=1), 3))

# Touch ONLY row 4 and look at what happens to row 0.
X2 = X.copy()
X2[4] += 10.0
by_columns = lambda M: (M - M.mean(axis=0)) / M.std(axis=0)
print("\\ntouch row 4; how far row 0 moves")
print("  normalising by rows   :",
      round(float(np.abs(layer_norm(X2, gamma, beta)[0] - Y[0]).max()), 12))
print("  normalising by columns:",
      round(float(np.abs(by_columns(X2)[0] - by_columns(X)[0]).max()), 3))
`} />

The five rows go in with means between $0.98$ and $2.89$ and come out with mean $0$ and deviation
$1$; with $\boldsymbol{\gamma} = 4$ and $\boldsymbol{\beta} = -1$ they come out with mean $-1$ and
deviation $4$, which is what those two vectors mean. The last two lines are the previous section
checked: touching row $4$ moves row $0$ by $0.0$ by rows and by $1.889$ by columns.

The second measures what the sum does, and to see it alone I take the layer norm and the attention
out of the block: what is left is $12$ position-wise perceptrons, one on top of another, with and
without the line that goes round them. I have set the scale of the matrices low on purpose, $0.15$:
at the start, a sublayer is a small correction.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
T, d_model, d_ff, N = 8, 16, 32, 12

matrices = [(rng.normal(size=(d_model, d_ff)) * 0.15,
             rng.normal(size=(d_ff, d_model)) * 0.15) for _ in range(N)]


def stack(X, n, with_sum):
    for W1, W2 in matrices[:n]:
        sub = np.maximum(0.0, X @ W1) @ W2        # the position-wise perceptron
        X = X + sub if with_sum else sub
    return X


X = rng.normal(size=(T, d_model))
h = 1e-5
Xh = X.copy()
Xh[0, 0] += h                                     # move ONE coordinate of row 0

print("layers   with the sum   without the sum")
for n in (1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12):
    on = np.linalg.norm((stack(Xh, n, True) - stack(X, n, True)) / h)
    off = np.linalg.norm((stack(Xh, n, False) - stack(X, n, False)) / h)
    print(f"{n:6d}   {on:12.4f}   {off:15.3e}")
`} />

Each number is a derivative measured by a numerical probe: how far the whole output moves when a
single coordinate of the input is pushed. Without the sum it falls from $0.109$ with one layer to
$5.182 \times 10^{-7}$ with twelve, because it is a product of twelve small factors: seven orders
of magnitude, and with twelve layers, not twelve thousand. With the sum it stays between $1.00$ and
$1.80$ the whole way: the term that crosses no matrix is worth $1$, and the rest only correct it.

The third puts the position-wise perceptron to the test with the XOR phrases, lengthened with filler
words between the <W>not</W> and the evaluative word. I have set the attention head by hand: the last
position reads all of them equally and copies, into a free coordinate, the one that marks whether a
word is <W>not</W>. Then I try to read the sentiment off that last row with a single neuron, fitted
with `np.linalg.lstsq` (the weights with the least mean squared error, computed in one go), first on
the row as it is and then on the row passed through the perceptron's first layer and its ReLU. As in the
previous cell, I take the layer norm out to see the piece alone, and I draw $\mathbf{W}_1$ and
$\mathbf{b}_1$ at random: only the readout is fitted.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
d_model, d_ff = 8, 32

# Coordinate 0: "this word is not". Coordinate 1: +1 if it is good, -1 if it is bad.
# Coordinate 2: free, the attention writes it. The rest: filler (it's, really, today...).
def phrase():
    negated, good = rng.random() < 0.5, rng.random() < 0.5
    filler = rng.normal(size=(rng.integers(1, 4), d_model)) * 0.5
    filler[:, :3] = 0.0
    not_row = np.eye(d_model)[[0]] if negated else np.zeros((0, d_model))
    evaluative = np.zeros((1, d_model))
    evaluative[0, 1] = 1.0 if good else -1.0
    return np.vstack([not_row, filler, evaluative]), negated, good

# The head: the last position reads all of them equally and copies their coordinate 0 into 2.
W_VO = np.zeros((d_model, d_model))
W_VO[0, 2] = 1.0

def last_row(X):
    alpha = np.full(len(X), 1 / len(X))         # the weights of the last row
    return X[-1] + alpha @ X @ W_VO             # the sum: x_t + MultiHead(X)_t

data = [phrase() for _ in range(800)]
H = np.array([last_row(X) for X, _, _ in data])
neg = np.array([n for _, n, _ in data])
pos = np.array([g for _, _, g in data])
y = (pos != neg).astype(float)                  # 1 if the phrase speaks well

W1, b1 = rng.normal(size=(d_model, d_ff)), rng.normal(size=d_ff)
readouts = {"the row as it is": H,
            "after the ReLU": np.maximum(0.0, H @ W1 + b1)}

kinds = [(False, True), (False, False), (True, True), (True, False)]
labels = ["it's good", "it's bad", "not good", "not bad"]
widths = (11, 10, 10, 9)
print(" " * 18 + "".join(f"{s:>{w}s}" for s, w in zip(labels, widths)))
for name, F in readouts.items():
    F = np.hstack([F, np.ones((len(F), 1))])    # column of ones: the bias
    w = np.linalg.lstsq(F[:500], y[:500], rcond=None)[0]
    ok = ((F @ w > 0.5) == (y > 0.5))[500:]     # hits on 300 unseen phrases
    n, g = neg[500:], pos[500:]
    hits = [ok[(n == a) & (g == c)].mean() for a, c in kinds]
    print(f"{name:18s}" + "".join(f"{x:{w}.2f}" for x, w in zip(hits, widths)))
`} />

Each number is the fraction of phrases of that kind the neuron classifies correctly, out of $300$ it
didn't see while being fitted. On the row as it is, it gets every one of three kinds right and none
of <W>not bad</W>: that is the most a straight line can get out of the corners of an XOR, leaving one
out. After the ReLU it gets all four, with a first layer that has learned nothing. Thirty-two random units
already give the readout coordinates in which the square separates; training $\mathbf{W}_1$ is
choosing which ones.
