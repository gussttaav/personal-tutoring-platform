Accuracy over examples the network has already seen isn't a measurement, it's a memory. The
previous lesson, on implementing an MLP (multilayer perceptron) from scratch, ended with a loop that
trains on its own and a final figure that was never actually at risk: four hand-made examples, and
a network with more than enough weights to memorise them outright. What's missing isn't a better
loop. It's a text the network has never read, and a number that says what it did with it.

Those two pieces come from different places entirely. The representation was settled a whole block
ago: the bag of words turns any text into a fixed-length vector, and does it without anybody having
to read the data first. The other piece hasn't shown up in the course at all yet, and it takes up
half this lesson: setting a few reviews aside before training even starts, letting them take part in
nothing whatsoever, and only asking the network about them once there's nothing left to adjust.
With both pieces in place, this is Block 1 feeding the input and Block 2 sitting on top of it,
without changing a single line of the loop (checking that is part of the work); the only genuinely
new thing is where each piece comes from.

I generated the reviews with artificial intelligence: 240 one-line film reviews, half speaking well
of the film and half speaking badly, in the same register as the ten in
<Leccion slug="la-neurona">the lesson on the artificial neuron</Leccion>. That's worth keeping in
mind when you read the figure at the end, because a corpus generated this way comes out more regular
than one collected off the internet (fewer typos, less irony, a more repetitive vocabulary), and all
of that makes the classifier's job easier than it should be. They live in
<a href="/courses/dl-nlp/resenas-cine.en.json" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">this
file</a>, and it's worth opening before reading any further: looking at the data a model is about to
train on is part of the work, and here it only takes two minutes. It also holds the split (180 for
training, 60 for measuring), written directly into the file rather than drawn at run time, so the
numbers below come out identical for you too.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
Training and measuring have to look at different reviews. The moment a review takes part in
anything at all (the weights, the vocabulary, any number a person chooses), it stops being any use
for saying what the network will do with a text that doesn't exist yet.
</Callout>

## From the review to the vector, and from there to the network

The input is already fixed by the previous block. Each review is a document $d$, and its bag of
words is the vector counting how many times each vocabulary entry appears in it,

$$
\mathbf{x}_d = \sum_{t=1}^{T_d} \mathbf{o}_{w_t} \in \mathbb{R}^{\lvert V \rvert},
$$

with $T_d$ the number of tokens in the document and $\mathbf{o}_w$ the one-hot of entry $w$. The
vocabulary $V$ is the 436 distinct types appearing in the 180 training reviews, with no frequency
cut at all: <Leccion slug="vocabulario-oov">the lesson on vocabulary and out-of-vocabulary (OOV)
words</Leccion> cut at the $k$ most frequent types only because that corpus was large, and here the
180 training reviews add up to no more than 1,359 occurrences total (the first cell below counts
them), which isn't enough to justify throwing anything away.

Stack one review per row, and the network's input is
$\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{\text{train}} \times \lvert V \rvert} = \mathbb{R}^{180 \times 436}$,
with the labels sitting in $\mathbf{Y} \in \mathbb{R}^{180 \times 1}$, worth $1$ when the review
speaks well. On top of it goes the same MLP as the previous lesson, only at different sizes: a
hidden layer of $d_1 = 12$ neurons with $\tanh$ and one output with a sigmoid, so
$d_0, d_1, d_2 = 436, 12, 1$. Each step still looks at the whole training set, so
$B = N_{\text{train}} = 180$, and the forward pass takes the usual two lines:

$$
\mathbf{H}^{(1)} = \tanh\left(\mathbf{X}\left(\mathbf{W}^{(1)}\right)^{\top} + \mathbf{1}_B\left(\mathbf{b}^{(1)}\right)^{\top}\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{180 \times 12},
\qquad
\hat{\mathbf{Y}} = \sigma\left(\mathbf{H}^{(1)}\left(\mathbf{W}^{(2)}\right)^{\top} + \mathbf{1}_B\left(\mathbf{b}^{(2)}\right)^{\top}\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{180 \times 1}.
$$

The loss is the binary cross-entropy from <Leccion slug="funcion-de-perdida">the lesson on loss
functions</Leccion>, averaged over the 180 reviews, and the backward pass is the previous lesson's
untouched: $\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(2)} = \hat{\mathbf{Y}} - \mathbf{Y}$, the recurrence carrying it to
the hidden layer, and the four gradients with their $1/B$,

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}^{(l)}}\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{B}\left(\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(l)}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{(l-1)},
\qquad
\nabla_{\mathbf{b}^{(l)}}\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{B}\left(\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(l)}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{1}_B.
$$

The only thing that's actually changed is the sizes, and they've changed enormously: where the
four-phrase network had seventeen numbers to adjust, this one has
$436 \times 12 + 12 + 12 \times 1 + 1 = 5\,257$: the weights and biases of the two layers together.

That jump forces one single decision back into view, the scale of the initialisation. Each review
lights up roughly seven of the 436 coordinates (the first cell below measures it exactly), so a
hidden neuron's pre-activation is a sum of about seven weights: with normal weights at scale $1$,
that produces values large enough to leave the hyperbolic tangent saturated from the very first
step, and at scale $0.1$ it doesn't. The difference shows up directly in the result ($0.700$ accuracy
on the new reviews versus $0.867$), which is exactly why the cell below multiplies by $0.1$. That the
right scale depends on the width of the layers and the depth of the network is the debt the previous
lesson left marked down for Block 3, and it's still there, unpaid.

## Measuring where accuracy actually means something

Call $D_{\text{train}}$ the 180 reviews the network trains on and $D_{\text{test}}$ the 60 that get
held back, with $N_{\text{train}} = 180$ and $N_{\text{test}} = 60$. They share no review at all,
and that's the entirety of their relationship.

The same figure can be asked of either one, the **accuracy**: what fraction of its reviews gets the
right verdict. The output is a probability, so the verdict counts as positive when
$\hat{y}_d \geq 1/2$ (the boundary from <Leccion slug="la-neurona">the lesson on the artificial
neuron</Leccion>) and negative otherwise, giving

$$
\text{accuracy}(D) = \frac{1}{\lvert D \rvert}\left\lvert\left\{\, d \in D \;:\; \hat{y}_d \geq \tfrac{1}{2} \iff y_d = 1 \,\right\}\right\rvert.
$$

The double implication says the two statements carry equal weight: either the network says yes and
the label agrees, or neither of them does. And which set gets counted over is written right there
inside it, which is exactly what tells $\text{accuracy}(D_{\text{train}})$ apart from
$\text{accuracy}(D_{\text{test}})$, two numbers this lesson is about to look at side by side that
don't mean remotely the same thing.

That $V$ comes only out of $D_{\text{train}}$ is part of the measurement itself, not some
implementation detail. A vocabulary built over all 240 would give every test review its own
coordinate for each of its rare types, coordinates that exist only because that specific review
exists, and the network would show up to the exam with the space already cut to its exact measure.
What would vanish is exactly what needs measuring: the types a genuinely new text brings that the
vocabulary doesn't have. It's the OOV problem from that earlier lesson, arriving here without the
safety net that lesson built underneath it: there's no <W>\<UNK></W> to catch these, so a token
outside the vocabulary never reaches the vector at all.

This concession needs stating in full, because it has an extreme case. A review whose tokens were
all outside the vocabulary would receive $\mathbf{x}_d = \mathbf{0}$, and with it

$$
\hat{y} = \sigma\left(\mathbf{W}^{(2)}\tanh\left(\mathbf{b}^{(1)}\right) + b^{(2)}\right),
$$

a number that depends not on the review at all, only on the biases: every review this vocabulary
can't read gets the exact same verdict, whatever that happens to be. It's the same hole
<Leccion slug="la-neurona">the lesson on the artificial neuron</Leccion> already pointed at, when it
looked at what a neuron answers for the null vector, now sitting behind a real piece of text. And a
second concession, duller but far more common: sixty reviews measured once give a number with about
the reliability of sixty coin tosses, enough to tell a $0.9$ apart from a $0.5$, but not from a
$0.87$.

## The classifier, end to end

The first cell loads the reviews, tokenises them and builds the two matrices. Run it and check three
things: the width of the vector, how many of its coordinates are non-zero, and how many test tokens
fall through the cracks.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import json, re
import numpy as np
from collections import Counter
from pyodide.http import open_url

D = json.load(open_url("/courses/dl-nlp/resenas-cine.en.json"))
train, test = D["training"], D["test"]


def tokenise(text):                           # the previous block's tokenisation, in one
    return re.findall(r"\\w+", text.lower())   # line: down to lower case, punctuation out


freq = Counter(w for r in train for w in tokenise(r["text"]))
V = sorted(freq)                              # the vocabulary comes ONLY from the training set
pos = {e: i for i, e in enumerate(V)}


def matrix(reviews):
    X = np.zeros((len(reviews), len(V)))
    for i, r in enumerate(reviews):
        for token in tokenise(r["text"]):
            if token in pos:                  # what is not in V never reaches the vector
                X[i, pos[token]] += 1.0
    return X, np.array([[float(r["label"])] for r in reviews])


X_train, Y_train = matrix(train)
X_test, Y_test = matrix(test)

print("training:   %d reviews, %d positive" % (len(train), int(Y_train.sum())))
print("test:       %d reviews, %d positive" % (len(test), int(Y_test.sum())))
print("vocabulary: %d entries over %d occurrences" % (len(V), sum(freq.values())))
print("X_train", X_train.shape, "  X_test", X_test.shape)
print("non-zero coordinates: %.1f per review, %.1f %% of the vector"
      % (X_train.astype(bool).sum(1).mean(), 100 * X_train.astype(bool).mean()))

test_tokens = [w for r in test for w in tokenise(r["text"])]
out = [w for w in test_tokens if w not in pos]
print("\\nout of vocabulary: %d of %d occurrences (%.1f %%), %d types"
      % (len(out), len(test_tokens), 100 * len(out) / len(test_tokens), len(set(out))))

print("\\ntest review:", test[0]["text"])
print("its vector, without the zeros:",
      {V[j]: int(X_test[0, j]) for j in np.nonzero(X_test[0])[0]})
`} />

The three numbers show exactly what the network has got itself into. The vector has 436 coordinates,
and a review lights up $7.3$ of them on average, a mere $1.7\%$: the representation is sparse, as
the previous block promised, and every example leaves roughly 429 coordinates saying nothing at
all. And of the 455 occurrences in the test set, 23 (that $5.1\%$, spread across 23 types) aren't in
the vocabulary. The last line shows one of them directly: the review
<W>a beautiful film that I recommend without reservations</W> reaches the network with only seven
coordinates, because <W>reservations</W> shows up in none of the 180 training reviews and vanishes
without a trace.

The second cell is the previous lesson's loop with the names changed. It needs whatever the first
cell left behind, so run them in order. Watch the last two columns together, since that's really the
whole lesson.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

# Needs the previous cell: X_train, Y_train, X_test, Y_test and V.
B, d0, d1, d2 = len(X_train), len(V), 12, 1
eta, steps = 0.5, 600

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)                # fixed seed: you will see these numbers
W1, b1 = rng.normal(size=(d1, d0)) * 0.1, np.zeros(d1)
W2, b2 = rng.normal(size=(d2, d1)) * 0.1, np.zeros(d2)


def output(X, W1, b1, W2, b2):
    H1 = np.tanh(X @ W1.T + b1)
    return H1, 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-(H1 @ W2.T + b2)))


def accuracy(X, Y, W1, b1, W2, b2):
    P = output(X, W1, b1, W2, b2)[1]
    return float(((P >= 0.5) == (Y == 1.0)).mean())


print(" step      loss   training accuracy   test accuracy")
for t in range(steps + 1):
    H1, Y_hat = output(X_train, W1, b1, W2, b2)
    if t % 50 == 0:
        p = np.clip(Y_hat, 1e-12, 1.0 - 1e-12)
        print("%5d   %7.4f   %17.3f   %13.3f"
              % (t, -np.mean(Y_train * np.log(p) + (1.0 - Y_train) * np.log(1.0 - p)),
                 accuracy(X_train, Y_train, W1, b1, W2, b2),
                 accuracy(X_test, Y_test, W1, b1, W2, b2)))
    if t == steps:
        break
    D2 = Y_hat - Y_train                      # the backward pass, as in the previous lesson
    D1 = (D2 @ W2) * (1.0 - H1 ** 2)
    W1 -= eta * (D1.T @ X_train) / B
    b1 -= eta * D1.sum(0) / B
    W2 -= eta * (D2.T @ H1) / B
    b2 -= eta * D2.sum(0) / B

P_test = output(X_test, W1, b1, W2, b2)[1]
wrong = np.nonzero((P_test >= 0.5) != (Y_test == 1.0))[0]
print("\\nit gets %d of the %d test reviews wrong:" % (len(wrong), len(test)))
for i in wrong:
    print("   ŷ = %.3f   y = %d   %s" % (P_test[i, 0], int(Y_test[i, 0]), test[i]["text"]))
`} />

At step $0$ the network answers by coin toss, $0.511$ and $0.550$, with the loss sitting at
$0.6930$ (the $\log 2$ of a network that answers the same thing to everything). By step $100$ it's
already right about every review it's looking at, and $0.850$ right about the ones it isn't, and by
step $150$ the second column reaches $0.867$ and never lets go again. What's genuinely interesting
starts right there: between step $150$ and step $600$ the loss drops by a factor of twelve, from
$0.0437$ to $0.0035$, and the right-hand column doesn't move by a thousandth. Run it for 20,000 steps
instead of 600, and it's still sitting at $0.867$. The eight reviews it gets wrong aren't missed for
lack of training.

Read them, since that's where this lesson's real result lives. Four of the eight carry a <W>not</W>.
Three speak well of the film and come back negative, <W>I was not bored for a single minute</W> at
$0.001$ and <W>it is not bad at all</W> at $0.004$; the fourth runs the opposite way and is the
sharpest of the set, because the training reviews carry <W>I recommend it to anyone who enjoys good
cinema</W> as a positive one, and this test review adds only two more words to it. The other four
have nothing to do with negation at all: one loses four of its eight tokens to the vocabulary and
arrives as only three coordinates, two lean on entries the training reviews met far more often while
speaking well, and the last comes back at $0.511$, a tie the threshold has to break somehow. The
third cell goes after the first four.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

# Needs the two previous cells: tokenise, pos, V, output and the trained weights.
def vector(text):
    x = np.zeros((1, len(V)))
    for token in tokenise(text):
        if token in pos:
            x[0, pos[token]] += 1.0
    return x


def probability(text):
    return float(output(vector(text), W1, b1, W2, b2)[1][0, 0])


a = "the film is not boring, it is fun"
b = "the film is not fun, it is boring"
print("the same vector?", bool(np.array_equal(vector(a), vector(b))))
print("ŷ = %.4f   %s" % (probability(a), a))
print("ŷ = %.4f   %s" % (probability(b), b))

# What can the network know about "not"? Whatever its column says, and its column says this.
n_pos = sum(tokenise(r["text"]).count("not") for r in train if r["label"] == 1)
n_neg = sum(tokenise(r["text"]).count("not") for r in train if r["label"] == 0)
print("\\n\\"not\\": %d occurrences in positive reviews, %d in negative" % (n_pos, n_neg))

# And a review that shares no entry at all with the vocabulary.
new = "sublime filmmaking, essential viewing"
print("\\n%s\\n   null vector: %s   ŷ = %.4f"
      % (new, not vector(new).any(), probability(new)))
`} />

The first two sentences share the same words, with the same repetitions, in a different order, and
they say opposite things. Their vectors turn out identical (the first line confirms it), and so does
their probability, $0.0002$ for both: the network calls them both negative, getting one of them right
purely by accident. No amount of accuracy can fix that, because the mistake is already locked in
before the network ever sees its input.

The second output explains the four failures involving <W>not</W>. That entry appears 5 times in
positive training reviews and 20 times in negative ones, so its coordinate ends up pulling towards
negative, which is genuinely the best it can do: the coordinate only records how many times
<W>not</W> shows up, never what it's actually negating. And the last line closes out the previous
section's concession with an actual number: <W>sublime filmmaking, essential viewing</W> shares no
entry with the vocabulary at all, receives the null vector, and with it a $0.4140$ that depends on
nothing about what it says, only on the biases. Any other unreadable review would get that exact
same $0.4140$, and since it sits below $1/2$, every one of them would come back negative.

The block ends with a classifier that works, and with the ceiling of its representation in full
view. The two sentences in the last cell aren't some contrived case built specifically to make it
fail: they follow directly from $\mathbf{x}_d$ summing the one-hots of the tokens, and a sum has no
way of remembering what order its summands arrived in. Everything this block has built (the neuron,
the layers, the descent, the whole backward pass) operates after that sum happens, and no amount of
training can reach back to recover what the input already threw away before it ever existed. That's
exactly why the eight reviews hold out against 20,000 steps: it isn't the network that's falling
short here.

What's missing, then, is an input that arrives in order and a network able to read it that way,
token by token, carrying along whatever it has already seen. That's Block 3, on recurrent neural
networks, and <Leccion slug="por-que-falla-el-mlp">its lesson on why the MLP fails with
sequences</Leccion> opens by measuring the exact size of the problem: what happens to a multilayer
perceptron once the input stops having a fixed length, and the position each token occupies starts
to actually mean something.
