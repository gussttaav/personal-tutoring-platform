Accuracy over examples the network has already seen is not a measurement, it is a memory. The
previous lesson, on implementing an MLP (multilayer perceptron) from scratch, ends with a loop that
trains on its own and a final figure that was never at risk: four hand-made examples, and a network
with enough weights to learn them by heart. What is missing is not a better loop. It is a text the
network has never read, and a number that says what it did with it.

Those two pieces come from different places. The representation was settled a block ago: the bag of
words turns any text into a fixed-length vector, and it does it without anybody having to read the
data first. The other one has not appeared in the course at all, and it takes half this lesson:
setting a few reviews aside before starting, letting them take part in nothing, and asking the
network about them when there is nothing left to adjust. With both in place, this is Block 1 at the
input and Block 2 on top of it, without changing a line of the loop (checking that is part of the
work); the only new thing is where each piece comes from.

I generated the reviews with artificial intelligence: 240 one-line film reviews, half speaking well
of the film and half speaking badly, in the same register as the ten in <Leccion
slug="la-neurona">the lesson on the artificial neuron</Leccion>. That is worth having in view when
you read the figure at the end, because a corpus generated this way comes out more regular than one
collected off the internet (fewer typos, less irony, a more repetitive vocabulary), and all of it
makes the classifier's job easy. They are in <a href="/courses/dl-nlp/resenas-cine.en.json"
target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">this file</a>, and open it before going on: reading the
data a model is about to train on is part of the work, and here it is two minutes. Inside it is the
split as well (180 to train with, 60 to measure with), written into the file rather than drawn at
run time, so that the numbers below come out identical for you.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
Training and measuring have to look at different reviews. The moment a review takes part in
anything (in the weights, in the vocabulary, in any number somebody chooses), it stops being any
use for saying what the network will do with a text that does not exist yet.
</Callout>

## From the review to the vector, and from there to the network

The input is fixed by the previous block. Each review is a document $d$, and its bag of words is the
vector counting how many times each vocabulary entry appears in it,

$$
\mathbf{x}_d = \sum_{t=1}^{T_d} \mathbf{o}_{w_t} \in \mathbb{R}^{\lvert V \rvert},
$$

with $T_d$ the number of tokens in the document and $\mathbf{o}_w$ the one-hot of entry $w$. The
vocabulary $V$ is the 436 distinct types that appear in the 180 training reviews, with no frequency
cut: <Leccion slug="vocabulario-oov">the lesson on vocabulary and out-of-vocabulary (OOV)
words</Leccion> cut at the $k$ most frequent types because the corpus was large, and here the 180
training reviews come to no more than 1,359 occurrences in total (the first cell below counts them),
which is not enough to throw anything away.

Stacking one review per row, the network's input is
$\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{\text{train}} \times \lvert V \rvert} = \mathbb{R}^{180 \times 436}$,
with the labels in $\mathbf{Y} \in \mathbb{R}^{180 \times 1}$ worth $1$ when the review speaks well. On top of it goes the same MLP as
the previous lesson with different sizes: a hidden layer of $d_1 = 12$ neurons with $\tanh$ and one
output with the sigmoid, so $d_0, d_1, d_2 = 436, 12, 1$. Each step looks at the whole training set,
so $B = N_{\text{train}} = 180$ and the forward pass takes the usual two lines:

$$
\mathbf{H}^{(1)} = \tanh\left(\mathbf{X}\left(\mathbf{W}^{(1)}\right)^{\top} + \mathbf{1}_B\left(\mathbf{b}^{(1)}\right)^{\top}\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{180 \times 12},
\qquad
\hat{\mathbf{Y}} = \sigma\left(\mathbf{H}^{(1)}\left(\mathbf{W}^{(2)}\right)^{\top} + \mathbf{1}_B\left(\mathbf{b}^{(2)}\right)^{\top}\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{180 \times 1}.
$$

The loss is the binary cross-entropy of <Leccion slug="funcion-de-perdida">the lesson on loss
functions</Leccion>, averaged over the 180 reviews, and the backward pass is the previous lesson's
with nothing touched: $\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(2)} = \hat{\mathbf{Y}} - \mathbf{Y}$, the recurrence
that carries it to the hidden layer and the four gradients with their $1/B$,

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}^{(l)}}\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{B}\left(\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(l)}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{(l-1)},
\qquad
\nabla_{\mathbf{b}^{(l)}}\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{B}\left(\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(l)}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{1}_B.
$$

The only thing that has changed is the sizes, and they have changed a lot: where the network of the
four phrases had seventeen numbers to adjust, this one has
$436 \times 12 + 12 + 12 \times 1 + 1 = 5\,257$: the weights and the biases of the two layers.

That jump forces one single decision to be looked at again, the scale of the initialisation. Each
review lights about seven of the 436 coordinates (the first cell below measures it), so the
pre-activation of a hidden neuron is a sum of about seven weights: with normal weights of scale $1$
that gives values large enough to leave the hyperbolic tangent saturated from the first step, and
with scale $0.1$ it does not. The difference is measured in the result ($0.700$ accuracy over the
new reviews against $0.867$), which is why the cell below multiplies by $0.1$. That the good scale
depends on the width of the layers and on the depth of the network is the debt the previous lesson
left noted down for Block 3, and it is still noted.

## Measuring where accuracy means something

Let us call $D_{\text{train}}$ the 180 reviews the network is trained with and $D_{\text{test}}$ the
60 that are kept back, with $N_{\text{train}} = 180$ and $N_{\text{test}} = 60$. They share no
review, and that is the whole of their relationship.

The same figure can be asked of each, the **accuracy**: what fraction of its reviews receives the
right verdict. The output is a probability, so the verdict is positive when $\hat{y}_d \geq 1/2$
(the boundary from <Leccion slug="la-neurona">the lesson on the artificial neuron</Leccion>) and
negative when it is not, and the count is

$$
\text{accuracy}(D) = \frac{1}{\lvert D \rvert}\left\lvert\left\{\, d \in D \;:\; \hat{y}_d \geq \tfrac{1}{2} \iff y_d = 1 \,\right\}\right\rvert.
$$

The double implication says that the two statements are worth the same: either the network says yes
and the label does too, or neither of them does. And the set that is counted over goes written
inside, which is what tells $\text{accuracy}(D_{\text{train}})$ from
$\text{accuracy}(D_{\text{test}})$ (two numbers this lesson is going to look at together and that
do not mean remotely the same thing).

That $V$ comes only out of $D_{\text{train}}$ is part of the measurement, not an implementation
detail. A vocabulary built over the 240 would give each test review a coordinate of its own for each
of its rare types, coordinates that would exist because that review exists, and the network would
arrive at the exam with the space cut to its measure. What would disappear is exactly what has to be
measured: the types a new text brings and the vocabulary does not have. It is the OOV problem of
that lesson, and here it arrives without the safety net it put underneath: there is no <W>\<UNK></W>
to catch them, so a token outside the vocabulary does not reach the vector at all.

The concession has to be stated whole, because it has an extreme case. A review whose tokens were
all outside the vocabulary would receive $\mathbf{x}_d = \mathbf{0}$, and with it

$$
\hat{y} = \sigma\left(\mathbf{W}^{(2)}\tanh\left(\mathbf{b}^{(1)}\right) + b^{(2)}\right),
$$

a number that does not depend on the review but only on the biases: every review this vocabulary
cannot read receives the same verdict, whatever it is. It is the hole <Leccion slug="la-neurona">the
lesson on the artificial neuron</Leccion> already pointed at, when it looked at what a neuron
answers to the null vector, now with a real text behind it. And a second concession, duller and more
frequent: sixty reviews measured once give a number with the firmness of sixty coin tosses, enough
to separate a $0.9$ from a $0.5$ and not from a $0.87$.

## The classifier, end to end

The first cell brings in the reviews, tokenises them and builds the two matrices. Run it and look at
three things: the width of the vector, how many of its coordinates are non-zero, and how many test
tokens fall by the wayside.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import json, re
import numpy as np
from collections import Counter
from pyodide.http import open_url

D = json.load(open_url("/courses/dl-nlp/resenas-cine.en.json"))
train, test = D["training"], D["test"]


def tokenise(text):                           # the previous block's tokenisation, in one
    return re.findall(r"\\w+", text.lower())   # line: down to lower case, punctuation out


freq = Counter(w for r in train for w in tokenise(r["text"]))
V = sorted(freq)                              # the vocabulary comes ONLY from the training set
pos = {e: i for i, e in enumerate(V)}


def matrix(reviews):
    X = np.zeros((len(reviews), len(V)))
    for i, r in enumerate(reviews):
        for token in tokenise(r["text"]):
            if token in pos:                  # what is not in V never reaches the vector
                X[i, pos[token]] += 1.0
    return X, np.array([[float(r["label"])] for r in reviews])


X_train, Y_train = matrix(train)
X_test, Y_test = matrix(test)

print("training:   %d reviews, %d positive" % (len(train), int(Y_train.sum())))
print("test:       %d reviews, %d positive" % (len(test), int(Y_test.sum())))
print("vocabulary: %d entries over %d occurrences" % (len(V), sum(freq.values())))
print("X_train", X_train.shape, "  X_test", X_test.shape)
print("non-zero coordinates: %.1f per review, %.1f %% of the vector"
      % (X_train.astype(bool).sum(1).mean(), 100 * X_train.astype(bool).mean()))

test_tokens = [w for r in test for w in tokenise(r["text"])]
out = [w for w in test_tokens if w not in pos]
print("\\nout of vocabulary: %d of %d occurrences (%.1f %%), %d types"
      % (len(out), len(test_tokens), 100 * len(out) / len(test_tokens), len(set(out))))

print("\\ntest review:", test[0]["text"])
print("its vector, without the zeros:",
      {V[j]: int(X_test[0, j]) for j in np.nonzero(X_test[0])[0]})
`} />

The three numbers say what the network has got itself into. The vector has 436 coordinates and a
review lights $7.3$ of them on average, the $1.7\ \%$: the representation is sparse, as the previous
block announced, and each example leaves some 429 coordinates saying nothing. And of the 455
occurrences of the test set, 23 (the $5.1\ \%$, spread over 23 types) are not in the vocabulary. The
last line shows one of them: the review <W>a beautiful film that I recommend without
reservations</W> reaches the network with seven coordinates, because <W>reservations</W> appears in
none of the 180 and disappears without a trace.

The second cell is the previous lesson's loop with the names changed. It needs what the first left
behind, so run them in order. Look at the last two columns together, which is the whole lesson.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

# Needs the previous cell: X_train, Y_train, X_test, Y_test and V.
B, d0, d1, d2 = len(X_train), len(V), 12, 1
eta, steps = 0.5, 600

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)                # fixed seed: you will see these numbers
W1, b1 = rng.normal(size=(d1, d0)) * 0.1, np.zeros(d1)
W2, b2 = rng.normal(size=(d2, d1)) * 0.1, np.zeros(d2)


def output(X, W1, b1, W2, b2):
    H1 = np.tanh(X @ W1.T + b1)
    return H1, 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-(H1 @ W2.T + b2)))


def accuracy(X, Y, W1, b1, W2, b2):
    P = output(X, W1, b1, W2, b2)[1]
    return float(((P >= 0.5) == (Y == 1.0)).mean())


print(" step      loss   training accuracy   test accuracy")
for t in range(steps + 1):
    H1, Y_hat = output(X_train, W1, b1, W2, b2)
    if t % 50 == 0:
        p = np.clip(Y_hat, 1e-12, 1.0 - 1e-12)
        print("%5d   %7.4f   %17.3f   %13.3f"
              % (t, -np.mean(Y_train * np.log(p) + (1.0 - Y_train) * np.log(1.0 - p)),
                 accuracy(X_train, Y_train, W1, b1, W2, b2),
                 accuracy(X_test, Y_test, W1, b1, W2, b2)))
    if t == steps:
        break
    D2 = Y_hat - Y_train                      # the backward pass, as in the previous lesson
    D1 = (D2 @ W2) * (1.0 - H1 ** 2)
    W1 -= eta * (D1.T @ X_train) / B
    b1 -= eta * D1.sum(0) / B
    W2 -= eta * (D2.T @ H1) / B
    b2 -= eta * D2.sum(0) / B

P_test = output(X_test, W1, b1, W2, b2)[1]
wrong = np.nonzero((P_test >= 0.5) != (Y_test == 1.0))[0]
print("\\nit gets %d of the %d test reviews wrong:" % (len(wrong), len(test)))
for i in wrong:
    print("   ŷ = %.3f   y = %d   %s" % (P_test[i, 0], int(Y_test[i, 0]), test[i]["text"]))
`} />

At step $0$ the network answers by coin toss, $0.511$ and $0.550$, with the loss at $0.6930$ (the
$\log 2$ of a network that says the same thing to everything). By step $100$ it is already right
about every review it is looking at and $0.850$ about the ones it is not, and by step $150$ the
second column reaches $0.867$ and never lets go. The interesting part starts there: between step
$150$ and step $600$ the loss is divided by twelve, from $0.0437$ to $0.0035$, and the right-hand
column does not move by a thousandth. With 20,000 steps instead of 600 it is still at $0.867$. The
eight reviews it gets wrong are not missed for want of training.

Read them, which is where this lesson's result lives. Four of the eight carry a <W>not</W>. Three
speak well of the film and come back negative, <W>I was not bored for a single minute</W> at $0.001$
and <W>it is not bad at all</W> at $0.004$; the fourth runs the other way and is the sharpest of the
set, because the training reviews carry <W>I recommend it to anyone who enjoys good cinema</W> as a
positive one and the test review adds two words to it. The other four have nothing to do with
negation: one loses four of its eight tokens to the vocabulary and arrives as three coordinates, two
lean on entries the training reviews met far more often while speaking well, and the last comes back
at $0.511$, a tie the threshold has to break somehow. The third cell goes after the first four.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

# Needs the two previous cells: tokenise, pos, V, output and the trained weights.
def vector(text):
    x = np.zeros((1, len(V)))
    for token in tokenise(text):
        if token in pos:
            x[0, pos[token]] += 1.0
    return x


def probability(text):
    return float(output(vector(text), W1, b1, W2, b2)[1][0, 0])


a = "the film is not boring, it is fun"
b = "the film is not fun, it is boring"
print("the same vector?", bool(np.array_equal(vector(a), vector(b))))
print("ŷ = %.4f   %s" % (probability(a), a))
print("ŷ = %.4f   %s" % (probability(b), b))

# What can the network know about "not"? Whatever its column says, and its column says this.
n_pos = sum(tokenise(r["text"]).count("not") for r in train if r["label"] == 1)
n_neg = sum(tokenise(r["text"]).count("not") for r in train if r["label"] == 0)
print("\\n\\"not\\": %d occurrences in positive reviews, %d in negative" % (n_pos, n_neg))

# And a review that shares no entry at all with the vocabulary.
new = "sublime filmmaking, essential viewing"
print("\\n%s\\n   null vector: %s   ŷ = %.4f"
      % (new, not vector(new).any(), probability(new)))
`} />

The first two sentences have the same words, with the same repetitions, in a different order, and
they say opposite things. Their vector is identical (the first line says so) and so is their
probability, $0.0002$ both: the network calls them negative, and with one of them it is right by
accident. No accuracy can get past that, because the mistake is already made by the time the network
receives its input.

The second output explains the four failures with <W>not</W>. That entry appears 5 times in the
positive training reviews and 20 in the negative ones, so its coordinate ends up weighing towards
the negative, which is the best it can do: the coordinate says how many times <W>not</W> comes up,
and not what it falls on. And the last line closes the previous section's concession with a number:
<W>sublime filmmaking, essential viewing</W> shares no entry with the vocabulary, receives the null
vector and with it a $0.4140$ that does not depend on what it says, only on the biases. Any other
unreadable review would receive that same $0.4140$, and since it is below $1/2$, all of them would
be negative.

The block ends with a classifier that works and with the ceiling of its representation in plain
sight. The two sentences in the last cell are not a contrived case built to make it fail: they are
the consequence of $\mathbf{x}_d$ summing the one-hots of the tokens, and a sum does not remember
what order its summands arrived in. Everything this block has built (the neuron, the layers, the
descent, the whole backward pass) works after that sum, and no amount of training reaches back to
recover what the input threw away before it existed. That is why the eight reviews hold out against
20,000 steps: it is not the network that is falling short.

What is missing, then, is an input that arrives in order and a network able to read it that way,
token by token, dragging along what it has already seen. That is Block 3, on recurrent neural
networks, and <Leccion slug="por-que-falla-el-mlp">its lesson on why the MLP fails with
sequences</Leccion> begins by measuring the exact size of the problem: what happens to a multilayer
perceptron when the input stops having a fixed length and the position each token occupies starts to
mean something.
