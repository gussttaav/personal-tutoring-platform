      import numpy as np


      def lstm_step(h, c, x, P):
          def pre(k):
              Wx, Wh, b = P[k]
              return Wx @ x + Wh @ h + b

          def sigmoid(z):
              return 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-z))

          f, i, o = sigmoid(pre("f")), sigmoid(pre("i")), sigmoid(pre("o"))
          c_new = f * c + i * np.tanh(pre("c"))
          return o * np.tanh(c_new), c_new

{/* COURSE-P5-03 — Block 3, Lesson 5. COURSE-P11-06 — English translation. */}

The previous lesson, on the vanishing gradient, closed with a diagnosis and a direction. The
diagnosis: as long as every step of the backward pass goes through the same matrix, the result is a
power of the distance. The direction: a state that moves forward as a running sum, and the network
built around one, the long short-term memory (LSTM). But naming it isn't building it. A memory that
only adds has a defect as serious as the one it fixes: it never erases anything. What's left is the
hard part, deciding step by step what to keep, what to write over it, and how much of what's stored
to let out.

An ordinary sentence shows that these are three decisions, not one. Take <W>The cameras that the
mayor installed in the square last year are broken</W>. To write <W>are</W> rather than <W>is</W>,
the network has to carry the plural of <W>cameras</W> across ten positions, without <W>mayor</W> or
<W>square</W> (both singular, both closer) overwriting it along the way. Carrying it intact is one
thing; storing it when <W>cameras</W> appears, and not at every passing word, is another; and keeping
it stored through those ten positions *without* letting it drive the network's answers at the
positions in between is a third. The vanilla recurrent neural network (RNN) handles all three with
the same move, because it only has one: rewriting the whole of $\mathbf{h}_t$ at every step. That's
why it can't do any of them well.

Separating the three jobs starts with separating two states. Alongside the $\mathbf{h}_t$ you
already know, a second vector appears, the **cell state** $\mathbf{c}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}$, which
is the memory proper, and the way it moves forward is the only genuinely new idea in this lesson.
Everything else in the LSTM exists to control that vector.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
The LSTM splits apart what the RNN kept together: a memory that crosses the sequence by adding to
itself, and three gates (numbers between $0$ and $1$ that the network computes at every step) that
decide how much of that memory is kept, what gets written into it, and what goes out from it to the
rest of the network.
</Callout>

## Three gates, each fixing a failure of the previous version

Start from the additive path on its own and add what it's missing as we go. The **candidate** is
the vector that step $t$ proposes to store, computed by an ordinary layer from the token and the
previous state:

$$
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_t = \tanh\left(\mathbf{W}_{xc}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{W}_{hc}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}_c\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h},
$$

and let the memory accumulate it: $\mathbf{c}_t = \mathbf{c}_{t-1} + \tilde{\mathbf{c}}_t$. For the
gradient this is perfect: the derivative of $\mathbf{c}_t$ with respect to $\mathbf{c}_{t-1}$ is the
identity, with no matrix applied $d$ times. As a memory, though, it's useless, for two reasons. It
never forgets: whatever went into coordinate 3 while reading <W>cameras</W> is still there three
hundred tokens later, in a paragraph about something else entirely. And it grows without limit: each
term lives in $(-1, 1)$ and $T$ of them get added, so a coordinate can end up at $40$, where the
$\tanh$ reading it is completely flat. That's the saturation you already saw in the explorable in <Leccion slug="funciones-activacion">the lesson on activation functions</Leccion>.

The two failures are really one: the sum has no way of saying *no*. Give it one, in the form of a
factor per coordinate between $0$ and $1$ that the network computes at every step from what it's
reading. That's the **forget gate**:

$$
\mathbf{f}_t = \sigma\left(\mathbf{W}_{xf}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{W}_{hf}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}_f\right) \in (0,1)^{d_h},
\qquad
\mathbf{c}_t = \mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{c}_{t-1} + \tilde{\mathbf{c}}_t.
$$

The activation is the logistic sigmoid rather than the candidate's $\tanh$, for the same reason as
the output layer in <Leccion slug="la-rnn-vanilla">the lesson on the vanilla RNN</Leccion>: what's needed is a fraction of what was there, and fractions
run from $0$ to $1$. The $\odot$ deserves an equally careful reading. Multiplying coordinate by
coordinate lets coordinate 3 be kept whole while coordinate 7 is erased in the same step; a matrix
would mix the two, and mixing is the last thing a memory wants.

That leaves the other side. This version writes $\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_t$ at *every* step, whatever the
token is worth: <W>that</W> and <W>the</W> write as much as <W>cameras</W>, and the only way to undo
what's been written is to close $\mathbf{f}_t$, which also wipes out whatever that coordinate was
already holding. Making the writing conditional takes another factor of the same kind, the **input
gate**:

$$
\mathbf{i}_t = \sigma\left(\mathbf{W}_{xi}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{W}_{hi}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}_i\right),
\qquad
\mathbf{c}_t = \mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{c}_{t-1} + \mathbf{i}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{c}}_t.
$$

That leaves the question of what the rest of the network gets to see. If the hidden state were
$\mathbf{h}_t = \tanh(\mathbf{c}_t)$ (all of the memory, all of the time), the plural of
<W>cameras</W> would drive the answer throughout those ten positions, when what's needed is for it to
wait. Keeping something *for later* and not using it *now* are two different things, so there's a
third gate, the **output gate**, which makes the hidden state a partial view of the memory:

$$
\mathbf{o}_t = \sigma\left(\mathbf{W}_{xo}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{W}_{ho}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}_o\right),
\qquad
\mathbf{h}_t = \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tanh\left(\mathbf{c}_t\right).
$$

That's what the vanilla RNN couldn't do: its $\mathbf{h}_t$ had two jobs at once, remembering and
showing. The LSTM splits them: $\mathbf{c}_t$ remembers, and $\mathbf{h}_t$ shows.

Putting the four pieces together (three gates and a candidate), the full step looks like this, with
$\mathbf{h}_0 = \mathbf{c}_0 = \mathbf{0}$ for the same reason as in the RNN:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{f}_t &= \sigma\left(\mathbf{W}_{xf}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{W}_{hf}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}_f\right), &
\mathbf{i}_t &= \sigma\left(\mathbf{W}_{xi}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{W}_{hi}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}_i\right), \\
\mathbf{o}_t &= \sigma\left(\mathbf{W}_{xo}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{W}_{ho}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}_o\right), &
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_t &= \tanh\left(\mathbf{W}_{xc}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{W}_{hc}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}_c\right), \\
\mathbf{c}_t &= \mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{c}_{t-1} + \mathbf{i}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{c}}_t, &
\mathbf{h}_t &= \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tanh\left(\mathbf{c}_t\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

Four of those equations share one form, and two don't. The top four are the recurrence from <Leccion slug="la-rnn-vanilla">the lesson on the vanilla RNN</Leccion>
copied four times, each with $\mathbf{W}_{x\ast} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h \times d_{\text{model}}}$,
$\mathbf{W}_{h\ast} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h \times d_h}$ and $\mathbf{b}_{\ast} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}$, and
there's the price: the LSTM costs
$4\left(d_h \cdot d_h + d_h \cdot d_{\text{model}} + d_h\right)$ weights, four times as many as the
vanilla RNN. What it doesn't cost is length: here too, $T$ appears in no shape.

## Why the gradient survives the additive path

The claim that the cell "adds" has come up three times now; time to cash it in. Look at the
second-to-last equation coordinate by coordinate, which is where the meaning of the $\odot$ becomes
visible:

$$
c_{t,j} = f_{t,j}\,c_{t-1,j} + i_{t,j}\,\tilde{c}_{t,j}.
$$

Coordinate $j$ of the new cell depends on coordinate $j$ of the old one and on no other: along this
path the coordinates don't mix, because there's no matrix to mix them. Differentiating while holding
the gates still (I'll come back to this), the direct path from one step to the one before is worth

$$
\frac{\partial c_{t,j}}{\partial c_{t-1,j}} = f_{t,j},
$$

a number, not a matrix. Chain it from the end back to a distant step $k$, the way the previous lesson
chained the backward recurrence, and what reaches the cell at $k$ along that path is

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial c_{k,j}} = \left(\prod_{t=k+1}^{T} f_{t,j}\right)\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial c_{T,j}} + \left(\text{what comes in through the gates}\right).
$$

Set that beside the previous lesson's $\left(\gamma\,\sigma_{\max}\right)^{T-k}$ and count the
differences. There are three. First, the base is no longer a property of one matrix shared by
everything, but a number per coordinate and per step that the network *computes* from what it's
reading. Second, there's no $\tanh$ mask biting at every step along this path (no $\gamma \le 1$ that
can only make things worse), because the cell crosses each step with a product and a sum and nothing
else. And the third clinches it: $f_{t,j} = 1$ is both reachable and stable, because a saturated
sigmoid sits at $1$ with almost zero slope, so a coordinate the network learns to keep open
multiplies by $1$ as many times as needed. The RNN's $\rho = 1$ was a knife-edge shared by every
coordinate; this is a decision each coordinate makes for itself.

<Details summary='See what "holding the gates still" left out'>
$\mathbf{f}_t$, $\mathbf{i}_t$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_t$ are computed from $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$, and
$\mathbf{h}_{t-1} = \mathbf{o}_{t-1} \odot \tanh(\mathbf{c}_{t-1})$ depends on $\mathbf{c}_{t-1}$. So
the full derivative adds, to the $f_{t,j}$ above, the paths that run through the three gates and
through the candidate, and every one of them crosses a $\mathbf{W}_{h\ast}$ and an activation mask:
they behave like the paths in the previous lesson, and they vanish the same way.

Their vanishing breaks nothing, and that's the part worth thinking about. Those paths adjust *how
the decision is made* at each step, and a decision at this step relies on what's nearby; the signal
that has to arrive from far away travels the additive path, and that path crosses no matrix. The
LSTM doesn't eliminate the previous lesson's product: it moves it off the long path and leaves it on
the short ones.
</Details>

Two concessions close the account, because without them this would read as a guarantee. If the
network learns $f_{t,j}$ around $0.7$, the product sinks as it did before; the difference is that it
sinks because the network chose that, not because the architecture forces it. And explosion is still
possible along the paths in the box above, so the previous lesson's gradient clipping stays in use,
unchanged.

## The LSTM, step by step, in NumPy

Two cells. The first transcribes the six-equation block and runs it on a short sequence; the second
measures how much of a nudge given at the start of a long sequence survives to the end.

I draw the weights from a generator with a fixed seed, and they're untrained, so the gates don't
mean anything yet. What I did set by hand is the forget gate's bias, and the cell runs with two
different values of it: it's the quickest way to see that $\mathbf{b}_f$ decides how much the
network remembers *before* it has learned anything. Look at the last line of each block.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

d_model, d_h, T = 4, 3, 6
X = np.random.default_rng(1).normal(size=(T, d_model)) * 0.5


def sigmoid(z):
    return 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-z))


def weights(forget_bias):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(0)                 # the same weights in both cases
    return {k: (rng.normal(size=(d_h, d_model)) * 0.5,
                rng.normal(size=(d_h, d_h)) * 0.5,
                np.full(d_h, b))
            for k, b in [("f", forget_bias), ("i", 0.0), ("o", 0.0), ("c", 0.0)]}


def step(P, h, c, x):
    def pre(k):
        Wx, Wh, b = P[k]
        return Wx @ x + Wh @ h + b
    f, i, o = sigmoid(pre("f")), sigmoid(pre("i")), sigmoid(pre("o"))
    c = f * c + i * np.tanh(pre("c"))              # the additive path, in one line
    return o * np.tanh(c), c, f, i, o


for bias in [1.0, 3.0]:
    P = weights(bias)
    h, c, forgets = np.zeros(d_h), np.zeros(d_h), []
    print("b_f = %.0f" % bias)
    print("  t      f_t              i_t              o_t              c_t")
    for t, x in enumerate(X, 1):
        h, c, f, i, o = step(P, h, c, x)
        forgets.append(f)
        print("  %d  %s %s %s %s" % (t, np.round(f, 2), np.round(i, 2),
                                     np.round(o, 2), np.round(c, 2)))
    print("  what survives of c_1 in c_6:", np.round(np.prod(forgets[1:], axis=0), 3))

The three gates live in $(0,1)$ and each takes a different value in every coordinate, which is all
the formula promised. Now compare the two blocks. With $\mathbf{b}_f = 1$, the forget gate hovers
around $0.7$ and about $20\%$ of $\mathbf{c}_1$ makes it to $\mathbf{c}_6$; with $\mathbf{b}_f = 3$,
it sits near $0.95$ and $78\%$ gets through, with the same weights and the same sequence. Five steps
are enough for a bias to make the difference between a memory and a sieve, hence the habit of
initialising $\mathbf{b}_f$ to a positive value: it's cheaper for the network to learn to forget than
to learn to remember. The first row of $\mathbf{c}_t$, on the other hand, is identical in both
blocks: at $t = 1$ the gate multiplies $\mathbf{c}_0 = \mathbf{0}$.

import numpy as np

T, eps = 40, 1e-6
X = np.random.default_rng(2).normal(size=T) * 0.5   # one coordinate, T steps


def sigmoid(z):
    return 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-z))


def rnn(nudge):                                     # h_t = tanh(w h_{t-1} + u x_t)
    h = 0.0
    for t, x in enumerate(X):
        h = np.tanh(0.9 * h + 0.5 * x)
        if t == 0:
            h += nudge                              # nudge the state of step 1
    return h


def lstm(nudge, b_f):                               # c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t c~_t
    c = 0.0
    for t, x in enumerate(X):
        c = sigmoid(0.5 * x + b_f) * c + sigmoid(0.5 * x) * np.tanh(0.5 * x)
        if t == 0:
            c += nudge                              # nudge the cell of step 1
    return c


print("of a nudge given at step 1, how much is left 39 steps later:")
print("  RNN,  w   = 0.9     %.2e" % abs((rnn(eps) - rnn(0.0)) / eps))
for b_f in [0.0, 3.0, 5.0]:
    q = abs((lstm(eps, b_f) - lstm(0.0, b_f)) / eps)
    print("  LSTM, b_f = %.0f       %.2e   (f_t around %.3f)" % (b_f, q, sigmoid(b_f)))

Four numbers, three readings. From the RNN, $1.1 \times 10^{-3}$ gets through: the matrix accounts
for $0.9^{39} \approx 0.016$ and the $\tanh$ mask for the rest (another order of magnitude), the
$\gamma \le 1$ that the previous lesson said could only make things worse. The second line shows the
LSTM coming out *worse* than the RNN: with $\mathbf{b}_f = 0$ the gate hovers around $0.5$, and
thirty-nine factors like that leave about $0.5^{39} \approx 2 \times 10^{-12}$ of the nudge, too
little for floating-point arithmetic to register, so the line prints a flat zero. This memory is
worse than the one it was meant to fix. The additive path is no free gift; all it does is make
reachable what was out of reach in the RNN. The last two lines are what "reachable" means: with the
gate at $0.95$, $14\%$ survives, and at $0.993$, $76\%$ does, over the same distance at which the RNN
was down to three zeros after the decimal point.

The LSTM solves the previous lesson's problem, but at a price the weight count only half captures.
Eight matrices, two states to carry and three gates to tune: each piece arrived to fix a specific
failure, true, but none came with a proof that it was necessary. And at least one of them looks
suspicious. The input gate decides how much to write at the very moment the forget gate has decided
how much to keep, and those are really one question seen from two sides: if you're going to store
something new, it's because the old matters less to you.

Someone pulled on that thread. Merging those two gates into one, dropping the output gate along with
the whole cell state, and getting by with three quarters of the weights, while holding on to almost
everything this lesson has gained, is the subject of <Leccion slug="gru">the next lesson, on the
gated recurrent unit (GRU): the simplified version</Leccion>.
