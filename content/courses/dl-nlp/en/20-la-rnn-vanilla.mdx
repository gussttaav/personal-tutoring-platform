      import numpy as np


      def step(h, x, Whh, Wxh, bh):
          return np.tanh(Whh @ h + Wxh @ x + bh)


      def forward(X, Whh, Wxh, bh):
          h = np.zeros(Whh.shape[0])
          states = []

{/* COURSE-P5-03 — Block 3, Lesson 2. COURSE-P11-06 — English translation. */}

The previous lesson, on why the multilayer perceptron (MLP) fails on sequences, left two defects
measured: what a long text loses to truncation, and what the network works out at one position and
can't use at another. They share one cause, and it lies neither in the size of the network nor in
how it's trained. It lies in the input: that network didn't read a sequence, it received a
vector. What follows is the fix, and it comes with a property nobody expects of a fix: the network
that applies it is smaller than the one that fails.

It's called a **recurrent neural network** (RNN), and in its original form (the one everyone calls
*vanilla*) it fits in two matrices and a loop. What's hard about it isn't how it works, which takes
two lines, but why something so short achieves what the big network couldn't. The whole answer is
in one thing it gives up: this network doesn't look at the sentence, it walks through it.

Walk through it yourself before going on. In the explorable below, each advance applies the same
step to one more position: the two matrices are always the same, and only the state coming in from
the left changes. It's also how you'll find it drawn in any book that covers it.

That sequence invites one reading, and it's false: the boxes are not different layers. They're the
same function drawn once per position, the way you'd draw the iterations of a `for` loop, which is
why the labels repeat instead of being numbered. A network with one layer per position would have a
set of weights for each; this one has a single set, however long the sequence.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
An RNN is a loop: the same step, applied at each position, carrying along a vector that summarises
what has been read so far. The weights don't know which position they're at: the state is the only
thing that tells step 8 from step 2.
</Callout>

## A state rewritten at every position

Each position contributes the vector of its vocabulary entry, $\mathbf{x}_t = \mathbf{e}_{w_t} \in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}$, the row of $\mathbf{E}$ that belongs to it, just as in the previous
lesson. What's new is the other vector, the one the network passes to itself from one step to the
next: the **hidden state** $\mathbf{h}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}$, where $d_h$ is chosen when the
network is built and is the only thing that decides how much fits in that memory. Let's also fix
$\mathbf{h}_0 = \mathbf{0}$, because before the first token has been read there's nothing to
remember.

With that, a step is an ordinary layer that takes two inputs instead of one:

$$
\mathbf{h}_t = \tanh\left(\mathbf{W}_{hh}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{W}_{xh}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_h\right),
$$

with $\mathbf{W}_{hh} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h \times d_h}$ reading the previous state,
$\mathbf{W}_{xh} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h \times d_{\text{model}}}$ reading the current token, and
$\mathbf{b}_h \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}$. The hyperbolic tangent takes the place of $\varphi$ from <Leccion slug="funciones-activacion">the
lesson on activation functions</Leccion>, and any of the three seen there could take it: the formula
is written the same with each. What separates them here is something no layer in the previous block
did, which is that the output comes back in. $\mathbf{h}_t$ is the input to step $t + 1$, which in
turn feeds step $t + 2$, so whatever the activation does to the state is repeated once per token.

That rules out ReLU first, because on the right it has no ceiling. Look at it in a single
coordinate, with a recurrent weight of $2$ and inputs that don't counteract it: ReLU lets the whole
state through, each iteration doubles it, and twenty iterations multiply it by more than a million.
And that happens in the forward pass, before a single gradient has been computed. The hyperbolic
tangent keeps every coordinate in $(-1, 1)$ at $t = 1$ just as at $t = 300$, because its ceiling
doesn't depend on what goes into it. There are RNNs with ReLU, but they need $\mathbf{W}_{hh}$
initialised carefully so that at the start it stretches nothing; the $\tanh$ asks for no such care.

The sigmoid is bounded too, and it loses to the $\tanh$ on the two differences that lesson pointed
out. The first is the centre. With the sigmoid, every coordinate of the state would be positive and
could say how much, but never which way; and the $\mathbf{h}_0 = \mathbf{0}$ the reading starts
from, which for the $\tanh$ is the centre of its range, would be for the sigmoid an edge it never
reaches. The second is the slope, which at the origin is $1$ for the $\tanh$ and $0.25$ for the
sigmoid. When the time comes to train this network, the error will travel from the last position to
the first and cross that slope once per step: with the sigmoid, that factor would divide it by at
least four at every one. That's why I write it by name rather than leaving $\varphi$: it's the one
the recurrence carries throughout the block.

Step $t = 1$ seems to call for a rule of its own, and it doesn't. With $\mathbf{h}_0 = \mathbf{0}$
the first term disappears by itself:

$$
\mathbf{h}_1 = \tanh\left(\mathbf{W}_{hh}\mathbf{0} + \mathbf{W}_{xh}\mathbf{x}_1 + \mathbf{b}_h\right) = \tanh\left(\mathbf{W}_{xh}\mathbf{x}_1 + \mathbf{b}_h\right).
$$

<Details summary="See the whole sequence inside the last state">
Replace $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ with its own definition, starting from the one above:

$$
\mathbf{h}_2 = \tanh\left(\mathbf{W}_{hh}\tanh\left(\mathbf{W}_{xh}\mathbf{x}_1 + \mathbf{b}_h\right) + \mathbf{W}_{xh}\mathbf{x}_2 + \mathbf{b}_h\right),
$$

and once more, which brings the pattern into view:

$$
\mathbf{h}_3 = \tanh\left(\mathbf{W}_{hh}\tanh\left(\mathbf{W}_{hh}\tanh\left(\mathbf{W}_{xh}\mathbf{x}_1 + \mathbf{b}_h\right) + \mathbf{W}_{xh}\mathbf{x}_2 + \mathbf{b}_h\right) + \mathbf{W}_{xh}\mathbf{x}_3 + \mathbf{b}_h\right).
$$

Each new position wraps everything before it in one more $\tanh$ and one more $\mathbf{W}_{hh}$,
without anything falling out along the way: $\mathbf{x}_1$ is still in there when $t$ is $3$, and
would still be at $t = 300$.
</Details>

That nesting answers the question of where the order is kept. It isn't in the weights (they're the
same at all three positions) but in *how many times* each token has passed through the matrix
$\mathbf{W}_{hh}$ before reaching the end: in $\mathbf{h}_3$, the vector $\mathbf{x}_1$ has crossed
it twice, $\mathbf{x}_2$ once and $\mathbf{x}_3$ not at all. Swap two tokens and you change that
count.

What's left is to get an answer out, and there are $T$ states to get it from. A verdict per document
takes the last one, $\mathbf{h}_T$, because it's the only one that has read the whole text: the
nesting above puts every token inside it, while any earlier $\mathbf{h}_t$ hasn't seen what comes
after $t$. The answer comes out of one more layer, placed on top of it:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \sigma\left(\mathbf{W}_{hy}\mathbf{h}_T + \mathbf{b}_y\right),
$$

with $\mathbf{W}_{hy}$ of shape $1 \times d_h$ (one row per output, and here there is one output)
and $\mathbf{b}_y$ of one coordinate. The activation is a different one, and not by oversight:
$\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid and not the loop's $\tanh$, because what the network delivers is a
probability and the $\tanh$ returns numbers between $-1$ and $1$. It's the output layer of the
previous block's review classifier without a single change; what's different is where its input
comes from. That's why the drawing above leaves it out, and so does the count further down: the
recurrence is the loop's two matrices, and this third one is a borrowed layer you already know how
to build.

Now go back over the shapes of those two matrices and check what appears in neither: $T$.
$\mathbf{W}_{hh}$ is $d_h \times d_h$, $\mathbf{W}_{xh}$ is $d_h \times d_{\text{model}}$, and
neither of them knows how many times it will be used. That leaves the two operations the fixed
length imposed with nothing to do: there's no $T_{\max}$ to truncate at, and no leftover positions
to fill with the zero vector (the previous lesson's padding), because there are no extra positions.
There are exactly the ones the document brings.

Underneath that there's a change of unit worth seeing. In the concatenated MLP, one input to the
network was a *document*: the first layer had as many columns as the whole document took up. Here
one input to the network is a *token*, because $\mathbf{W}_{xh}$ has $d_{\text{model}}$ columns and
not one more, and at each step the network sees nothing besides the vector of that position and the
state it brings with it. The length of the text hasn't disappeared from the system: it has moved
from the shapes to the number of iterations of the loop. And that move is what changes everything,
because a shape is fixed when the network is built and isn't touched again (that's what forced the
truncation), whereas the number of iterations is decided by each document as it arrives.

## One matrix for every position

Add up what the recurrence's three pieces take:

$$
\underbrace{d_h \cdot d_h}_{\mathbf{W}_{hh}} \;+\; \underbrace{d_h \cdot d_{\text{model}}}_{\mathbf{W}_{xh}} \;+\; \underbrace{d_h}_{\mathbf{b}_h} \quad \text{weights}.
$$

Set it beside the previous lesson's, $d_1 \cdot T_{\max} \cdot d_{\text{model}}$. What's worth
looking at isn't which one comes out smaller (that's a consequence) but which letters appear in
each: $T_{\max}$ was in that one, and it isn't in this one. Reading documents ten times longer
doesn't add a single weight.

And the saving is the least of it. What changes is what each weight is for. In concatenation, the
piece of the first layer that read position 2 and the one that read position 8 were two different
matrices, adjusted with different examples, and the second stayed as it came out of initialisation
if the corpus never brought anything interesting that far along. In the recurrence there is *one*
$\mathbf{W}_{xh}$, and the vectors of every position of every document pass through it. Whatever it
learns to do with the vector of <W>script</W> it applies wherever <W>script</W> is, because it has
no way of knowing where that is.

Sharing the weights is what makes an RNN an RNN. Take it away (a different matrix per position) and
what's left is the concatenated MLP under another name.

The flip side comes with the gradient, and it's worth stating here even though this lesson doesn't
resolve it. $\mathbf{W}_{hh}$ doesn't take part once in computing $\mathbf{h}_T$: it takes part $T$
times, as the nesting above shows. So the loss depends on that matrix along $T$ different paths, and
its gradient will have to collect them all.

## Running the recurrence on two sentences of different length

Twelve vocabulary entries (this cell uses eight; the last four are for the next one), embeddings of
$d_{\text{model}} = 4$ and a state of $d_h = 3$ coordinates. I take the weights from a generator
with a fixed seed, and they're untrained: what to look at in this cell isn't which values come out
but how many times each matrix is used. `step` is the recurrence transcribed line for line, and
`read` applies it to a sentence, printing the state at each position.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

V = ["the", "it", "actress", "film", "script", "saves", "sinks", "and",
     "is", "not", "boring", "fun"]
d_model, d_h = 4, 3
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

E = rng.normal(size=(len(V), d_model)) * 0.5     # (12, 4): one row per entry
Wxh = rng.normal(size=(d_h, d_model)) * 0.5      # (3, 4): reads the current token
Whh = rng.normal(size=(d_h, d_h)) * 0.5          # (3, 3): reads the previous state
bh = np.zeros(d_h)


def step(h, x):
    return np.tanh(Whh @ h + Wxh @ x + bh)       # the same weights at every position


def read(sentence):
    h, states = np.zeros(d_h), []                # h_0 = 0, before reading anything
    print('"%s"' % sentence)
    for t, word in enumerate(sentence.split(), 1):
        h = step(h, E[V.index(word)])
        states.append(h)
        print("   h_%-2d  %s" % (t, np.round(h, 3)))
    return states


short = read("the script sinks the film")
long = read("the actress saves the film and the script sinks it")

print("\\nshapes:  Whh %s   Wxh %s   bh %s   (none mentions T)" % (
    Whh.shape, Wxh.shape, bh.shape))
print('"the script" read at positions 1-2 and at 7-8:')
print("   %s   %s" % (np.round(short[0], 3), np.round(short[1], 3)))
print("   %s   %s" % (np.round(long[6], 3), np.round(long[7], 3)))
`} />

Count the rows: five for the first sentence, ten for the second, and the same `Whh` and `Wxh` in
all fifteen. There's no version of the network for short texts and another for long ones, and
nothing has had to be truncated or padded: the loop runs as many iterations as the sentence asks
for.

The last two lines are the previous lesson answered. <W>the script</W> occupies positions 1 and 2
of the first sentence and 7 and 8 of the second, and the states it produces are almost the same:
$(-0.602,\ -0.098,\ -0.527)$ against $(-0.632,\ -0.026,\ -0.540)$. Almost, not exactly, and both
halves matter. They're alike because the weights that read those two tokens are the same weights,
wherever the tokens are; in the concatenated MLP there was no reason for them to be alike, and the
previous lesson measured that they weren't alike at all. And they differ because the state they
brought with them wasn't the same, which is exactly the order information the bag of words had
nowhere to keep.

You can isolate that difference, and the previous block left the case ready for it: the two
sentences that closed <Leccion slug="proyecto-sentimiento">the sentiment project</Leccion>. They
have the same tokens, with the same repetitions, and they say opposite things.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

V = ["the", "it", "actress", "film", "script", "saves", "sinks", "and",
     "is", "not", "boring", "fun"]
d_model, d_h = 4, 3
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)                   # the same network as the previous cell
E = rng.normal(size=(len(V), d_model)) * 0.5
Wxh = rng.normal(size=(d_h, d_model)) * 0.5
Whh = rng.normal(size=(d_h, d_h)) * 0.5
bh = np.zeros(d_h)


def tokens(sentence):
    return sentence.replace(",", " ").split()    # drop the comma, as that project did


def states(sentence):
    h, hs = np.zeros(d_h), []
    for word in tokens(sentence):
        h = np.tanh(Whh @ h + Wxh @ E[V.index(word)] + bh)
        hs.append(h)
    return hs


def bag(sentence):
    v = np.zeros(len(V), dtype=int)
    for word in tokens(sentence):
        v[V.index(word)] += 1
    return v


a = "the film is not boring, it is fun"
b = "the film is not fun, it is boring"
print('a  "%s"\\nb  "%s"' % (a, b))
print("\\nbag of a  %s\\nbag of b  %s\\n" % (bag(a), bag(b)))
for t, (pa, ha, pb, hb) in enumerate(zip(tokens(a), states(a), tokens(b), states(b)), 1):
    print("h_%d  %-10s %-24s %-10s %s" % (t, pa, np.round(ha, 3), pb, np.round(hb, 3)))

print("\\n           T_max=30      T_max=300")
concat = [128 * t * 100 for t in (30, 300)]      # d1 · T_max · d_model
recurr = [128 * 128 + 128 * 100 + 128] * 2       # d_h² + d_h · d_model + d_h
print("concat.    %-13d %d" % tuple(concat))
print("recurr.    %-13d %d" % tuple(recurr))
`} />

The bag of words returns the same vector for both sentences, coordinate by coordinate, because it
adds and addition has no order. That's the input that project's classifier received, and it's why
it gave both of them $0.0002$.

The states tell the reading step by step, and the table splits in two. The first four rows match
digit for digit, because up to there both sentences say the same thing, <W>the film is not</W>, and
the network has nothing to tell apart yet. At the fifth they part: it's the position where
<W>is not</W> lands on <W>boring</W> in one sentence and on <W>fun</W> in the other. That project
concluded that the coordinate of <W>not</W> recorded how many times it appeared, but not what it
landed on. Here the <W>not</W> travels inside the state that reaches position 5, and the network
reads the adjective with it in front.

The final states end up at $(-0.828,\ -0.077,\ -0.810)$ and $(-0.715,\ 0.167,\ -0.671)$. With
untrained weights, how far apart they are means nothing yet, and neither of them means "positive"
yet. What the result does guarantee is that the output layer will receive two different vectors,
which is the condition without which no training could give them different probabilities. With the
bag, that condition had already failed before anything started. The recurrence has kept the good
part of both representations the block has been comparing: it tells order apart, like
concatenation, and it shares its weights, like the bag.

The last two lines put the price in numbers, with the previous lesson's dimensions:
$d_{\text{model}} = 100$ and a layer (or a state) of $128$ coordinates. Going from reading sentences
to reading paragraphs multiplies the concatenated MLP's first layer by ten and leaves the recurrence
exactly where it was, at $29\,312$ weights. The $3\,810\,688$ the MLP adds all start at their
initialisation, and training has to adjust them with the same corpus as before.

The network can read now, and it can't learn yet. Everything above is the forward pass: weights set
by a generator, not a single gradient, not one descent step. And the gradient is where sharing the
weights stops coming free. In a network from the previous block each matrix appeared once on the
path from the input to the loss, and that's what made its gradient a single product;
$\mathbf{W}_{hh}$ appears $T$ times.

What to do with those $T$ paths (add them up, and walk the whole sequence backwards to get them) is
<Leccion slug="bptt">the next lesson, on backpropagation through time (BPTT)</Leccion>. It's the
previous block's chain rule without any variation, applied to a graph that instead of being $L$
