A vector decides nothing on its own. Eight lessons of tokenising, counting and fitting have turned the
text into coordinates, and that settles the input side: what is missing is the object that reads those
coordinates and decides. Of that object you know only the type ($f : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^k$,
written in <Leccion slug="texto-como-numeros">the lesson on the problem of representing language</Leccion>
and parked right there), and a type, on its own, computes nothing. This lesson builds the unit that $f$
is made of inside, and it turns out that a single one already decides.

Put a case on the table. Ten reviews of a movie, one line each, and a vocabulary of eight entries;
each review is its bag of words, the vector that counts how many times each entry appears, the same as in
<Leccion slug="bolsa-de-palabras">the lesson on the bag of words</Leccion>. So <W>good movie i recommend it</W>
and <W>bad movie very slow</W> are two points of $\mathbb{R}^{8}$, and from each you want the same thing:
whether the review speaks well or badly of the movie. Two possible answers.

Each of those ten reviews is an **example**: a vector that goes in, and beside it the answer you want
out. That answer is its **label**, a number $y$ that here is $1$ if the review speaks well and $0$ if it
speaks badly. A person set all ten by reading them (they come from no calculation), and without them
there would be no way to say an answer is wrong. Between the input and the answer, for now, there is
nothing.

The shortest thing that fits in between is a vote. Each coordinate of the review's vector counts for or
against depending on its value, and how strongly it counts is fixed by a number tied to that coordinate.
Add up every contribution, out comes a single number, and that number is compared against a threshold:
above it, one answer; below it, the other. There the object ends. There is nothing more.

That sum has a geometric reading worth seeing before writing it down. With two coordinates per point,
which is what fits in a drawing, the set of points where the sum equals the threshold exactly is a line,
and what the object does is look at which side of the line you fell on.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
A neuron compares the input with nothing stored: it splits the space in two with a flat surface and looks
at which side you fell on. Everything it can ever tell apart is in where it puts that surface.
</Callout>

## What a neuron computes

Fix the input: a vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$, a column, like every vector in the course.
A **neuron** is made of two things: a vector of **weights** $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$, one per
coordinate of the input, and a scalar $b \in \mathbb{R}$ called the **bias**. With them it computes a
number,

$$
z = \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b = \sum_{i=1}^{d} w_i x_i + b,
$$

which is the **pre-activation**, and to that number it applies an **activation function** $\varphi$, whose
result

$$
a = \varphi(z)
$$

is what the neuron returns. Both are scalars: a neuron, whatever vector it looks at, returns one number.
The type parked at the start of the course asked for $k$ output numbers, and one is enough to decide
between two answers; the other $k - 1$ come from placing more neurons alongside, each with its own
weights, and that comes later in this block.

$\varphi$ is still to be chosen, and here I fix it by decree with the oldest of them all, the step:

$$
\text{step}(z) = \begin{cases}
  1 & \text{if } z \geq 0 \\
  0 & \text{if } z < 0
\end{cases}.
$$

With that the neuron returns a $1$ or a $0$, and nothing in between. A neuron with a step has borne a name
of its own since 1958, and it is the one that gives this block its title: the **perceptron**. That there
are other candidates, and that the choice matters, you will see shortly; the step is here because it is the
one that leaves the geometry in plain view.

It is worth reading $\mathbf{w}$ slowly when the input is a bag of words, because then each coordinate of
$\mathbf{x}$ counts occurrences of a vocabulary entry and each weight is the neuron's opinion of that
entry: one number, positive or negative, and only one. Since $z$ is a sum, those opinions pile up without
getting in each other's way (two occurrences of <W>good</W> contribute twice what one does), and the order
of the review plays no part anywhere, because it played none in the representation either. What the neuron
adds on top of that bag is the verdict; what the bag had already lost stays lost.

The bias is not the opinion of any word: it is what the neuron thinks before reading anything. It fixes
where the threshold falls, because comparing $z$ with zero is comparing the sum of the opinions with $-b$.
And in the drawing, it is what lets the line not have to pass through the origin.

The term neuron is borrowed from biology and is best not stretched. A nerve cell integrates signals and
fires once it passes a certain threshold, and there the resemblance ends. What you have read is a weighted
sum followed by a comparison, and that is all there is to defend.

### The boundary that separates, and how far from it you fall

$\varphi$ does not look at $z$, it looks at its sign. So everything the neuron tells apart is contained in
the set of points where $z$ changes sign, its **decision boundary**:

$$
\{\, \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d} \;:\; \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b = 0 \,\}.
$$

With $d = 2$ and $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2)^{\top}$ that equation solves out,

$$
x_2 = -\frac{w_1 x_1 + b}{w_2},
$$

and it is the line of the explorable above, as long as $w_2 \neq 0$; if it is zero the line is vertical and
the original equation describes it equally well. In $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ the same set is a plane, and in
$\mathbb{R}^{d}$ a **hyperplane**: an object of dimension $d - 1$ that splits the space into two halves.
With the reviews below $d = 8$, and with a real vocabulary it would be tens of thousands; the hyperplane is
not drawn, but it is the same.

The weights do not only place that boundary, they are perpendicular to it. Take any two points $\mathbf{x}$
and $\mathbf{x}^{\prime}$ that lie on it. Both make the equation zero, so subtracting one from the other
cancels the bias and leaves

$$
\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}^{\prime} = \mathbf{w}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right) = 0,
$$

where $\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}^{\prime}$ is any direction lying in the boundary. The dot product of
$\mathbf{w}$ with all of them is zero: $\mathbf{w}$ points perpendicular to the boundary, towards the side
on which $z$ is positive.

And the bias shifts it. With $b = 0$ the zero vector satisfies the equation, so the boundary passes through
the origin and cannot pass anywhere else; as soon as $b \neq 0$ it comes away from it. Without a bias, the
answer depends only on the direction of $\mathbf{x}$; with it, also on where $\mathbf{x}$ is.

What is missing is what makes $z$ worth more than its sign. The distance of any point to the boundary,
signed (positive on one side, negative on the other), is

$$
\frac{\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b}{\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert} = \frac{z}{\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert},
$$

so that $z$ is that distance multiplied by $\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert$. Its magnitude says how much margin
the decision has, and the step keeps the sign and throws the rest away.

<Details summary="The signed distance, step by step">

Let $\mathbf{x}$ be any point and $\mathbf{p}$ the point of the boundary closest to it. The segment joining
them is perpendicular to the boundary, and the perpendicular direction we saw a moment ago: it is that of
$\mathbf{w}$. Write then

$$
\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{p} + t\,\frac{\mathbf{w}}{\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert},
$$

where $\mathbf{w} / \lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert$ has length $1$ and $t \in \mathbb{R}$ is therefore the signed
distance we are after. Multiplying both sides by $\mathbf{w}^{\top}$ and adding $b$,

$$
\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b = \underbrace{\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{p} + b}_{= \,0} + \; t\,\frac{\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{w}}{\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert}.
$$

The first summand on the right is zero because $\mathbf{p}$ is on the boundary, which is what being on it
means. In the second, $\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{w} = \lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert^{2}$ by definition of the norm,
so it comes down to $t \, \lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert$. The left side is $z$, so $z = t \, \lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert$
and

$$
t = \frac{z}{\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert}.
$$

</Details>

## A neuron that scores reviews in NumPy

The cell builds the case from the start: ten reviews, eight entries, the bag of words of each and a neuron
on top. I set the weights by hand ($+1$ for the entries that count in favour, $-1$ for those that count
against, $0$ for those with no opinion), because in this lesson there is nothing yet to choose them. Run it
and look at three things: the column of $z$, what happens when you multiply the weights by ten, and what
happens when you move the bias half a point.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

# --- Ten one-line reviews, with what a person would say about each.
reviews = [
    ("the movie is fun and i recommend it", 1),
    ("the movie is slow and boring", 0),
    ("good movie i recommend it", 1),
    ("bad movie very slow", 0),
    ("fun and good", 1),
    ("the movie is bad", 0),
    ("boring and slow", 0),
    ("i recommend it", 1),
    ("good movie but slow", 0),
    ("fun and i recommend it", 1),
]

# Eight entries. Anything not in the vocabulary never reaches the vector.
V = ["bad", "boring", "fun", "good", "movie", "recommend", "slow", "the"]
pos = {e: i for i, e in enumerate(V)}

X = np.zeros((len(reviews), len(V)))
for f, (text, _) in enumerate(reviews):
    for token in text.split():
        if token in pos:
            X[f, pos[token]] += 1.0
y = np.array([label for _, label in reviews])

print("X has shape", X.shape, "with one row per review and", len(V), "columns")
print("   ", " ".join(e.rjust(10) for e in V))
print("x1: ", " ".join(str(int(v)).rjust(10) for v in X[0]))
print()

# I set the weights by hand: +1 counts in favour, -1 counts against, 0 no opinion.
w = np.array([-1.0, -1.0, 1.0, 1.0, 0.0, 1.0, -1.0, 0.0])
b = 0.0
print("w:  ", " ".join(("%+d" % v).rjust(10) for v in w), "   b =", b)
print()


def decide(X, w, b):
    z = X @ w + b
    return z, (z >= 0).astype(int)        # step: 1 if z >= 0, else 0


z, a = decide(X, w, b)
print("review".ljust(37), "z".rjust(5), " decision".ljust(12), "label")
for f, (text, label) in enumerate(reviews):
    print(text.ljust(37), ("%.1f" % z[f]).rjust(5),
          (" positive" if a[f] else " negative").ljust(12),
          "positive" if label else "negative")
print()
print("correct:", int((a == y).sum()), "of", len(y))
print()

# 1. Scaling (w, b) by ten scales every z and moves not a single decision.
z10, a10 = decide(X, 10 * w, 10 * b)
print("with (10w, 10b)  z =", np.round(z10, 1))
print("                 same decisions:", bool(np.array_equal(a, a10)))
print()

# 2. The bias slides the boundary: the review that sat right on it flips sides.
z05, a05 = decide(X, w, -0.5)
changed = [reviews[f][0] for f in np.nonzero(a != a05)[0]]
print("with b = -0.5    flips sides:", changed)
print("                 correct:", int((a05 == y).sum()), "of", len(y))
print()

# 3. How far from the boundary each review falls: z over the norm of w.
norm = float(np.linalg.norm(w))
print("norm of w =", round(norm, 3))
for f in [0, 5, 8]:
    print("  ", reviews[f][0].ljust(37), "distance", round(float(z[f]) / norm, 3))
`} />

Nine correct out of ten, and the miss is where it had to be. <W>good movie but slow</W> adds $+1$ for
<W>good</W> and $-1$ for <W>slow</W>, so its $z$ is exactly $0$ and falls right on the boundary; the step
resolves the tie upward and sends it to the positive side, and the label said negative.

The next two experiments are the subject of the lesson. Multiplying $\mathbf{w}$ and $b$ by ten multiplies
the ten pre-activations by ten ($2.0$ becomes $20$, $-1.0$ becomes $-10$) and changes not a single decision:
the boundary is the set where $z$ is zero, and which points are zero does not depend on the scale. Moving the
bias to $-0.5$, on the other hand, slides the boundary half a point, the review that sat right on it falls to
the other side, and the correct count rises to ten out of ten.

The last lines print the distance. With $\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert = 2.449$, the first review falls $0.816$
from the boundary and <W>the movie is bad</W> at $-0.408$: half as far, on the other side. That difference
exists in $z$, and it does not reach $a$.

Two things are left open, and they are not equally pressing. I wrote the eight weights above by looking at
the reviews, and that cannot be repeated with a vocabulary of fifty thousand entries: getting the neuron to
find them on its own, from examples, takes up much of what is left of the block. The other is nearer. The
geometry gave the magnitude of $z$ a meaning (the distance to the boundary, scaled by $\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert$),
and the step throws it away whole: to the review that falls at $0.816$ and to the one that falls grazing the
boundary it hands the same $1$.

$\varphi$ was fixed by decree, and there are many more functions to choose from. Some keep the distance
instead of crushing it; others change at the root what can be computed when several neurons are stacked.
Which one to assign to $\varphi$, and what is gained and lost with each, is <Leccion slug="funciones-activacion">the
next lesson, on activation functions and non-linearity</Leccion>.
