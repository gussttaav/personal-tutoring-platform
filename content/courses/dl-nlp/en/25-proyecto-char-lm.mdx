{/* COURSE-P5-03: Block 3, Lesson 7. COURSE-P11-06: English translation. */}

The block now has a recurrent neural network (RNN) that reads texts of any length, a gradient that
runs back through it in time, and two gated cells, the long short-term memory (LSTM) and the gated
recurrent unit (GRU), that give it a memory able to survive distance. This lesson gives that
machinery a job: building a model that, having read a text, can carry on writing it.

That model has a name: a **language model** is a network that, given a stretch of text, predicts
what comes next. **At the character level** (the tokenisation by characters of <Leccion slug="tokenizacion">the lesson on tokenisation</Leccion>) that means the next character, a letter, a space or a full stop,
and the whole task fits in that tiny question. It trains on a small corpus, a few thousand
characters, and is then asked to write, character by character, feeding itself what it produces. The
text that comes out is the network talking to itself.

The network we trained in <Leccion slug="bptt">the lesson on backpropagation through time (BPTT)</Leccion> read a whole review and gave a single verdict, one output and one
loss, hung from the last state. A language model changes one thing and one thing only: it makes a
prediction at *every* position. At step $t$, with the state $\mathbf{h}_t$ summarising what has been
read so far, it predicts character $t+1$; so a text of $T$ characters is $T$ predictions in a single
pass, and its loss adds up all $T$. That is what turns a text into a training set without anyone
labelling it: the right answer at each position is the character at the next one, already written in
the corpus.

The network is, on purpose, the simplest in the block: the vanilla RNN of <Leccion slug="la-rnn-vanilla">its own lesson</Leccion>, trained with
BPTT as it stands, and not the LSTM or the GRU of the two previous lessons. What is new here is the
task, predicting the next character, and it's worth trying it out first on the cell whose backward
pass is already derived in two lines. With a gated cell the backward pass would also have to cross
every gate, and if something didn't make sense there would be no telling whether it came from the
task or from the cell. What gets built around it (one output per position, the loss that adds them
up, the sampling to write) works the same for any of the three cells.

The honest thing is to say up front what it learns and what it doesn't. With a few thousand
characters and a state of a few dozen coordinates, the network learns the statistics of the
characters: where the spaces fall, that <W>t</W> is very often followed by <W>h</W>, that
<W>the</W>, <W>and</W> and <W>of</W> are everywhere. It learns nothing about meaning or grammar. So
what it generates looks like English heard from the far end of a noisy room: real short words,
plausible long words that don't exist, and not one sentence that tries to say anything. That isn't a
defect this lesson has come to fix: it is what a character model of this size learns, and naming it
outright is half the lesson.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
A character-level language model predicts the next character at every position: a text of $T$
characters is $T$ examples stacked in one sequence, and its label at each step is, for free, the
character that comes next. Generating inverts that: pick a character according to the probabilities
the network gives, feed it back, and repeat.
</Callout>

## One output at every position

The recurrence is the one from <Leccion slug="la-rnn-vanilla">the lesson on the vanilla RNN</Leccion>, without changing a letter:

$$
\mathbf{h}_t = \tanh\left(\mathbf{W}_{xh}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{W}_{hh}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}_h\right),
$$

with $\mathbf{h}_0 = \mathbf{0}$. What is new is $\mathbf{x}_t$: since every token is a character, the
input at position $t$ is the one-hot vector of that character,
$\mathbf{x}_t = \mathbf{o}_{c_t} \in \{0,1\}^{\lvert V \rvert}$, where $V$ is the set of distinct
characters in the corpus. The vocabulary is no longer words but characters, so
$d_{\text{model}} = \lvert V \rvert$ and $\mathbf{W}_{xh} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h \times \lvert V \rvert}$.

That one-hot vector brings the shortcut from <Leccion slug="one-hot">the lesson on one-hot encoding</Leccion>: multiplying a matrix by a
one-hot vector picks out one of its columns, $\mathbf{W}_{xh}\mathbf{o}_{c} =$ column $c$ of
$\mathbf{W}_{xh}$. So the columns of $\mathbf{W}_{xh}$ are the input vectors of each character, which
the network learns as it goes.

What is new is the output. <Leccion slug="la-rnn-vanilla">The lesson on the vanilla RNN</Leccion> read a verdict from the last state; the language model reads one
from *every* state, and it is a distribution over the next character:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t = \text{softmax}\left(\mathbf{W}_{hy}\mathbf{h}_t + \mathbf{b}_y\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{\lvert V \rvert},
$$

with $\mathbf{W}_{hy} \in \mathbb{R}^{\lvert V \rvert \times d_h}$, one row for each character that
might come. The label is the one the corpus actually put there: the one-hot vector of the next
character, $\mathbf{y}_t = \mathbf{o}_{c_{t+1}}$. The loss at that position is the cross-entropy of <Leccion slug="funcion-de-perdida">the lesson on loss functions</Leccion>, and that of the whole sequence adds one per position,
averaged per character:

$$
\ell = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} -\log \hat{y}_{t,\,c_{t+1}},
$$

where $\hat{y}_{t,\,c_{t+1}}$ is the probability the network gave to the character that actually came.
The $1/T$ is the mean per position, the counterpart of the previous block's $1/B$ sharing
$\mathcal{L}$ out over the batch (except that here the "batch" is the $T$ positions of a single
sequence).

## The backward pass: every step receives two errors

<Leccion slug="bptt">The lesson on BPTT</Leccion> already did almost all of this calculation. There only the last state touched the
loss, so only $\boldsymbol{\delta}_T$ started from an output and the interior steps received their
error transported from the next one. The language model changes exactly that: every position has its
own output, so every step starts an error of its own *and* also passes back the one coming from ahead.

It is still $\boldsymbol{\delta}_t = \nabla_{\mathbf{p}_t}\ell$, the error of the pre-activation. What
changes is that the state $\mathbf{h}_t$ now reaches the loss along two paths (its output at $t$ and
step $t+1$), and <Leccion slug="regla-de-la-cadena">the chain rule</Leccion> from the previous block adds them:

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{h}_t}\ell = \mathbf{W}_{hy}^{\top}\left(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t - \mathbf{y}_t\right) + \mathbf{W}_{hh}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{t+1},
$$

where the first term is the output from <Leccion slug="funcion-de-perdida">the lesson on loss functions</Leccion> (softmax with cross-entropy
delivers $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t - \mathbf{y}_t$) shared out among the coordinates of the state by
$\mathbf{W}_{hy}^{\top}$, and the second is the transport from <Leccion slug="bptt">the lesson on BPTT</Leccion>, untouched. Mask with the
slope of the $\tanh$ and you have the error of the step:

$$
\boldsymbol{\delta}_t = \left(\mathbf{W}_{hy}^{\top}\left(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t - \mathbf{y}_t\right) + \mathbf{W}_{hh}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{t+1}\right) \odot \left(\mathbf{1} - \mathbf{h}_t \odot \mathbf{h}_t\right),
$$

with $\boldsymbol{\delta}_{T+1} = \mathbf{0}$, because after the last step there is none. Put it next
to the two lines of <Leccion slug="bptt">the lesson on BPTT</Leccion> and you'll see they are its two halves together: the start from the
output, which there belonged to $\boldsymbol{\delta}_T$ alone, and the transport, which there belonged
to the interior steps alone.

With the error of each step in hand, the gradients are those of <Leccion slug="bptt">the lesson on BPTT</Leccion> plus that of the output
layer, all summed over the $T$ positions:

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{hh}}\ell = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \boldsymbol{\delta}_t\mathbf{h}_{t-1}^{\top},
\qquad
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{xh}}\ell = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \boldsymbol{\delta}_t\mathbf{x}_t^{\top},
\qquad
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{hy}}\ell = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \left(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t - \mathbf{y}_t\right)\mathbf{h}_t^{\top},
$$

and the biases likewise, summed. Everything under the sum is a piece already derived; the only new
thing is that $\mathbf{W}_{hy}$ is also shared across positions (one output per step, not one at the
end), so its gradient joins the sum over time instead of being a lone product.

<Details summary="See why nearby statistics are learnt and distant ones aren't">
The transport still multiplies by $\mathbf{W}_{hh}^{\top}$ once per step, so the vanishing of <Leccion slug="gradiente-desvanecido">the lesson on the vanishing gradient</Leccion> still stands: an error that has to travel many steps back arrives faded.
The difference from the classifier is that now every position injects a fresh error from its own
output, before it has crossed any $\mathbf{W}_{hh}$. That's why the model learns what is nearby well
and what is distant escapes it: the signal that ties a character to its neighbour doesn't travel, and
the one that would tie it to something twenty positions back fades on the way. It is the frontier
that motivated the gated cells, seen from the text they produce.
</Details>

## Training and generating in NumPy

Three cells: the corpus, the training and the writing. Run them in order.

The first fetches the corpus (a few paragraphs about a seaside village, written for the course),
lowercases it, strips everything that isn't a letter, a space, a full stop or a comma, and leaves it
as a string of indices. Look at the vocabulary that remains, and at the last line, which shows the
whole model in two words: what goes **in** is the text, and what comes **out** is the same text
shifted by one position.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`

Out come 2,796 characters and 26 distinct ones (23 letters, since no <W>j</W>, <W>x</W> or <W>z</W>
appears, plus the space and two marks), so $\lvert V \rvert = 26$: the same number as the alphabet,
and not the same set. The last two lines are the whole training set: <W>the village</W> and a space
as input and <W>he village s</W> as label, the same string moved one place along. That is where the
almost 2,796 examples come from, one per position, with nothing labelled.

The second is the complete model: the forward pass of <Leccion slug="la-rnn-vanilla">the lesson on the vanilla RNN</Leccion>, which at every position produces one score per
character that the softmax turns into probabilities; the backward pass of <Leccion slug="bptt">the lesson on BPTT</Leccion> with the output's
error added at every step; the gradient clipping of <Leccion slug="gradiente-desvanecido">the lesson on the vanishing gradient</Leccion>; and a descent that updates the five groups of
weights. It trains for 1,200 steps on chunks of 25 characters, and each chunk is an independent
sequence that starts at $\mathbf{h}_0 = \mathbf{0}$, as in <Leccion slug="bptt">the lesson on BPTT</Leccion>. Look at the two things it prints every
few hundred steps: the loss per character, and a sample of what the network would write at that
point.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

# Needs the previous cell: data, V, to_ix, n_v.
d_h, seq = 64, 25                                   # width of the state and length of each chunk
eta, kappa, iters = 0.3, 5.0, 1200

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)                      # fixed seed: you'll see these numbers
Wxh = rng.normal(size=(d_h, n_v)) * 0.01            # (d_h, n_v): column c = vector of character c
Whh = rng.normal(size=(d_h, d_h)) * 0.01            # (d_h, d_h): the vanilla RNN's recurrence
Why = rng.normal(size=(n_v, d_h)) * 0.01            # (n_v, d_h): from the state to one score per character
bh, by = np.zeros(d_h), np.zeros(n_v)


def forward(inp, tgt):
    H, h = np.zeros((d_h, len(inp))), np.zeros(d_h)
    for t in range(len(inp)):
        h = np.tanh(Wxh[:, inp[t]] + Whh @ h + bh)  # the recurrence step by step (Wxh @ one-hot = column)
        H[:, t] = h
    O = Why @ H + by[:, None]                        # (n_v, seq): one column of scores per position
    O -= O.max(0); P = np.exp(O); P /= P.sum(0)      # softmax in every column
    loss = -np.log(P[tgt, np.arange(len(inp))]).mean()   # cross-entropy, mean per character
    return H, P, loss


def backward(inp, tgt, H, P):
    T = len(inp)
    dO = P.copy(); dO[tgt, np.arange(T)] -= 1.0      # (y_hat - y) at every position
    gWhy = dO @ H.T; gby = dO.sum(1); dHy = Why.T @ dO   # output layer, summed over time
    gWxh, gWhh, gbh = np.zeros_like(Wxh), np.zeros_like(Whh), np.zeros_like(bh)
    dh_next = np.zeros(d_h)
    for t in reversed(range(T)):
        dp = (1 - H[:, t] ** 2) * (dHy[:, t] + dh_next)  # delta_t: the error from the output at t and from step t+1
        h_prev = H[:, t - 1] if t > 0 else np.zeros(d_h)
        gWhh += np.outer(dp, h_prev); gWxh[:, inp[t]] += dp; gbh += dp
        dh_next = Whh.T @ dp                         # transports the error to t-1, as in BPTT
    return [g / T for g in (gWxh, gWhh, gWhy, gbh, gby)]


def generate(n, seed):
    g = np.random.default_rng(seed); h = np.zeros(d_h); ix = to_ix[" "]; out = []
    for _ in range(n):
        h = np.tanh(Wxh[:, ix] + Whh @ h + bh)
        o = Why @ h + by; o -= o.max(); p = np.exp(o); p /= p.sum()
        ix = g.choice(n_v, p=p); out.append(ix)     # samples the next one and feeds it back
    return "".join(V[i] for i in out)


j, smooth = 0, None
for it in range(iters):
    if j + seq + 1 >= len(data): j = 0
    inp, tgt = data[j:j + seq], data[j + 1:j + seq + 1]; j += seq
    H, P, loss = forward(inp, tgt)
    grads = backward(inp, tgt, H, P)
    for g in grads:
        nrm = np.linalg.norm(g)
        if nrm > kappa: g *= kappa / nrm            # gradient clipping
    for Pm, g in zip([Wxh, Whh, Why, bh, by], grads):
        Pm -= eta * g
    smooth = loss if smooth is None else 0.999 * smooth + 0.001 * loss
    if it % 300 == 0 or it == iters - 1:
        print("iter %4d   loss/char %.3f   \\"%s\\"" % (it, smooth, generate(48, 0)))
`} />

The loss starts at $3.26$, which is no accident: it is $\ln 26$, the loss of a network that
spreads its bets evenly over the 26 characters (the echo of the $\log 2$ of the previous block's
classifier, with 26 faces instead of 2). From there it falls without a jolt to around $2.5$, and it
would keep falling: the loop is cut short to fit in the ten seconds the browser gives a cell, because
mediocre and on time beats good and never. The sample on each line tells the same story from another
side: the first is noise, characters at random; by the middle there are spaces in their place and
short words; by the end it is broken English: real short words (<W>of</W>, <W>the</W>, <W>age</W>)
threaded between long words that sound like English without being it.

The cutting into chunks has a second price: since every chunk starts from zero, the network never
sees a dependency longer than 25 characters. For a model that only chases local statistics it is a
price worth paying; and the long dependency given up is exactly the one the gated cells of
<Leccion slug="lstm">the LSTM</Leccion> and <Leccion slug="gru">the GRU</Leccion> knew how to keep.

The third cell uses the trained weights to write. Generating inverts training: instead of the real
character that came next, the network receives the one it proposes itself. It starts at
$\mathbf{h}_0 = \mathbf{0}$ with any character (a space, to begin a word), computes
$\hat{\mathbf{y}}$, **samples** the next one from that distribution (draws one at random with the
probability it is given), feeds it back as input, and repeats. The cell generates twice by sampling
and a third time by always taking the most probable character, to see the difference.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

# Needs the two previous cells: the trained weights, generate, V, to_ix, d_h.
print("SAMPLING (varies with the seed):")
for s in [0, 1]:
    print("  \\"%s\\"" % generate(200, s))


def generate_greedy(n):                             # always the most probable: deterministic
    h = np.zeros(d_h); ix = to_ix[" "]; out = []
    for _ in range(n):
        h = np.tanh(Wxh[:, ix] + Whh @ h + bh)
        o = Why @ h + by; o -= o.max(); p = np.exp(o); p /= p.sum()
        ix = int(p.argmax()); out.append(ix)        # the most probable, with no chance
    return "".join(V[i] for i in out)


print("\\nALWAYS THE MOST PROBABLE (deterministic):")
print("  \\"%s\\"" % generate_greedy(200))
`} />

The first two lines come out different from each other (the chance in the sampling) and both are
broken English. The last one doesn't: always taking the most probable character is deterministic,
and as soon as it falls into a high-probability run it gets stuck repeating the same fragment over
and over, here <W>she</W>. That's why it samples instead of playing safe: chance is what stops the
network from chasing its own tail. And even so, neither of the two writes anything that means
something. It can't: it never saw a word as a unit, only characters, and it forgot anything more than
25 back. It is exactly what was promised, that it would come out bad, and watching it come out is
the lesson.

---

For the first time in the course, a network has written. It is little and it is bad, but it is its
own: none of the characters in those lines was in the corpus in that order, and still they look like
English because the network learnt, letter by letter, what looking like English consists of. All the
machinery of the block (reading any length, training through time, repairing the path the gradient
comes back along) was there to get this far and switch something on.

But this model does one thing: carry on a string of text in the same language it read it in. It
