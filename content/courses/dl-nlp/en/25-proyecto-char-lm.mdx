{/* COURSE-P5-03: Block 3, Lesson 7. COURSE-P11-06: English translation. */}

By now the block has a recurrent neural network (RNN) that reads texts of any length, a gradient
that runs back through it in time, and two gated cells, the long short-term memory (LSTM) and the
gated recurrent unit (GRU), that give it a memory able to survive distance. This lesson puts that
machinery to work: building a model that, having read a text, can carry on writing it.

That kind of model has a name: a **language model** is a network that, given a stretch of text,
predicts what comes next. *At the character level* (character tokenisation, from <Leccion slug="tokenizacion">the lesson on tokenisation</Leccion>), that means the next character, whether a letter, a space or a full
stop, and the whole task fits into that tiny question. It trains on a small corpus of a few thousand
characters and is then asked to write, character by character, feeding itself what it produces. The
text that comes out is the network talking to itself.

The network in <Leccion slug="bptt">the lesson on backpropagation through time (BPTT)</Leccion> read a whole review and gave a single verdict:
one output and one loss, hanging off the last state. A language model changes one thing and one thing
only: it makes a prediction at *every* position. At step $t$, with the state $\mathbf{h}_t$
summarising everything read so far, it predicts character $t+1$; so a text of $T$ characters means
$T$ predictions in a single pass, and its loss adds up all $T$ of them. That's what turns a text into
a training set without anyone labelling it: the right answer at each position is the character at
the next one, already written in the corpus.

The network is deliberately the simplest one in the block: the vanilla RNN from <Leccion slug="la-rnn-vanilla">its own lesson</Leccion>, trained
with BPTT as it stands, rather than the LSTM or GRU from the last two lessons. What's new here is the
task, predicting the next character, and it's worth trying it out first on the cell whose backward
pass has already been derived in two lines. With a gated cell, the backward pass would also have to
cross every gate, and if something didn't add up, there'd be no telling whether the problem lay in
the task or in the cell. What gets built around it (one output per position, the loss that adds them
up, the sampling for writing) works the same for any of the three cells.

The honest thing is to say up front what it learns and what it doesn't. With a few thousand
characters and a state of a few dozen coordinates, the network learns character statistics: where the
spaces fall, that <W>t</W> is very often followed by <W>h</W>, that <W>the</W>, <W>and</W> and
<W>of</W> are everywhere. It learns nothing about meaning or grammar. So what it generates sounds like
English overheard from the far end of a noisy room: real short words, plausible long words that don't
exist, and not a single sentence that tries to say anything. That isn't a defect this lesson sets out
to fix: it's what a character model of this size learns, and naming it outright is half the lesson.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
A character-level language model predicts the next character at every position: a text of $T$
characters is $T$ examples stacked into one sequence, and the label at each step comes for free,
since it's the character that comes next. Generating runs this in reverse: pick a character according
to the probabilities the network gives, feed it back in, and repeat.
</Callout>

## One output at every position

The recurrence is the one from <Leccion slug="la-rnn-vanilla">the lesson on the vanilla RNN</Leccion>, without a single letter changed:

$$
\mathbf{h}_t = \tanh\left(\mathbf{W}_{xh}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{W}_{hh}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}_h\right),
$$

with $\mathbf{h}_0 = \mathbf{0}$. The first change is $\mathbf{x}_t$: since every token is a
character, the input at position $t$ is that character's one-hot vector,
$\mathbf{x}_t = \mathbf{o}_{c_t} \in \{0,1\}^{\lvert V \rvert}$, where $V$ is the set of distinct
characters in the corpus. The vocabulary is made of characters now, not words, so
$d_{\text{model}} = \lvert V \rvert$ and $\mathbf{W}_{xh} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h \times \lvert V \rvert}$.

That one-hot vector brings with it the shortcut from <Leccion slug="one-hot">the lesson on one-hot encoding</Leccion>: multiplying a matrix
by a one-hot vector picks out one of its columns, $\mathbf{W}_{xh}\mathbf{o}_{c} =$ column $c$ of
$\mathbf{W}_{xh}$. So the columns of $\mathbf{W}_{xh}$ are the input vectors for each character,
which the network learns as it goes.

The bigger change is the output. <Leccion slug="la-rnn-vanilla">The lesson on the vanilla RNN</Leccion> read a verdict off the last state; the language model reads one
off *every* state, and it's a distribution over the next character:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t = \text{softmax}\left(\mathbf{W}_{hy}\mathbf{h}_t + \mathbf{b}_y\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{\lvert V \rvert},
$$

with $\mathbf{W}_{hy} \in \mathbb{R}^{\lvert V \rvert \times d_h}$, one row for each character that
might come next. The label is whatever the corpus actually put there: the one-hot vector of the next
character, $\mathbf{y}_t = \mathbf{o}_{c_{t+1}}$. The loss at that position is the cross-entropy from <Leccion slug="funcion-de-perdida">the lesson on loss functions</Leccion>, and the loss of the whole sequence adds one per
position, averaged per character:

$$
\ell = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} -\log \hat{y}_{t,\,c_{t+1}},
$$

where $\hat{y}_{t,\,c_{t+1}}$ is the probability the network gave to the character that actually
came. The $1/T$ takes the mean per position, the counterpart of the previous block's $1/B$ spreading
$\mathcal{L}$ over the batch (except that here the "batch" is the $T$ positions of a single
sequence).

## The backward pass: every step receives two errors

<Leccion slug="bptt">The lesson on BPTT</Leccion> already did almost all of this calculation. There, only the last state touched the
loss, so only $\boldsymbol{\delta}_T$ started from an output, and the interior steps received their
error transported back from the next step. The language model changes exactly that: every position
has its own output, so every step starts an error of its own *and* passes back the one arriving from
further ahead.

It's still $\boldsymbol{\delta}_t = \nabla_{\mathbf{p}_t}\ell$, the error at the pre-activation. What
changes is that the state $\mathbf{h}_t$ now reaches the loss along two paths (its own output at $t$,
and step $t+1$), and <Leccion slug="regla-de-la-cadena">the chain rule</Leccion> from the previous block adds them:

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{h}_t}\ell = \mathbf{W}_{hy}^{\top}\left(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t - \mathbf{y}_t\right) + \mathbf{W}_{hh}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{t+1},
$$

where the first term is the output error from <Leccion slug="funcion-de-perdida">the lesson on loss functions</Leccion> (softmax with cross-entropy
delivers $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t - \mathbf{y}_t$), distributed across the coordinates of the state by
$\mathbf{W}_{hy}^{\top}$, and the second is the transport from <Leccion slug="bptt">the lesson on BPTT</Leccion>, untouched. Mask with the
slope of the $\tanh$ and you have the step's error:

$$
\boldsymbol{\delta}_t = \left(\mathbf{W}_{hy}^{\top}\left(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t - \mathbf{y}_t\right) + \mathbf{W}_{hh}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{t+1}\right) \odot \left(\mathbf{1} - \mathbf{h}_t \odot \mathbf{h}_t\right),
$$

with $\boldsymbol{\delta}_{T+1} = \mathbf{0}$, because there's no step after the last one. Put it next
to the two equations from <Leccion slug="bptt">the lesson on BPTT</Leccion> and you'll see it's both of them joined together: the start from
the output, which there belonged only to $\boldsymbol{\delta}_T$, and the transport, which there
belonged only to the interior steps.

With each step's error in hand, the gradients are the ones from <Leccion slug="bptt">the lesson on BPTT</Leccion> plus the output layer's,
all summed over the $T$ positions:

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{hh}}\ell = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \boldsymbol{\delta}_t\mathbf{h}_{t-1}^{\top},
\qquad
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{xh}}\ell = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \boldsymbol{\delta}_t\mathbf{x}_t^{\top},
\qquad
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{hy}}\ell = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \left(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t - \mathbf{y}_t\right)\mathbf{h}_t^{\top},
$$

and likewise for the biases, summed. Everything inside the sums is a piece you've already derived;
the only new thing is that $\mathbf{W}_{hy}$ is now shared across positions too (one output per
step, not one at the end), so its gradient joins the sum over time instead of being a lone product.

<Details summary="See why nearby statistics get learned and distant ones don't">
The transport still multiplies by $\mathbf{W}_{hh}^{\top}$ once per step, so the vanishing from <Leccion slug="gradiente-desvanecido">the lesson on the vanishing gradient</Leccion> still applies: an error that has to travel many steps back arrives faded.
The difference from the classifier is that now every position injects a fresh error from its own
output, before it has crossed any $\mathbf{W}_{hh}$. That's why the model learns nearby patterns
well while distant ones escape it: the signal that ties a character to its neighbour doesn't have to
travel, and the one that would tie it to something twenty positions back fades along the way. It's
the same boundary that motivated the gated cells, seen this time through the text the network
produces.
</Details>

## Training and generating in NumPy

Three cells: the corpus, the training and the writing. Run them in order.

The first fetches the corpus (a few paragraphs about a seaside village, written for the course),
lowercases it, strips out everything that isn't a letter, a space, a full stop or a comma, and turns
it into a string of indices. Look at the vocabulary that's left, and at the last two lines, which sum
up the whole model: what goes *in* is the text, and what comes *out* is the same text shifted by one
position.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`

Out come 2,796 characters, 26 of them distinct (23 letters, since there's no <W>j</W>, <W>x</W> or
<W>z</W>, plus the space and two punctuation marks), so $\lvert V \rvert = 26$: the same number as
the alphabet, but not the same set. The last two lines are the entire training set in miniature:
<W>the village</W> plus a space as input, and <W>he village s</W> as label, the same string moved
one place along. That's where the almost 2,796 examples come from, one per position, with nothing
labelled by hand.

The second cell is the complete model: the forward pass from <Leccion slug="la-rnn-vanilla">the lesson on the vanilla RNN</Leccion>, which at every position produces one score per
character for the softmax to turn into probabilities; the backward pass from <Leccion slug="bptt">the lesson on BPTT</Leccion>, with the
output's error added at every step; the gradient clipping from <Leccion slug="gradiente-desvanecido">the lesson on the vanishing gradient</Leccion>; and a descent step that updates the five groups
of weights. It trains for 1,200 steps on 25-character chunks, each chunk an independent sequence
starting from $\mathbf{h}_0 = \mathbf{0}$, as in <Leccion slug="bptt">the lesson on BPTT</Leccion>. Watch the two things it prints every
few hundred steps: the loss per character, and a snippet of what the network would write at that
point.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

# Needs the previous cell: data, V, to_ix, n_v.
d_h, seq = 64, 25                                   # width of the state and length of each chunk
eta, kappa, iters = 0.3, 5.0, 1200

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)                      # fixed seed: you'll see these numbers
Wxh = rng.normal(size=(d_h, n_v)) * 0.01            # (d_h, n_v): column c = vector of character c
Whh = rng.normal(size=(d_h, d_h)) * 0.01            # (d_h, d_h): the vanilla RNN's recurrence
Why = rng.normal(size=(n_v, d_h)) * 0.01            # (n_v, d_h): from the state to one score per character
bh, by = np.zeros(d_h), np.zeros(n_v)


def forward(inp, tgt):
    H, h = np.zeros((d_h, len(inp))), np.zeros(d_h)
    for t in range(len(inp)):
        h = np.tanh(Wxh[:, inp[t]] + Whh @ h + bh)  # the recurrence step by step (Wxh @ one-hot = column)
        H[:, t] = h
    O = Why @ H + by[:, None]                        # (n_v, seq): one column of scores per position
    O -= O.max(0); P = np.exp(O); P /= P.sum(0)      # softmax in every column
    loss = -np.log(P[tgt, np.arange(len(inp))]).mean()   # cross-entropy, mean per character
    return H, P, loss


def backward(inp, tgt, H, P):
    T = len(inp)
    dO = P.copy(); dO[tgt, np.arange(T)] -= 1.0      # (y_hat - y) at every position
    gWhy = dO @ H.T; gby = dO.sum(1); dHy = Why.T @ dO   # output layer, summed over time
    gWxh, gWhh, gbh = np.zeros_like(Wxh), np.zeros_like(Whh), np.zeros_like(bh)
    dh_next = np.zeros(d_h)
    for t in reversed(range(T)):
        dp = (1 - H[:, t] ** 2) * (dHy[:, t] + dh_next)  # delta_t: the error from the output at t and from step t+1
        h_prev = H[:, t - 1] if t > 0 else np.zeros(d_h)
        gWhh += np.outer(dp, h_prev); gWxh[:, inp[t]] += dp; gbh += dp
        dh_next = Whh.T @ dp                         # transports the error to t-1, as in BPTT
    return [g / T for g in (gWxh, gWhh, gWhy, gbh, gby)]


def generate(n, seed):
    g = np.random.default_rng(seed); h = np.zeros(d_h); ix = to_ix[" "]; out = []
    for _ in range(n):
        h = np.tanh(Wxh[:, ix] + Whh @ h + bh)
        o = Why @ h + by; o -= o.max(); p = np.exp(o); p /= p.sum()
        ix = g.choice(n_v, p=p); out.append(ix)     # samples the next one and feeds it back
    return "".join(V[i] for i in out)


j, smooth = 0, None
for it in range(iters):
    if j + seq + 1 >= len(data): j = 0
    inp, tgt = data[j:j + seq], data[j + 1:j + seq + 1]; j += seq
    H, P, loss = forward(inp, tgt)
    grads = backward(inp, tgt, H, P)
    for g in grads:
        nrm = np.linalg.norm(g)
        if nrm > kappa: g *= kappa / nrm            # gradient clipping
    for Pm, g in zip([Wxh, Whh, Why, bh, by], grads):
        Pm -= eta * g
    smooth = loss if smooth is None else 0.999 * smooth + 0.001 * loss
    if it % 300 == 0 or it == iters - 1:
        print("iter %4d   loss/char %.3f   \\"%s\\"" % (it, smooth, generate(48, 0)))
`} />

The loss starts at $3.26$, and that's no accident: it's $\ln 26$, the loss of a network that spreads
its bets evenly across all 26 characters (an echo of the $\log 2$ from the previous block's
classifier, with a 26-sided die instead of a coin). From there it falls smoothly to around $2.5$,
and it would keep falling: the loop is cut short to fit within the ten seconds the browser allows a
cell, because mediocre and on time beats good and never. The snippet on each line tells the same
story from another angle: the first is noise, random characters; by the middle, there are spaces in
the right places and some short words; by the end, it's broken English, real short words
(<W>of</W>, <W>the</W>, <W>age</W>) threaded between long words that sound English without being
English.

Chopping the text into chunks has a second cost: since every chunk starts from zero, the network
never sees a dependency longer than 25 characters. For a model that only chases local statistics,
that's a price worth paying; and the long-range dependency it gives up is exactly the kind the gated
cells, <Leccion slug="lstm">the LSTM</Leccion> and <Leccion slug="gru">the GRU</Leccion>, knew how to
keep.

The third cell uses the trained weights to write. Generating turns training inside out: instead of
the real character that came next, the network receives the one it proposed itself. It starts at
$\mathbf{h}_0 = \mathbf{0}$ with any character (a space, to begin a word), computes
$\hat{\mathbf{y}}$, **samples** the next character from that distribution (draws one at random, each
with the probability the network gives it), feeds it back as input, and repeats. The cell generates
twice by sampling, and a third time by always taking the most probable character, so you can see the
difference.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

# Needs the two previous cells: the trained weights, generate, V, to_ix, d_h.
print("SAMPLING (varies with the seed):")
for s in [0, 1]:
    print("  \\"%s\\"" % generate(200, s))


def generate_greedy(n):                             # always the most probable: deterministic
    h = np.zeros(d_h); ix = to_ix[" "]; out = []
    for _ in range(n):
        h = np.tanh(Wxh[:, ix] + Whh @ h + bh)
        o = Why @ h + by; o -= o.max(); p = np.exp(o); p /= p.sum()
        ix = int(p.argmax()); out.append(ix)        # the most probable, with no chance
    return "".join(V[i] for i in out)


print("\\nALWAYS THE MOST PROBABLE (deterministic):")
print("  \\"%s\\"" % generate_greedy(200))
`} />

The first two lines differ from each other (that's the chance in the sampling), and both are broken
English. The third behaves differently: always taking the most probable character is deterministic,
and as soon as it falls into a high-probability run, it gets stuck repeating the same fragment over
and over, in this case <W>she</W>. That's why generation samples instead of playing it safe: chance
is what stops the network from chasing its own tail. Even so, neither of the sampled lines says
anything meaningful. It can't: the network never saw a word as a unit, only characters, and it forgot
anything more than 25 characters back. It's exactly what was promised, that the result would be bad,
and watching it turn out that way is the lesson.

For the first time in the course, a network has written something. It's not much, and it's not good,
but it's its own: none of those strings of characters appears in the corpus in that order, and still
they look like English, because the network learned, letter by letter, what looking like English

But this model does only one thing: continue a piece of text in the same language it read. It can't
take one sequence and return a different one (a question and its answer, a sentence in Spanish and
the same sentence in English), because it handles one sequence, not two. Reading a whole sequence
with one network and writing another with a second, tying them together through what the first one
understood, is the subject of <Leccion slug="seq2seq">the next lesson, on sequence-to-sequence models</Leccion>: the encoder that summarises what
was read and the decoder that writes from that summary, the last step before leaving the block.
