      def errors(Ws, zs, y_hat, y):
          deltas = [None] * len(Ws)
          deltas[-1] = np.asarray(y_hat, dtype=float) - np.asarray(y, dtype=float)
          for l in range(len(Ws) - 2, -1, -1):

{/* COURSE-P5-02 — Block 2, Lesson 8. */}

Everything backpropagation does, it does with the chain rule, and the chain rule is already
differentiated. The previous lesson left it in its vector form (Jacobians that multiply together, a
gradient that comes in at one end and leaves converted into the one of the layer before) and with
it the reason why, as it stands, it cannot be run: applying it parameter by parameter costs a
complete run over the network for every number there is to adjust. What is missing is not new
mathematics. It is an order.

And the order shows better small than large. Take the smallest network that still has a hidden
layer: two inputs, two hidden neurons and one output. It has nine parameters (four input weights,
two hidden biases, two output weights and one bias), so there are nine derivatives to compute.
Behind the nine there are three numbers, one per pre-activation of the network. With those three
computed, each of the nine derivatives is a product of two factors, and neither of the two has to be
fetched from far away.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
What travels backwards is not one derivative per weight: it is one number per neuron. Each weight
gets its own by multiplying its neuron's number by what that neuron had in front of it.
</Callout>

## One number per pre-activation, and the derivatives that hang off it

Let's fix a layer $l$ and a single example. Differentiating with respect to a weight is settled
already; what has to be decided is with respect to what it pays to differentiate *first*, and the
answer is the pre-activation. Let's call the **error** of layer $l$ the gradient of that example's
loss with respect to $\mathbf{z}^{(l)}$, and write $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}$ for it:

$$
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)} = \nabla_{\mathbf{z}^{(l)}}\,\ell \in \mathbb{R}^{d_l},
$$

one coordinate per neuron of the layer, and a column, which is the transpose of the one-row Jacobian
with which the previous lesson wrote the gradient of a scalar. It is $\ell$ and not $\mathcal{L}$:
the loss of *one* example, as everywhere in the block since <Leccion slug="funcion-de-perdida">the lesson on loss functions</Leccion>.

With that vector in hand, the parameters of layer $l$ come out with no walk at all. Its
pre-activation is:

$$
\mathbf{z}^{(l)} = \mathbf{W}^{(l)}\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(l)},
$$

and coordinate by coordinate:

$$
z^{(l)}_i = \sum_k \mathbf{W}^{(l)}_{ik} h^{(l-1)}_k + b^{(l)}_i.
$$

Look at where a particular weight appears: $\mathbf{W}^{(l)}_{jk}$ is in a single one of the $d_l$
coordinates, the $j$-th, so only one summand of the previous lesson's sum over paths survives:

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mathbf{W}^{(l)}_{jk}}
= \sum_{i=1}^{d_l} \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z^{(l)}_i}\,\frac{\partial z^{(l)}_i}{\partial \mathbf{W}^{(l)}_{jk}}
= \delta^{(l)}_j\, h^{(l-1)}_k,
\qquad
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial b^{(l)}_j} = \delta^{(l)}_j,
$$

because $\partial z^{(l)}_i / \partial \mathbf{W}^{(l)}_{jk}$ is $h^{(l-1)}_k$ when $i = j$ and zero
in the other $d_l - 1$ cases, and the derivative with respect to the bias is the same computation
with a $1$ in place of an activation. Stacked, the $d_l \times d_{l-1}$ entries are an outer product:

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}^{(l)}}\,\ell = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}\left(\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)}\right)^{\top} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_l \times d_{l-1}},
\qquad
\nabla_{\mathbf{b}^{(l)}}\,\ell = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}.
$$

A column of $d_l$ by a row of $d_{l-1}$, which is exactly the shape of $\mathbf{W}^{(l)}$, and that
agreement is the cheapest check there is: if the outer product does not come out with the shape of
the matrix it differentiates, something is transposed.

Now count what has happened, because it is the whole argument. Layer $l$ has
$d_l \cdot d_{l-1} + d_l$ parameters, and its derivatives all come out of $d_l + d_{l-1}$ numbers:
the coordinates of $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}$ and those of $\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)}$, which the forward
pass has computed already. In the first layer of the previous lesson's network that is thirty-six
derivatives read off twelve numbers. One question is left, and it takes up the rest of the lesson:
where $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}$ comes from.

## Where that number comes from: the backward recurrence

The value of $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}$ comes from the layer next door. The error of a layer is
computed out of the error of the one after it, and that is why the journey runs from the end to the
beginning.

Start at the end, where there is nothing to do. The last layer delivers $\mathbf{z}^{(L)}$, softmax
turns it into $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ and cross-entropy compares it with the label; <Leccion slug="funcion-de-perdida">the lesson on loss functions</Leccion> differentiated that whole pair, and out of the two together came

$$
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(L)} = \hat{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbf{y}.
$$

Nothing that follows depends on it being that one. Change the loss or the output activation and that
vector changes; the rest of the algorithm never finds out, because it only receives it. The small
network from the opening is the example, and this is the place to say what had not yet been said
about it: it carries a sigmoid in both layers and the loss $\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\hat{y} - y\right)^{2}$ (the $\tfrac{1}{2}$ is the convention <Leccion slug="funcion-de-perdida">the lesson on loss functions</Leccion> said it would not adopt, and it is here so the numbers come out short), so its output layer
enters with $\delta^{(2)}_1 = \left(\hat{y} - y\right)\hat{y}\left(1 - \hat{y}\right)$ and not with
$\hat{y} - y$: the extra factor is the sigmoid, which here cancels against nothing. Everything else
is identical.

Now the backward step. Let $l < L$. The pre-activation of the next layer is built out of this one's
in two movements:

$$
\mathbf{z}^{(l+1)} = \mathbf{W}^{(l+1)}\varphi\left(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}\right) + \mathbf{b}^{(l+1)},
$$

and the previous lesson has the Jacobian of both: that of an affine map is its own matrix, and that
of a $\varphi$ applied coordinate by coordinate is diagonal. Composing them,

$$
\frac{\partial \mathbf{z}^{(l+1)}}{\partial \mathbf{z}^{(l)}} = \mathbf{W}^{(l+1)}\,\text{diag}\left(\varphi^{\prime}\left(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}\right)\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{l+1} \times d_l},
$$

and that same lesson said what to do with a Jacobian when what is being propagated is the gradient
of a scalar: transpose it, and multiply the gradient in from the right. Since $\text{diag}$ is
symmetric and transposing a product reverses the order of the factors,

$$
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}
= \left(\frac{\partial \mathbf{z}^{(l+1)}}{\partial \mathbf{z}^{(l)}}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l+1)}
= \text{diag}\left(\varphi^{\prime}\left(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}\right)\right)\left(\mathbf{W}^{(l+1)}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l+1)},
$$

which, written with the Hadamard product so that the diagonal is never built, is the form that
reaches the code:

$$
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)} = \left(\left(\mathbf{W}^{(l+1)}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l+1)}\right) \odot \varphi^{\prime}\left(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}\right).
$$

Read it in two movements, because they do two different things. The first,
$\left(\mathbf{W}^{(l+1)}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l+1)}$, **transports**: it shares the
error of the $d_{l+1}$ neurons above out among the $d_l$ below, each according to the weight with
which it fed them on the forward pass. They are the same weights, with no new parameter. The second
**masks**: it multiplies what reaches each neuron by that neuron's own slope. With ReLU (rectified
linear unit) that slope is $0$ or $1$, so a neuron with a negative pre-activation receives a zero
and cuts off there everything that was coming from above.

<Details summary="The same recurrence, index by index">

With no matrices in the way. Coordinate $j$ of $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}$ is the derivative of
$\ell$ with respect to $z^{(l)}_j$, and $z^{(l)}_j$ reaches the loss through the $d_{l+1}$
pre-activations of the next layer: it is the previous lesson's sum over paths, with $d_{l+1}$ paths.

$$
\delta^{(l)}_j = \sum_{i=1}^{d_{l+1}} \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z^{(l+1)}_i}\,\frac{\partial z^{(l+1)}_i}{\partial z^{(l)}_j} = \sum_{i=1}^{d_{l+1}} \delta^{(l+1)}_i\,\frac{\partial z^{(l+1)}_i}{\partial z^{(l)}_j},
$$

where the second equality does nothing but name the first factor. The second comes out of writing
the next layer with its indices:

$$
z^{(l+1)}_i = \sum_{k=1}^{d_l} \mathbf{W}^{(l+1)}_{ik}\,\varphi\left(z^{(l)}_k\right) + b^{(l+1)}_i.
$$

Differentiating with respect to $z^{(l)}_j$, a single summand of that sum survives, the one with
$k = j$, and the activation contributes its derivative:

$$
\frac{\partial z^{(l+1)}_i}{\partial z^{(l)}_j} = \mathbf{W}^{(l+1)}_{ij}\,\varphi^{\prime}\left(z^{(l)}_j\right).
$$

That last factor does not depend on $i$, so it comes outside the sum:

$$
\delta^{(l)}_j = \left(\sum_{i=1}^{d_{l+1}} \mathbf{W}^{(l+1)}_{ij}\,\delta^{(l+1)}_i\right)\varphi^{\prime}\left(z^{(l)}_j\right).
$$

And the formula above is written. The sum runs along column $j$ of $\mathbf{W}^{(l+1)}$, which is
row $j$ of its transpose, that is, coordinate $j$ of the transport; the factor outside is coordinate
$j$ of the masking. The two forms say the same thing, and all the one with the matrices has on top
is that the computer runs it without a single index being written.

</Details>

### The whole algorithm, written out for two hidden layers

With $L = 3$ it fits whole and with no ellipses. Forwards, which is <Leccion slug="forward-pass">the lesson on the forward pass</Leccion>:

$$
\mathbf{h}^{(0)} = \mathbf{x},
\quad
\mathbf{z}^{(l)} = \mathbf{W}^{(l)}\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(l)},
\quad
\mathbf{h}^{(l)} = \varphi\left(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}\right)
\;\; (l = 1, 2),
\quad
\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \text{softmax}\left(\mathbf{z}^{(3)}\right),
$$

keeping the $\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)}$ and the $\mathbf{z}^{(l)}$ along the way. Backwards, from the end to
the beginning:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(3)} &= \hat{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbf{y}, \\[2pt]
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(2)} &= \left(\left(\mathbf{W}^{(3)}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(3)}\right) \odot \varphi^{\prime}\left(\mathbf{z}^{(2)}\right), \\[2pt]
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)} &= \left(\left(\mathbf{W}^{(2)}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(2)}\right) \odot \varphi^{\prime}\left(\mathbf{z}^{(1)}\right),
\end{aligned}
$$

and out of those three vectors come the six gradients:

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}^{(l)}}\ell = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}\left(\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)}\right)^{\top},
\qquad
\nabla_{\mathbf{b}^{(l)}}\ell = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)},
\qquad
l = 1, 2, 3.
$$

That is backpropagation, complete. Three errors, six gradients, and there is not one step more.

What it costs is read off the shapes. The backward pass does one product of matrix by vector at
each layer, with the same matrices as the forward one: the same order of operations, once only,
whatever $L$ is. Probing the parameters one at a time costs two forward passes per parameter, and
nothing bounds the number of parameters. In the network of the code cell below that is $63$
parameters, $126$ passes against one; with the twenty-thousand-entry vocabulary of <Leccion slug="forward-pass">the lesson on the forward pass</Leccion> the proportion does not change
shape, only size.

There is a price and it is worth saying, because it is not time. The backward pass needs the
$\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)}$ and the $\mathbf{z}^{(l)}$ of every layer, so they have to be kept while the
forward computation runs. A network that only predicts can throw each layer away as soon as it has
used it; one that is going to train cannot, and that memory grows with depth and with the batch.

import numpy as np

# The explorable's network: two inputs, two hidden neurons and one output, with a
# sigmoid in both layers and the loss ½(ŷ − y)². The weights are its own.
x, y = np.array([1.0, 0.5]), 1.0
W1 = np.array([[0.5, -0.3], [0.8, 0.2]])
b1 = np.array([0.1, -0.2])
W2 = np.array([[0.7, -0.6]])          # one row: the output layer has one neuron
b2 = np.array([0.15])
sigmoid = lambda z: 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-z))


def forward():
    h1 = sigmoid(W1 @ x + b1)
    y_hat = sigmoid(W2 @ h1 + b2)[0]
    return h1, y_hat, 0.5 * (y_hat - y) ** 2


h1, y_hat, loss = forward()
print("h(1) =", np.round(h1, 3), "  ŷ = %.3f   ℓ = %.3f" % (y_hat, loss))

# Backwards. One δ per pre-activation: three numbers, and there the chain ends.
d2 = np.array([(y_hat - y) * y_hat * (1.0 - y_hat)])
d1 = (W2.T @ d2) * h1 * (1.0 - h1)          # transport with the transpose, then mask
print("∂ℓ/∂ŷ = %+.3f   ∂ŷ/∂z(2)₁ = %.3f   δ(2)₁ = %+.3f"
      % (y_hat - y, y_hat * (1.0 - y_hat), d2[0]))
print("∂ℓ/∂h(1) =", np.round(W2.T @ d2, 3),
      "  φ'(z(1)) =", np.round(h1 * (1.0 - h1), 3),
      "  δ(1) =", np.round(d1, 3))

# The nine gradients, read off those three numbers.
grad = {"W1": np.outer(d1, x), "b1": d1, "W2": np.outer(d2, h1), "b2": d2}
print("∇W(1) =", np.round(grad["W1"], 3).tolist())
print("∇W(2) =", np.round(grad["W2"], 3).tolist(), "  ∂ℓ/∂b(2)₁ = %+.3f" % d2[0])

# And the check: probe the nine parameters one at a time.
worst = 0.0
for name, P in [("W1", W1), ("b1", b1), ("W2", W2), ("b2", b2)]:
    for k in np.ndindex(P.shape):
        old = P[k]
        P[k] = old + 1e-6
        plus = forward()[2]
        P[k] = old - 1e-6
        minus = forward()[2]
        P[k] = old
        worst = max(worst, abs((plus - minus) / 2e-6 - grad[name][k]))
print("nine parameters, largest difference against the probe: %.1e" % worst)
`} />

The numbers are the explorable's to three figures, and so are the names, so you can read the two in
parallel. The third line shows the two movements separately. The transport leaves $\partial \ell / \partial \mathbf{h}^{(1)} = (-0.079,\ 0.068)$:
a single error, $-0.113$, shared out between the two hidden neurons according to their weights $0.7$
and $-0.6$, which is why the second coordinate comes out with the sign flipped. The masking is the
$(0.238,\ 0.222)$ beside it, which is the derivative of the sigmoid at each pre-activation.

And look at $\nabla_{\mathbf{W}^{(1)}}\ell$, which is $(-0.019,\ -0.009)$ on top and
$(0.015,\ 0.008)$ below. Its first column is $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)}$ as it stands, because
$x_1 = 1$; the second is $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)}$ halved, because $x_2 = 0.5$. That is an outer
product seen close up, and it is the reason nine derivatives fit into three numbers and two inputs.

The second cell is the reviews network with one more hidden layer, which is where the recurrence
applies twice instead of once: eight inputs, four hidden neurons, three, and three outputs with
softmax. I chose the weights so the pre-activations would come out round. Run it and look at two
things: where zeros appear, and the last two lines.

import numpy as np

# The previous lesson's network with one more hidden layer: 8 -> 4 -> 3 -> 3.
x = np.array([0., 0., 1., 0., 1., 1., 0., 1.])   # "the movie is fun and i recommend it"
y = np.array([1., 0., 0.])                       # label: positive, out of three classes
W1 = np.array([[-1., -1., 1., 1., 0., 1., -1., 0.],    # I set the weights by hand:
               [0., 0., -1., 0., 1., 0., 0., 0.5],     # there is not one random number
               [0., 0., 0., 0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 0., 0.5],
               [1., 1., 0., -1., 0.5, -1., 1., 0.5]])
W2 = np.array([[0.5, 0., 0., -1.], [-1., 0.5, 0., 1.], [0., 1., 1., 0.5]])
W3 = np.array([[-0.5, 1., 0.5], [1., -1., -0.5], [0.5, 0.5, -0.5]])
Ws = [W1, W2, W3]
bs = [np.array([0., 0., -0.5, -1.5]), np.array([0., 0.5, -0.5]), np.zeros(3)]
calls = 0


def forward():
    global calls
    calls += 1
    hs, zs = [x], []                              # h(0) = x
    for l in range(3):
        zs.append(Ws[l] @ hs[-1] + bs[l])
        hs.append(np.maximum(zs[-1], 0.))         # ReLU; layer 3's is not used
    e = np.exp(zs[-1] - zs[-1].max())
    return hs, zs, e / e.sum()


hs, zs, y_hat = forward()
print("z(1) =", zs[0], " h(1) =", hs[1], "  <- one neuron switched off")
print("z(2) =", zs[1], "   h(2) =", hs[2], "         <- another")
print("z(3) =", zs[2], " ŷ =", np.round(y_hat, 4), " ℓ = %.6f" % -np.log(y_hat @ y))

# Backwards: one δ per layer, from the end to the beginning, and there is nothing else.
deltas = [None, None, y_hat - y]                  # softmax + cross-entropy
for l in (1, 0):
    deltas[l] = np.where(zs[l] > 0., Ws[l + 1].T @ deltas[l + 1], 0.)
for l in (2, 1, 0):                               # in the order they come out
    print("δ(%d) =" % (l + 1), np.round(deltas[l], 6))
grad_W = [np.outer(deltas[l], hs[l]) for l in range(3)]
print("∇W(2) =", (np.round(grad_W[1], 4) + 0.).tolist())   # the + 0. drops signed zeros

# The 63 gradients against the probe, which is what the other route costs.
worst, calls = 0., 0
for P, G in [(Ws[l], grad_W[l]) for l in range(3)] + [(bs[l], deltas[l]) for l in range(3)]:
    for k in np.ndindex(P.shape):
        v = P[k]
        P[k] = v + 1e-6
        d = -np.log(forward()[2] @ y)
        P[k] = v - 1e-6
        d -= -np.log(forward()[2] @ y)
        P[k] = v
        worst = max(worst, abs(d / 2e-6 - G[k]))
print("63 parameters, largest difference against the probe: %.1e" % worst)
print("forward passes spent on the probe: %d.  By backpropagation: 1." % calls)

The zeros are ReLU at work, and there are two. The fourth neuron of the first hidden layer has
$z^{(1)}_4 = -1.5$ and the second of the second has $z^{(2)}_2 = -1.25$: both are switched off for
this review and both assign a zero to their $\boldsymbol{\delta}$. That zero shows up twice in
$\nabla_{\mathbf{W}^{(2)}}\ell$, and for different reasons. Its second row is null because
$\delta^{(2)}_2 = 0$, that is, none of that neuron's four weights is corrected; its fourth column is
null because $h^{(1)}_4 = 0$, that is, the input those weights read contributes nothing. Row and
column are the two halves of the outer product, and each one vanishes on its own account.

The last two lines are the lesson. The $63$ gradients agree with the probe to $10^{-10}$, which is
the probe's error and not the algorithm's. And getting them by probing has cost $126$ forward passes
against the single one backpropagation makes. That in a toy network, with three layers and eight
inputs: the factor is the number of parameters multiplied by two, so it grows with every weight
added and never stops growing.
