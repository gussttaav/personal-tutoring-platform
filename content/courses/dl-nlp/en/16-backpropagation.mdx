      import numpy as np


      def errors(Ws, zs, y_hat, y):
          deltas = [None] * len(Ws)
          deltas[-1] = np.asarray(y_hat, dtype=float) - np.asarray(y, dtype=float)
          for l in range(len(Ws) - 2, -1, -1):
              deltas[l] = (Ws[l + 1].T @ deltas[l + 1]) * (zs[l] > 0.)

          return [np.outer(deltas[l], hs[l]) for l in range(len(deltas))]

{/* COURSE-P5-02 — Block 2, Lesson 8. */}

Everything backpropagation does, it does with the chain rule, and the chain rule is already
differentiated. The previous lesson left it in its vector form (Jacobians multiplying together, a
gradient coming in at one end and leaving converted into that of the layer before) and, with it, the
reason why, as it stands, it can't actually be run: applying it parameter by parameter costs a
complete run over the network for every single number there is to adjust. What's missing isn't new
mathematics. It's an order of operations.

And that order is easier to see on a small network than a large one. Take the smallest network
that still has a hidden layer: two inputs, two hidden neurons, one output. It has nine parameters
(four input weights, two hidden biases, two output weights and one bias), so nine derivatives need
computing. Behind those nine sit three numbers, one per pre-activation in the network. Once those
three are known, each of the nine derivatives is a product of two factors, and neither factor
has to be fetched from far away.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
What travels backwards isn't one derivative per weight, it's one number per neuron. Each weight then
gets its own derivative by multiplying its neuron's number by whatever that neuron had in front of
it.
</Callout>

## One number per pre-activation, and the derivatives that hang off it

Fix a layer $l$ and a single example. Differentiating with respect to a weight is already settled;
what needs deciding is with respect to what it pays off to differentiate *first*, and the answer is
the pre-activation. Call the **error** of layer $l$ the gradient of that example's loss with respect
to $\mathbf{z}^{(l)}$, and write $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}$ for it:

$$
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)} = \nabla_{\mathbf{z}^{(l)}}\,\ell \in \mathbb{R}^{d_l},
$$

one coordinate per neuron in the layer, and a column, the transpose of the one-row Jacobian the
previous lesson used to write the gradient of a scalar. It's $\ell$ and not $\mathcal{L}$: the loss
of *one* example, as everywhere in this block since <Leccion slug="funcion-de-perdida">the lesson
on loss functions</Leccion>.

With that vector in hand, the parameters of layer $l$ come out without any walking at all. Its
pre-activation is

$$
\mathbf{z}^{(l)} = \mathbf{W}^{(l)}\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(l)},
$$

or, coordinate by coordinate,

$$
z^{(l)}_i = \sum_k \mathbf{W}^{(l)}_{ik} h^{(l-1)}_k + b^{(l)}_i.
$$

Look at where any particular weight shows up: $\mathbf{W}^{(l)}_{jk}$ appears in exactly one of the
$d_l$ coordinates, the $j$-th, so only one summand of the previous lesson's sum over paths survives:

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mathbf{W}^{(l)}_{jk}}
= \sum_{i=1}^{d_l} \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z^{(l)}_i}\,\frac{\partial z^{(l)}_i}{\partial \mathbf{W}^{(l)}_{jk}}
= \delta^{(l)}_j\, h^{(l-1)}_k,
\qquad
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial b^{(l)}_j} = \delta^{(l)}_j,
$$

because $\partial z^{(l)}_i / \partial \mathbf{W}^{(l)}_{jk}$ equals $h^{(l-1)}_k$ when $i = j$ and
zero in the other $d_l - 1$ cases, and the bias derivative is the same computation with a $1$ in
place of an activation. Stacked, the $d_l \times d_{l-1}$ entries form an outer product:

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}^{(l)}}\,\ell = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}\left(\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)}\right)^{\top} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_l \times d_{l-1}},
\qquad
\nabla_{\mathbf{b}^{(l)}}\,\ell = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}.
$$

A column of $d_l$ by a row of $d_{l-1}$, exactly the shape of $\mathbf{W}^{(l)}$, and that match is
the cheapest check there is: if an outer product doesn't come out with the shape of the matrix it's
differentiating, something along the way got transposed.

Now count what happened here, because it's the whole argument. Layer $l$ has
$d_l \cdot d_{l-1} + d_l$ parameters, and every one of its derivatives comes out of only
$d_l + d_{l-1}$ numbers: the coordinates of $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}$ and those of
$\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)}$, which the forward pass has already computed. In the first layer of the
previous lesson's network, that's thirty-six derivatives read off twelve numbers. One question
remains, and it takes up the rest of this lesson: where does $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}$ actually
come from?

## Where that number comes from: the backward recurrence

The value of $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}$ comes from the layer next door. A layer's error is computed
out of the error of the layer after it, which is exactly why the journey runs from the end back to
the beginning.

Start at the end, where there's nothing left to do. The last layer delivers $\mathbf{z}^{(L)}$,
softmax turns it into $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$, and cross-entropy compares that with the label;
<Leccion slug="funcion-de-perdida">the lesson on loss functions</Leccion> already differentiated
that entire pair, and out of the two together came

$$
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(L)} = \hat{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbf{y}.
$$

Nothing that follows depends on it being that particular vector. Change the loss or the output
activation and that vector changes; the rest of the algorithm never even finds out, since it only
ever receives it. The small network from the opening is the running example, and this is the place
to say what hasn't been said about it yet: it carries a sigmoid in both layers and the loss
$\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\hat{y} - y\right)^{2}$ (the $\tfrac{1}{2}$ is the convention
<Leccion slug="funcion-de-perdida">the lesson on loss functions</Leccion> chose not to adopt, kept
here only so the numbers come out short), so its output layer enters with
$\delta^{(2)}_1 = \left(\hat{y} - y\right)\hat{y}\left(1 - \hat{y}\right)$ rather than
$\hat{y} - y$: the extra factor is the sigmoid, which here has nothing to cancel against. Everything
else stays identical.

Now for the backward step. Let $l < L$. The pre-activation of the next layer is built out of this
one's in two moves:

$$
\mathbf{z}^{(l+1)} = \mathbf{W}^{(l+1)}\varphi\left(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}\right) + \mathbf{b}^{(l+1)},
$$

and the previous lesson already has the Jacobian of both pieces: an affine map's Jacobian is its own
matrix, and a $\varphi$ applied coordinate by coordinate has a diagonal one. Compose them,

$$
\frac{\partial \mathbf{z}^{(l+1)}}{\partial \mathbf{z}^{(l)}} = \mathbf{W}^{(l+1)}\,\text{diag}\left(\varphi^{\prime}\left(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}\right)\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{l+1} \times d_l},
$$

and that same lesson said exactly what to do with a Jacobian when what's being propagated is the
gradient of a scalar: transpose it, and multiply the gradient in from the right. Since
$\text{diag}$ is symmetric and transposing a product reverses the order of its factors,

$$
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}
= \left(\frac{\partial \mathbf{z}^{(l+1)}}{\partial \mathbf{z}^{(l)}}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l+1)}
= \text{diag}\left(\varphi^{\prime}\left(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}\right)\right)\left(\mathbf{W}^{(l+1)}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l+1)},
$$

which, rewritten with the Hadamard product so the diagonal is never actually built, is the form that
reaches real code:

$$
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)} = \left(\left(\mathbf{W}^{(l+1)}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l+1)}\right) \odot \varphi^{\prime}\left(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}\right).
$$

Read it as two separate moves, because they do two different jobs. The first,
$\left(\mathbf{W}^{(l+1)}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l+1)}$, **transports**: it shares the
error from the $d_{l+1}$ neurons above out among the $d_l$ below, each according to the weight it
used to feed them on the forward pass. Same weights, no new parameter anywhere. The second **masks**:
it multiplies whatever reaches each neuron by that neuron's own slope. With ReLU (rectified linear
unit), that slope is $0$ or $1$, so a neuron with a negative pre-activation receives a zero and cuts
off everything that was coming down from above.

<Details summary="The same recurrence, index by index">

With no matrices in the way this time. Coordinate $j$ of $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}$ is the
derivative of $\ell$ with respect to $z^{(l)}_j$, and $z^{(l)}_j$ reaches the loss through the
$d_{l+1}$ pre-activations of the next layer: it's the previous lesson's sum over paths, with
$d_{l+1}$ paths this time.

$$
\delta^{(l)}_j = \sum_{i=1}^{d_{l+1}} \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z^{(l+1)}_i}\,\frac{\partial z^{(l+1)}_i}{\partial z^{(l)}_j} = \sum_{i=1}^{d_{l+1}} \delta^{(l+1)}_i\,\frac{\partial z^{(l+1)}_i}{\partial z^{(l)}_j},
$$

where the second equality only names the first factor. The second factor comes from writing out the
next layer with its indices:

$$
z^{(l+1)}_i = \sum_{k=1}^{d_l} \mathbf{W}^{(l+1)}_{ik}\,\varphi\left(z^{(l)}_k\right) + b^{(l+1)}_i.
$$

Differentiate with respect to $z^{(l)}_j$, and a single summand of that sum survives, the one with
$k = j$, with the activation contributing its own derivative:

$$
\frac{\partial z^{(l+1)}_i}{\partial z^{(l)}_j} = \mathbf{W}^{(l+1)}_{ij}\,\varphi^{\prime}\left(z^{(l)}_j\right).
$$

That last factor doesn't depend on $i$, so it moves outside the sum:

$$
\delta^{(l)}_j = \left(\sum_{i=1}^{d_{l+1}} \mathbf{W}^{(l+1)}_{ij}\,\delta^{(l+1)}_i\right)\varphi^{\prime}\left(z^{(l)}_j\right).
$$

Which is exactly the formula written above. The sum runs down column $j$ of $\mathbf{W}^{(l+1)}$,
which is row $j$ of its transpose, that is, coordinate $j$ of the transport; the factor sitting
outside is coordinate $j$ of the masking. Both forms say the same thing, and all the matrix version
adds is that the computer runs it without a single index ever being written by hand.

</Details>

### The whole algorithm, written out for two hidden layers

With $L = 3$ it fits whole, with no ellipses needed. Forwards, which is
<Leccion slug="forward-pass">the lesson on the forward pass</Leccion>:

$$
\mathbf{h}^{(0)} = \mathbf{x},
\quad
\mathbf{z}^{(l)} = \mathbf{W}^{(l)}\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(l)},
\quad
\mathbf{h}^{(l)} = \varphi\left(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}\right)
\;\; (l = 1, 2),
\quad
\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \text{softmax}\left(\mathbf{z}^{(3)}\right),
$$

keeping the $\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)}$ and the $\mathbf{z}^{(l)}$ along the way. Backwards, from the end
back to the beginning:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(3)} &= \hat{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbf{y}, \\[2pt]
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(2)} &= \left(\left(\mathbf{W}^{(3)}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(3)}\right) \odot \varphi^{\prime}\left(\mathbf{z}^{(2)}\right), \\[2pt]
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)} &= \left(\left(\mathbf{W}^{(2)}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(2)}\right) \odot \varphi^{\prime}\left(\mathbf{z}^{(1)}\right),
\end{aligned}
$$

and out of those three vectors come all six gradients:

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}^{(l)}}\ell = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}\left(\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)}\right)^{\top},
\qquad
\nabla_{\mathbf{b}^{(l)}}\ell = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)},
\qquad
l = 1, 2, 3.
$$

That's backpropagation, complete. Three errors, six gradients, and not one step more than that.

What it costs can be read straight off the shapes. The backward pass does one matrix-by-vector
product at each layer, using the same matrices as the forward one: the same order of operations,
done once, no matter what $L$ is. Probing the parameters one at a time costs two forward passes per
parameter, and nothing bounds how many parameters there might be. In the network from the code cell
below that's $63$ parameters, $126$ passes against a single one; with the twenty-thousand-entry
vocabulary from <Leccion slug="forward-pass">the lesson on the forward pass</Leccion>, the ratio
never changes shape, only size.

There is a price, and it's worth naming, because it isn't time. The backward pass needs the
$\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)}$ and the $\mathbf{z}^{(l)}$ of every layer, so all of them have to stay in memory
while the forward computation runs. A network that only predicts can throw each layer away the
moment it's used; one that's about to train cannot, and that memory grows with both depth and batch
size.

import numpy as np

# The explorable's network: two inputs, two hidden neurons and one output, with a
# sigmoid in both layers and the loss ½(ŷ − y)². The weights are its own.
x, y = np.array([1.0, 0.5]), 1.0
W1 = np.array([[0.5, -0.3], [0.8, 0.2]])
b1 = np.array([0.1, -0.2])
W2 = np.array([[0.7, -0.6]])          # one row: the output layer has one neuron
b2 = np.array([0.15])
sigmoid = lambda z: 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-z))


def forward():
    h1 = sigmoid(W1 @ x + b1)
    y_hat = sigmoid(W2 @ h1 + b2)[0]
    return h1, y_hat, 0.5 * (y_hat - y) ** 2


h1, y_hat, loss = forward()
print("h(1) =", np.round(h1, 3), "  ŷ = %.3f   ℓ = %.3f" % (y_hat, loss))

# Backwards. One δ per pre-activation: three numbers, and there the chain ends.
d2 = np.array([(y_hat - y) * y_hat * (1.0 - y_hat)])
d1 = (W2.T @ d2) * h1 * (1.0 - h1)          # transport with the transpose, then mask
print("∂ℓ/∂ŷ = %+.3f   ∂ŷ/∂z(2)₁ = %.3f   δ(2)₁ = %+.3f"
      % (y_hat - y, y_hat * (1.0 - y_hat), d2[0]))
print("∂ℓ/∂h(1) =", np.round(W2.T @ d2, 3),
      "  φ'(z(1)) =", np.round(h1 * (1.0 - h1), 3),
      "  δ(1) =", np.round(d1, 3))

# The nine gradients, read off those three numbers.
grad = {"W1": np.outer(d1, x), "b1": d1, "W2": np.outer(d2, h1), "b2": d2}
print("∇W(1) =", np.round(grad["W1"], 3).tolist())
print("∇W(2) =", np.round(grad["W2"], 3).tolist(), "  ∂ℓ/∂b(2)₁ = %+.3f" % d2[0])

# And the check: probe the nine parameters one at a time.
worst = 0.0
for name, P in [("W1", W1), ("b1", b1), ("W2", W2), ("b2", b2)]:
    for k in np.ndindex(P.shape):
        old = P[k]
        P[k] = old + 1e-6
        plus = forward()[2]
        P[k] = old - 1e-6
        minus = forward()[2]
        P[k] = old
        worst = max(worst, abs((plus - minus) / 2e-6 - grad[name][k]))
print("nine parameters, largest difference against the probe: %.1e" % worst)
`} />

The numbers match the explorable's to three figures, and so do the names, so you can read the two
side by side. The third line shows the two moves separately. The transport leaves
$\partial \ell / \partial \mathbf{h}^{(1)} = (-0.079,\ 0.068)$: a single error, $-0.113$, shared
between the two hidden neurons according to their weights $0.7$ and $-0.6$, which is why the second
coordinate flips sign. The masking is the $(0.238,\ 0.222)$ right beside it, the derivative of the
sigmoid at each pre-activation.

Now look at $\nabla_{\mathbf{W}^{(1)}}\ell$, which reads $(-0.019,\ -0.009)$ on top and
$(0.015,\ 0.008)$ below. Its first column is $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)}$ untouched, since $x_1 = 1$;
the second is $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)}$ halved, since $x_2 = 0.5$. That's an outer product seen up
close, and it's the whole reason nine derivatives collapse into three numbers and two inputs.

The second cell is the reviews network with one extra hidden layer, where the recurrence applies
twice instead of once: eight inputs, four hidden neurons, three more, and three outputs with
softmax. I chose the weights so the pre-activations would come out round. Run it and watch for two
things: where zeros appear, and the last two lines.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

# The previous lesson's network with one more hidden layer: 8 -> 4 -> 3 -> 3.
x = np.array([0., 0., 1., 0., 1., 1., 0., 1.])   # "the movie is fun and i recommend it"
y = np.array([1., 0., 0.])                       # label: positive, out of three classes
W1 = np.array([[-1., -1., 1., 1., 0., 1., -1., 0.],    # I set the weights by hand:
               [0., 0., -1., 0., 1., 0., 0., 0.5],     # there is not one random number
               [0., 0., 0., 0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 0., 0.5],
               [1., 1., 0., -1., 0.5, -1., 1., 0.5]])
W2 = np.array([[0.5, 0., 0., -1.], [-1., 0.5, 0., 1.], [0., 1., 1., 0.5]])
W3 = np.array([[-0.5, 1., 0.5], [1., -1., -0.5], [0.5, 0.5, -0.5]])
Ws = [W1, W2, W3]
bs = [np.array([0., 0., -0.5, -1.5]), np.array([0., 0.5, -0.5]), np.zeros(3)]
calls = 0


def forward():
    global calls
    calls += 1
    hs, zs = [x], []                              # h(0) = x
    for l in range(3):
        zs.append(Ws[l] @ hs[-1] + bs[l])
        hs.append(np.maximum(zs[-1], 0.))         # ReLU; layer 3's is not used
    e = np.exp(zs[-1] - zs[-1].max())
    return hs, zs, e / e.sum()


hs, zs, y_hat = forward()
print("z(1) =", zs[0], " h(1) =", hs[1], "  <- one neuron switched off")
print("z(2) =", zs[1], "   h(2) =", hs[2], "         <- another")
print("z(3) =", zs[2], " ŷ =", np.round(y_hat, 4), " ℓ = %.6f" % -np.log(y_hat @ y))

# Backwards: one δ per layer, from the end to the beginning, and there is nothing else.
deltas = [None, None, y_hat - y]                  # softmax + cross-entropy
for l in (1, 0):
    deltas[l] = np.where(zs[l] > 0., Ws[l + 1].T @ deltas[l + 1], 0.)
for l in (2, 1, 0):                               # in the order they come out
    print("δ(%d) =" % (l + 1), np.round(deltas[l], 6))
grad_W = [np.outer(deltas[l], hs[l]) for l in range(3)]
print("∇W(2) =", (np.round(grad_W[1], 4) + 0.).tolist())   # the + 0. drops signed zeros

# The 63 gradients against the probe, which is what the other route costs.
worst, calls = 0., 0
for P, G in [(Ws[l], grad_W[l]) for l in range(3)] + [(bs[l], deltas[l]) for l in range(3)]:
    for k in np.ndindex(P.shape):
        v = P[k]
        P[k] = v + 1e-6
        d = -np.log(forward()[2] @ y)
        P[k] = v - 1e-6
        d -= -np.log(forward()[2] @ y)
        P[k] = v
        worst = max(worst, abs(d / 2e-6 - G[k]))
print("63 parameters, largest difference against the probe: %.1e" % worst)
print("forward passes spent on the probe: %d.  By backpropagation: 1." % calls)
`} />

The zeros are ReLU at work, and there are two of them. The fourth neuron of the first hidden layer
has $z^{(1)}_4 = -1.5$, and the second neuron of the second layer has $z^{(2)}_2 = -1.25$: both are
switched off for this review, and both assign a zero to their $\boldsymbol{\delta}$. That zero shows
up twice in $\nabla_{\mathbf{W}^{(2)}}\ell$, and for two different reasons. Its second row is null
because $\delta^{(2)}_2 = 0$, meaning none of that neuron's four weights gets corrected; its fourth
column is null because $h^{(1)}_4 = 0$, meaning the input those weights read contributes nothing at
all. Row and column are the two halves of the outer product, and each vanishes for its own reason.

The last two lines are really the whole lesson. The $63$ gradients agree with the probe to
$10^{-10}$, which is the probe's own error and not the algorithm's. And getting them by probing cost
$126$ forward passes against the single one backpropagation needs. That's in a toy network, with
three layers and eight inputs: the ratio is the number of parameters times two, so it only grows as
weights get added, and it never stops growing.

The algorithm is complete, and it hasn't trained anything yet. The two cells compute the gradient of
one example with weights I supplied, print it, and stop there: nobody has subtracted anything, and
nobody has started over. What's missing to close the circle has already been written down, back in

lesson has now produced.

Putting the two halves together calls for three decisions neither lesson has made yet. Examples
arrive $B$ at a time, so the errors stop being columns and $\mathcal{L}$ goes back to being the mean
of the $\ell$. The weights have to start somewhere, and the obvious choice, all of them at zero,
fails for a reason you can read straight off the formula for $\boldsymbol{\delta}$ derived above.
And when to stop has to be decided too. Making those three calls, mounting them on a multilayer
perceptron (MLP) written from scratch, and watching the loss actually fall, is
