{/* COURSE-P5-04: Block 4, Lesson 5. COURSE-P11-07: English translation. */}

A model with attention scores pairs: one number for each output step and each input position, and
those numbers become the weights that mix the encoder states. The previous lesson, on Bahdanau's
additive attention, filled the slot that computes them with a small network (a hidden layer of $d_a$
units and a linear read-out, trained along with everything else), and it left one cost standing:
scoring a pair means building a whole vector of $d_a$ coordinates only to reduce it to the single
number that was wanted.

This lesson asks whether it takes a hidden layer to say that two vectors correspond. Linear algebra
has been producing that number since Block 1 with no network in between, and in 2015 Luong, Pham and
Manning proposed using it with a single adjustment. What comes out doesn't look like a network; it
looks like a product. And since the two forms are comparable in quality (the experiments in their
papers measure that; no derivation does), what decides between them is everything else: what they
cost, how much memory they move, and what shape their computation takes.

Go back to what the score has to do. A number that goes up when two vectors point the same way has
been in the course since <Leccion slug="bolsa-de-palabras">the lesson on the bag of words</Leccion>:
the dot product $\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{v}$, whose normalised version is cosine similarity. It would
be a free score (zero parameters) and a function of the pair, which is the condition the previous
lesson declared non-negotiable: $\mathbf{s}_{i-1}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j$ can't be split into one
opinion about a state plus another about the other.

What fails is a hidden assumption. The dot product multiplies coordinate against coordinate, so it
takes for granted that coordinate $k$ of the decoder means the same thing as coordinate $k$ of the
encoder, on the same scale. But these are two different recurrences, with two sets of weights and two
jobs (one reads Spanish, the other writes English), and nothing forces them to have chosen the same
directions for the same things, or even to have the same number of coordinates. So the fix is to
change basis first and measure afterwards.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
Scoring a pair is measuring how alike two vectors are; the only thing to learn is which basis to
compare them in.
</Callout>

## Multiplying instead of adding

The multiplicative score puts a matrix between the two states and does nothing else:

$$
e_{ij} = a\left(\mathbf{s}_{i-1}, \bar{\mathbf{h}}_j\right)
= \mathbf{s}_{i-1}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_a\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j \in \mathbb{R},
$$

with $\mathbf{W}_a \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h \times d_h}$. Read it from right to left:
$\mathbf{W}_a\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j$ is the encoder state rewritten in the decoder's coordinates, and the
dot product with $\mathbf{s}_{i-1}$ then measures what a dot product always measures. There's no
hidden layer, no saturation and no space of the function's own, so the previous lesson's $d_a$ drops
out of the problem.

Count the weights: $d_h^2$, and as before, none of them depends on the pair or on the lengths. With
$d_h = 64$ that's $4\,096$, against the $4\,128$ the additive score needed with $d_a = 32$.
Practically the same, which is useful to know early: *what separates the two scores isn't how many
weights they have, but what they do with them.*

This is the place to say what the course leaves out. Luong, Pham and Manning also change where the
context comes in (they score with the current step's state, $\mathbf{s}_i$, and mix in the context
*after* the recurrence instead of inside it), and this course doesn't follow them. It keeps the
placement from <Leccion slug="la-idea-de-atencion">the lesson on the idea of attention</Leccion>, so
that across that lesson, the previous one and this one, only one thing changes. The comparison is of
the function $a$, with everything else held fixed.

The gradient is shorter than the additive score's, for the same reason the computation is. The three
derivatives of a score come from differentiating a product of three factors:

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_a} e_{ij} = \mathbf{s}_{i-1}\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j^{\top},
\qquad
\nabla_{\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j} e_{ij} = \mathbf{W}_a^{\top}\mathbf{s}_{i-1},
\qquad
\nabla_{\mathbf{s}_{i-1}} e_{ij} = \mathbf{W}_a\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j.
$$

None of them carries a factor $1 - \tanh^{2}$, because there's no $\tanh$ to differentiate. And
everything *above* $e_{ij}$ in the chain is the previous lesson's, without a comma changed: the step
through the softmax and the derivative

$$
\partial\ell/\partial e_{ij} = \alpha_{ij}\left(\nabla_{\mathbf{c}_i}\ell\right)^{\top}\left(\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j - \mathbf{c}_i\right)
$$

never mentioned what form $a$ had. The attention stays as it is; only the alignment model changes.

<Details summary="And the accumulation over the pairs is a product too">

$\mathbf{W}_a$ is one matrix for all $T_x \cdot T_y$ pairs, so its gradient adds them all up:

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_a}\ell = \sum_{i=1}^{T_y}\sum_{j=1}^{T_x} \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial e_{ij}}\,\mathbf{s}_{i-1}\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j^{\top},
$$

which is the grid of derivatives sandwiched between the decoder states and the encoder states (two
matrix products, with the grid in the middle). The backward pass has the same shape as the forward
pass, which is why the next section holds for both.

</Details>

### Without a matrix: the dot product

The extreme case is to take $\mathbf{W}_a$ equal to the identity, which leaves the score as

$$
e_{ij} = \mathbf{s}_{i-1}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j,
$$

and the function $a$ without a single parameter to train. What it asks in return is exactly what the
matrix was there to avoid asking: that the two sides be the same width ($d_h$ coordinates in both) and
that they have also learned to use those coordinates in the same way. That's a lot to demand when the
encoder and the decoder are two different networks, each trained for its own language, which is why
the matrix is the form Luong's paper takes as its default. The parameter-free variant stays in the
course (it's the cheapest way to score that exists), but it only holds up where the two sides share a
basis.

## The whole grid in one matrix product

So far the lesson has written the scores one at a time. Now stack them. Store the encoder states by
rows in $\bar{\mathbf{H}} \in \mathbb{R}^{T_x \times d_h}$ (row $j$ is $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j^{\top}$,
transposed because in this course vectors are columns) and the decoder's in
$\mathbf{S} \in \mathbb{R}^{T_y \times d_h}$, whose row $i$ is $\mathbf{s}_{i-1}^{\top}$, the state
that scores step $i$. Then the $T_x \cdot T_y$ scores are a single object:

$$
\mathbf{A} = \text{softmax}\left(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_a\bar{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{T_y \times T_x},
$$

with the softmax applied by rows, that is, over the input positions, as in
<Leccion slug="la-idea-de-atencion">the lesson on the idea of attention</Leccion>. Check entry
$(i, j)$: row $i$ of $\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_a$ is $\mathbf{s}_{i-1}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_a$ and column $j$
of $\bar{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}$ is $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j$, so their product is $e_{ij}$ and nothing else.

The additive score can't be written this way, and no amount of cleverness changes that. Its two
projections do come out, but the $\tanh$ sits between the sum and the read-out, so a
three-dimensional block (one entry per pair, and $d_a$ coordinates within each) has to be
materialised before it can be reduced to numbers.

The counts put a price on that difference. Take $T_x = T_y = 60$ and $d_h = d_a = 256$, a long
sentence and a small model. In products and sums the two are close: about $8.8$ million for the
additive score against about $4.9$ million for the multiplicative one, a factor of less than two, and
in both most of the work goes into the projections. Where they part ways is everything else. The
additive score evaluates the $\tanh$ $921\,600$ times and the multiplicative one never does. Above
all, the additive score has to store those $921\,600$ numbers ($7.4$ MB for each pair of sentences,
before multiplying by the batch size), while the multiplicative one needs only the $3\,600$ scores
and the $60$ states already projected. Between the two, the factor is exactly $d_a$: one number per
pair against $d_a$ numbers per pair.

There's also a difference of shape that doesn't show up in any of those numbers. The multiplicative
score's work is two matrix products; the additive score's is sums and $\tanh$ over a
three-dimensional block, coordinate by coordinate. The same amount of arithmetic, organised in those
two ways, doesn't cost the same on the hardware that trains these networks, which is built precisely
for the first (that's what <Leccion slug="forward-pass">the lesson on the forward pass</Leccion>
measured, with a loop against a product).

What's left is the honest part, and it's what opens the next block. Writing $\mathbf{S}$ means having
all $T_y$ decoder states, and the decoder is recurrent: $\mathbf{s}_{i-1}$ needs $\mathbf{c}_{i-1}$,
which needs row $i-1$ of $\mathbf{A}$, which needs $\mathbf{s}_{i-2}$. While the model translates, the
grid fills up row by row and the big product never gets to happen. What can come out of the loop is
$\mathbf{W}_a\bar{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}$, which depends only on the source and needs computing once per
sentence, leaving each decoder step a single matrix-vector product:

$$
\left(\bar{\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{W}_a^{\top}\right)\mathbf{s}_{i-1} \in \mathbb{R}^{T_x},
$$

row $i$ of unnormalised scores. The score is no longer the computation's bottleneck. What strangles
the parallelism now is the recurrence that produces the states.

## The two scores, in NumPy

The first cell computes the multiplicative alignment the short way and checks that the product says
nothing different from scoring the pairs one by one. Look at the last two lines too: with
$\mathbf{W}_a$ equal to the identity, the score reduces to the dot product of the two states.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

T_y, T_x, d_h, d_a = 8, 6, 5, 4
rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
S = rng.normal(size=(T_y, d_h)) * 0.6      # row i: the state s_{i-1} that scores step i
H = rng.normal(size=(T_x, d_h)) * 0.6      # row j: the encoder state at position j
Wa = rng.normal(size=(d_h, d_h)) * 0.4     # untrained


def softmax_rows(E):
    Z = np.exp(E - E.max(axis=1, keepdims=True))     # shift invariance
    return Z / Z.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)


E = (S @ Wa) @ H.T                                   # (T_y, T_x): the whole grid, two products
A = softmax_rows(E)
E_loop = np.array([[S[i] @ Wa @ H[j] for j in range(T_x)] for i in range(T_y)])

print("shapes:  S", S.shape, " H", H.shape, " Wa", Wa.shape, " ->  A", A.shape)
print("every row sums to 1:", bool(np.allclose(A.sum(axis=1), 1.0)))
print("the product and the loop agree:", bool(np.allclose(E, E_loop)))
print("weights of a:  multiplicative", d_h * d_h, " additive", 2 * d_a * d_h + d_a)
print("largest weight in each row:", np.round(A.max(axis=1), 3), " uniform:", round(1 / T_x, 3))
print()

# Wa = I: no change of basis, and the score is the dot product of the two states.
identity = softmax_rows((S @ np.eye(d_h)) @ H.T)
print("with Wa = I, row 1:", np.round(identity[0], 3))
print("same as scoring with S @ H.T:", bool(np.allclose(identity, softmax_rows(S @ H.T))))
`} />

The line that matters is the loop's: scoring pair by pair gives the same numbers as the product, which
is all there is to check about the short form. The largest-weight line holds a surprise. With freshly
initialised weights the map already shares out unevenly (one row takes $0.851$ when an even share
would be $0.167$), which didn't happen in the previous lesson at a similar size, where the largest
weight in each row hovered around the even share. The difference is the $\tanh$: it bounds each
coordinate before $\mathbf{v}_a$ reads it, so the additive score starts out small. The multiplicative
score has nothing to bound it, and its size grows with the size of the states. That peak isn't an
alignment, it's the direction $\mathbf{W}_a$ happened to take at random; what the formula guarantees
isn't that it starts out saying something, but that there's a route along which to train it. Scores
that grow without bound as the model gets larger are a real problem, and the next block brings a
one-line fix.

Wsa, Wha = rng.normal(size=(d_a, d_h)) * 0.05, rng.normal(size=(d_a, d_h)) * 0.05

run to run without ever getting close to one. There's an experiment that settles the question. Change
$d_a$ to $64$ and run the cell again: the additive score now does *less* arithmetic than the
multiplicative one ($2.2$ million operations against $4.9$) and still takes longer, about twice as
long in the runs behind this lesson. What the clock is measuring isn't how many operations there are.

With the two scores side by side, something shows that neither could reveal alone. In both, the
encoder state enters the mechanism twice, through different doors: once as an argument of $a$, which
is where its weight is decided, and once as a term of $\mathbf{c}_i$, which is how its content
reaches the decoder. The decoder state, by contrast, enters only once, always from the same side: it's
the one asking. That makes three roles, and neither formula requires the first two to be played by the
same vector. One vector plays both only because nobody has yet proposed separating them.
