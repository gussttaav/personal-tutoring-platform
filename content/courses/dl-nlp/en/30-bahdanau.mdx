      import numpy as np


      def alignment(S, H, Wsa, Wha, va):
          Ps = S @ Wsa.T                          # (T_y, d_a): one projection per step
          Ph = H @ Wha.T                          # (T_x, d_a): one per position
          P = Ps[:, None, :] + Ph[None, :, :]     # (T_y, T_x, d_a): the sum, pair by pair
          E = np.tanh(P) @ va                     # (T_y, T_x): the score
          Z = np.exp(E - E.max(axis=1, keepdims=True))
          return Z / Z.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)

{/* COURSE-P5-04: Block 4, Lesson 4. COURSE-P11-07: English translation. */}

A model with attention needs, at every output step, one weight for each input position. The
previous lesson, on the idea of attention, set up everything around those weights: they come out of
a softmax that runs over the positions of the source, they mix the encoder states into a context
vector of each step's own, and that vector enters the decoder's recurrence through its matrix
$\mathbf{W}_{ch}$. What it didn't write down is what produces them. Before the softmax there's a
function, $a$, that takes a pair (where the decoder is, and what's at one input position) and
returns a score, and so far it has had to do two things only: return a number, and be
differentiable. This lesson fills it in, with the form Bahdanau gave it in 2014.

The question is small and concrete: what function swallows two vectors and returns a number saying
how well the two correspond? Bahdanau's answer is a tiny neural network, with a single hidden layer.
But what teaches most isn't that network, it's the constraint that decides its shape: the score has
to be a function of the *pair*. A function that gives an opinion on each vector separately and adds
the two opinions up doesn't score a correspondence: it scores two loose things, and further down
you'll see that this destroys the whole mechanism.

Go back to the previous lesson's map and look at three rows. The row for <W>i</W> and the row for
<W>read</W> take nearly everything from the same position, the one holding <W>leí</W>, which says
<W>i read</W> in a single word, with weights of $0.74$ and $0.81$. The row for <W>a</W>, the next
one, gives that same position $0.05$. The state $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_2$ hasn't changed between those
three rows (the encoder computed it once and there it stays), and yet it receives two high scores
and one low one. The only thing that differs is which decoder state it was being compared with each
time.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
Scoring a pair is a network with one hidden layer: project the two vectors into the same space, add
them there, squash the sum and read it out with a vector.
</Callout>

## A small network that scores pairs

The additive score projects the two states into a space of its own, of $d_a$ coordinates, and adds
them inside it:

$$
\mathbf{p}_{ij} = \mathbf{W}_{sa}\mathbf{s}_{i-1} + \mathbf{W}_{ha}\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j \in \mathbb{R}^{d_a},
$$

with $\mathbf{W}_{sa}, \mathbf{W}_{ha} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_a \times d_h}$, one for each side. Both have
$d_h$ columns because in this block the encoder and the decoder are the same width, but the sum
doesn't require it. All it asks is that the two projections land in the same space, that is, that
the two matrices have $d_a$ rows; the columns of each are their own side's business. If the decoder
states had $d_s$ coordinates, $\mathbf{W}_{sa}$ would be $d_a \times d_s$ and the rest of the score
would stay as it is. In Bahdanau's paper, in fact, the two widths differ: the paper's encoder reads
the sentence in both directions and concatenates the two states, so its states are twice as wide as
the decoder's.

That sum is the pre-activation of a hidden layer, and what follows is what any layer already does:
squash it and read it out.

$$
e_{ij} = a\left(\mathbf{s}_{i-1}, \bar{\mathbf{h}}_j\right)
= \mathbf{v}_a^{\top}\tanh\left(\mathbf{p}_{ij}\right) \in \mathbb{R},
$$

with $\mathbf{v}_a \in \mathbb{R}^{d_a}$. It's a multilayer perceptron (MLP) from Block 2 at its
smallest size: one hidden layer of $d_a$ units with $\tanh$, and a linear output of a single neuron.
That neuron is the one from <Leccion slug="la-neurona">the lesson on the artificial neuron</Leccion>,
$z = \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b$: its input is the hidden layer, $\tanh(\mathbf{p}_{ij})$, and
its weights are $\mathbf{v}_a$, one per hidden unit. We call $\mathbf{v}_a$ the **read-out vector**,
because it turns the $d_a$ numbers of the hidden layer into the single number that is the score.

Neither of the two layers has a bias, and for different reasons. The output's would be redundant,
because it would add the same number to the whole row, and a constant per row doesn't survive the
softmax. The hidden layer's wouldn't be: inside the $\tanh$ it shifts the point where each unit
works, closer to saturation or further from it, and that does change the scores. Bahdanau leaves it
out and many implementations put it in; here I follow the paper's formula.

Count the weights: $2 d_a d_h + d_a$. $T_x$ doesn't appear, nor does $T_y$, nor the pair. There's a
single set of weights, and it scores the $T_x \cdot T_y$ pairs of this sentence and those of every
other, which is the same economy as the previous block's recurrence (one $\mathbf{W}_{hh}$ for every
position) applied to a grid instead of a row.

### Without the non-linearity, every step would look at the same place

Remove the $\tanh$ and look at what's left. The read-out vector goes into the sum and distributes
over it:

$$
e_{ij} = \mathbf{v}_a^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{sa}\mathbf{s}_{i-1} + \mathbf{v}_a^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{ha}\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j,
$$

two numbers that are added together without either having looked at the other. The first,
$\mathbf{v}_a^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{sa}\mathbf{s}_{i-1}$, depends on step $i$ but not on position $j$.
In row $i$, that is, in the $T_x$ scores $e_{i1}, \dots, e_{iT_x}$ that step $i$'s softmax turns into
weights, it's the same in every one: a constant added to the whole row. And a constant like that
doesn't change the softmax. It's the shift invariance from <Leccion slug="funcion-de-perdida">the
lesson on loss functions</Leccion>, the same one that lets you subtract the maximum before
exponentiating, and here it makes that first number cancel between numerator and denominator:

$$
\alpha_{ij} = \frac{\exp\left(\mathbf{v}_a^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{ha}\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j\right)}{\sum_{k=1}^{T_x} \exp\left(\mathbf{v}_a^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{ha}\bar{\mathbf{h}}_k\right)}.
$$

The index $i$ has disappeared from the right-hand side. All $T_y$ steps receive the same row of
weights, and with it the same context vector, so the model is back to being the fixed summary of the
earlier lessons: a learned alignment, yes, but frozen, only frozen wherever training says instead of
on the last token. The $\tanh$ is what prevents that split. It squashes the sum *before* reading it,
and a non-linear function of a sum doesn't distribute over its terms, so the score can't come apart
again into an opinion about the decoder plus another about the encoder.

## How the gradient reaches it

Now there are parameters to train, and the question is whether the loss reaches them. The chain
starts at the mixture, where $\alpha_{ij}$ multiplies a known vector:

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \alpha_{ij}} = \left(\nabla_{\mathbf{c}_i}\ell\right)^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j,
$$

and continues through the softmax, which spreads each score across every weight in its row. Out the
other side comes the derivative with respect to a score:

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial e_{ij}} = \alpha_{ij}\left(\nabla_{\mathbf{c}_i}\ell\right)^{\top}\left(\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j - \mathbf{c}_i\right).
$$

<Details summary="The step through the softmax, with its two cases">

With $i$ fixed, $\alpha_{ij}$ depends on all $T_x$ scores of the row, so the derivative with respect
to one of them sums over the whole row. The softmax's derivative has the two cases from
<Leccion slug="funcion-de-perdida">the lesson on loss functions</Leccion>: when the weight and the
score share an index it's $\alpha_{ij}\left(1 - \alpha_{ij}\right)$, and when they don't,
$-\alpha_{ik}\alpha_{ij}$. Separating the term $k = j$ from the rest:

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial e_{ij}}
= \alpha_{ij}\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \alpha_{ij}} - \alpha_{ij}\sum_{k=1}^{T_x} \alpha_{ik}\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \alpha_{ik}}.
$$

The sum is $\left(\nabla_{\mathbf{c}_i}\ell\right)^{\top}\sum_k \alpha_{ik}\bar{\mathbf{h}}_k$, and
that weighted sum is $\mathbf{c}_i$. Substituting $\partial \ell / \partial \alpha_{ij}$ and taking
$\alpha_{ij}$ out as a common factor gives the derivative above.

</Details>

Read it slowly, because it says how attention learns where to look. It has two parts, and the first
is what happens to the mixture when the score goes up. If $e_{ij}$ grows a little, position $j$
gains weight at the expense of the others and $\mathbf{c}_i$ shifts towards $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j$.
Precisely,
$\partial \mathbf{c}_i / \partial e_{ij} = \alpha_{ij}\left(\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j - \mathbf{c}_i\right)$,
which is the same calculation as in the drop-down with $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_k$ in place of
$\partial \ell / \partial \alpha_{ik}$. The second part is $\nabla_{\mathbf{c}_i}\ell$,
whose opposite points to where the loss goes down: to where gradient descent would want to take the
mixture. The derivative is the dot product of the two, so it measures whether moving $\mathbf{c}_i$
towards $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j$ takes it where it ought to go.

That gives what training does with the score. If moving the mixture towards $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j$
makes the loss go down, the score goes up; if it makes the loss go up, the score goes down; and if
$\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j$ coincides with $\mathbf{c}_i$, raising the score doesn't move the mixture, so it
gets no correction at all. A score, then, isn't corrected according to what its state is worth on
its own, but according to what it adds against the mixture the row has already built. It makes sense
for this to be a comparison and not a grade: the weights sum to $1$, so raising one is lowering the
others, and there's no way to reward one position without saying which one pays for it.

From there back, it's the chain rule as you already know it. The read-out hands back
$\tanh(\mathbf{p}_{ij})$, the $\tanh$ contributes its derivative $1 - \tanh^{2}$, and each matrix
collects the outer product with the vector it multiplied:

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{v}_a}\ell = \sum_{i,j} \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial e_{ij}}\tanh\left(\mathbf{p}_{ij}\right),
\qquad
\nabla_{\mathbf{p}_{ij}}\ell = \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial e_{ij}}\,\mathbf{v}_a \odot \left(1 - \tanh^{2}\left(\mathbf{p}_{ij}\right)\right),
$$

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{ha}}\ell = \sum_{i,j} \left(\nabla_{\mathbf{p}_{ij}}\ell\right)\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j^{\top},
\qquad
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{sa}}\ell = \sum_{i,j} \left(\nabla_{\mathbf{p}_{ij}}\ell\right)\mathbf{s}_{i-1}^{\top}.
$$

All $T_x \cdot T_y$ pairs add into the same three arrays, which is what sharing weights means.

And there's a consequence that isn't in the parameters. Look at where $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j$ appears:
in the mixture, as one of the terms, and in the $T_y$ scores, as an argument of $a$. That's two
places, so the gradient reaches it along two routes and they have to be added:

$$
\nabla_{\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j}\ell = \sum_{i=1}^{T_y} \alpha_{ij}\nabla_{\mathbf{c}_i}\ell + \sum_{i=1}^{T_y} \mathbf{W}_{ha}^{\top}\nabla_{\mathbf{p}_{ij}}\ell.
$$

On top of those two comes the one the previous block already had, the one that comes down from
$\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{j+1}$ through the encoder's recurrence, and that's where it gets interesting: the
two above do *not* go through it. They go from step $i$ of the decoder to position $j$ of the input
without travelling the stretch of recurrence that separates that position from the end of the
sentence, so they don't multiply by $\mathbf{W}^{\text{enc}}_{hh}$ even once, and their length
doesn't grow as $j$ moves further away. The product of matrices that
<Leccion slug="gradiente-desvanecido">the lesson on the vanishing gradient</Leccion> pointed to as
the cause of the problem doesn't appear on these two routes.

## The score, in NumPy

The first cell computes the whole grid. It stores the states by rows (one row per step, one row per
position), which is why the matrices appear transposed relative to the equations. Look at two
things: how much the sharing-out that comes from freshly initialised weights looks like a map, and
what happens when you ask for the version without the $\tanh$.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

T_y, T_x, d_h, d_a = 8, 6, 5, 4
rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
S = rng.normal(size=(T_y, d_h)) * 0.6      # row i: the state s_{i-1} that scores step i
H = rng.normal(size=(T_x, d_h)) * 0.6      # row j: the encoder state at position j
Wsa = rng.normal(size=(d_a, d_h)) * 0.5    # untrained: nobody has corrected this yet
Wha = rng.normal(size=(d_a, d_h)) * 0.5
va = rng.normal(size=d_a) * 0.5


def softmax_rows(E):
    Z = np.exp(E - E.max(axis=1, keepdims=True))     # shift invariance
    return Z / Z.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)


def score(S, H, with_tanh=True):
    Ps = S @ Wsa.T                                   # (T_y, d_a): one projection per step
    Ph = H @ Wha.T                                   # (T_x, d_a): one per position
    P = Ps[:, None, :] + Ph[None, :, :]              # (T_y, T_x, d_a): the sum, pair by pair
    return (np.tanh(P) if with_tanh else P) @ va     # (T_y, T_x)


alpha = softmax_rows(score(S, H))
assert np.allclose(alpha.sum(axis=1), 1.0), "some row doesn't sum to 1"

print("weights:", 2 * d_a * d_h + d_a, "  shapes:  alpha", alpha.shape)
print("row 1:", np.round(alpha[0], 3))
print("largest weight in each row:", np.round(alpha.max(axis=1), 3), " uniform:", round(1 / T_x, 3))

linear = softmax_rows(score(S, H, with_tanh=False))
print()
print("without tanh, row 1:", np.round(linear[0], 3))
print("without tanh, row 8:", np.round(linear[7], 3))
print("without tanh, all rows equal:", bool(np.allclose(linear, linear[0])))
`} />

The freshly initialised map says nothing: the largest weight in each row is around $0.18$ when an
even share would be $0.167$, so the eight rows are practically the mean of the sentence. The
explorable's interpretable alignment doesn't come from the architecture, it comes from training;
what the architecture guarantees is that there's a route along which to train it. And the output
after the blank line is the previous section checked by hand: without the $\tanh$, row $1$ and row
$8$ come out equal to the last decimal.

The second cell follows the two routes separately. It needs a loss and uses the cheapest one
possible (one whose gradient with respect to each $\mathbf{c}_i$ is a given vector), so that the
only thing being measured is the mechanism.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

# Needs the previous cell: S, H, Wsa, Wha, va, score, softmax_rows.
# Toy loss: l = sum_i G[i] . c_i, so the gradient of l with respect to c_i
# is exactly G[i], without differentiating the decoder or the cross-entropy.
G = np.random.default_rng(9).normal(size=(T_y, d_h))


def mix(H):
    alpha = softmax_rows(score(S, H))
    return alpha @ H, alpha


def loss(H):
    C, _ = mix(H)
    return float((G * C).sum())


C, alpha = mix(H)
via_value = alpha.T @ G                                         # route 1: as a term of c_i
dl_de = alpha * (G @ H.T - (G * C).sum(axis=1, keepdims=True))   # alpha_ij * G_i . (h_j - c_i)
P = (S @ Wsa.T)[:, None, :] + (H @ Wha.T)[None, :, :]
D = dl_de[:, :, None] * va * (1 - np.tanh(P) ** 2)              # (T_y, T_x, d_a)
via_score = np.einsum("ijk,kl->jl", D, Wha)                     # route 2: as an argument of a

num = np.zeros_like(H)                                          # numerical probe, central difference
for j in range(T_x):
    for l in range(d_h):
        Hplus = H.copy(); Hplus[j, l] += 1e-6
        Hminus = H.copy(); Hminus[j, l] -= 1e-6
        num[j, l] = (loss(Hplus) - loss(Hminus)) / 2e-6

print("norm of the value route:      %.4f" % np.linalg.norm(via_value))
print("norm of the score route:      %.4f" % np.linalg.norm(via_score))
print("difference, each on its own:  %.1e   %.1e"
      % (np.abs(via_value - num).max(), np.abs(via_score - num).max()))
print("difference, the two added:    %.1e" % np.abs(via_value + via_score - num).max())
`} />

The last line is the check: added together, the two routes reproduce the numerical gradient to the
order of $10^{-10}$, and on its own each one is wrong in the first digit. The norms say something
more, and it's honest to look at it: with untrained weights, the value route is eleven times larger
than the score route. At the start almost all of the gradient arrives along the route it would take
even if the alignment were uniform, and the small route, the one through $a$, is the only one that
can pull the map out of that uniformity. Change the scale of `va` to $2.0$ and run both cells again:
the scores spread apart, the weights sharpen and the two norms draw closer.

The last question leaves a bill unsettled. The two projections come out of the loop because each
depends on a single index, but between the sum and the read-out vector there's a $\tanh$, and that
forces the grid of $T_x \cdot T_y$ vectors of $d_a$ coordinates to be built one vector at a time. A
sixty-token sentence translated into sixty asks for $3\,600$ of those vectors, and none of the
operations that produce them is a large matrix product: they're sums and $\tanh$s over a
three-dimensional block. The hardware that trains these networks does one thing far better than
everything else, and it isn't that.

There's another way to score a pair that avoids the whole problem, and its formula fits in five
symbols: Luong's attention, the multiplicative kind, which is <Leccion slug="luong">the next
lesson</Leccion>. Instead of projecting, adding and squashing, it multiplies the two states through
a matrix, so the complete grid of scores becomes *one* matrix product. That lesson compares the two
scores (what each costs, what each can do), and that's where what Block 5 looks like starts to be
decided.
