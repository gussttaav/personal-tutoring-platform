      import numpy as np


      def alignment(S, H, Wsa, Wha, va):
          Ps = S @ Wsa.T                          # (T_y, d_a): one projection per step
          Ph = H @ Wha.T                          # (T_x, d_a): one per position
          P = Ps[:, None, :] + Ph[None, :, :]     # (T_y, T_x, d_a): the sum, pair by pair

          Z = np.exp(E - E.max(axis=1, keepdims=True))
          return Z / Z.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)

{/* COURSE-P5-04: Block 4, Lesson 4. COURSE-P11-07: English translation. */}

A model with attention needs one weight for each input position at every output step. The previous
lesson, on the idea of attention, set up everything around those weights: they come out of a softmax
over the positions of the source, they mix the encoder states into a context vector of each step's
own, and that vector enters the decoder's recurrence through the matrix $\mathbf{W}_{ch}$. What that
lesson didn't write down is what produces the weights. Before the softmax there's a function, $a$,
that takes a pair (where the decoder is, and what's at one input position) and returns a score. So
far it has only had to do two things: return a number, and be differentiable. This lesson fills it in
with the form Bahdanau gave it in 2014.

The question is small and concrete: what function takes two vectors and returns a number saying how
well they correspond? Bahdanau's answer is a tiny neural network with a single hidden layer. But the
network isn't what teaches the most; the constraint that decides its shape is. The score has to be a
function of the *pair*. A function that gives an opinion on each vector separately and adds the two
opinions up doesn't score a correspondence, only two unrelated things, and further down you'll see
that this destroys the whole mechanism.

Go back to the previous lesson's map and look at three rows. The rows for <W>i</W> and <W>read</W>
take nearly everything from the same position, the one holding <W>leí</W>, which says <W>i read</W>
in a single word, with weights of $0.74$ and $0.81$. The next row, for <W>a</W>, gives that same
position $0.05$. The state $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_2$ hasn't changed between those three rows (the encoder
computed it once, and there it stays), and yet it receives two high scores and one low one. The only
thing that differs is which decoder state it was compared with each time.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
Scoring a pair is a network with one hidden layer: project the two vectors into the same space, add
them there, squash the sum and read it out with a vector.
</Callout>

## A small network that scores pairs

The additive score projects the two states into a space of its own, with $d_a$ coordinates, and adds
them there:

$$
\mathbf{p}_{ij} = \mathbf{W}_{sa}\mathbf{s}_{i-1} + \mathbf{W}_{ha}\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j \in \mathbb{R}^{d_a},
$$

with $\mathbf{W}_{sa}, \mathbf{W}_{ha} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_a \times d_h}$, one for each side. Both have
$d_h$ columns because the encoder and the decoder are the same width in this block, but the sum
doesn't require that. All it asks is that the two projections land in the same space, that is, that
the two matrices have $d_a$ rows; the number of columns in each is its own side's business. If the
decoder states had $d_s$ coordinates, $\mathbf{W}_{sa}$ would be $d_a \times d_s$ and the rest of the
score would stay as it is. In fact, in Bahdanau's paper the two widths do differ: its encoder reads
the sentence in both directions and concatenates the two states, so its encoder states are twice as
wide as the decoder's.

That sum is the pre-activation of a hidden layer, and what comes next is what any layer already does:
squash it and read it out.

$$
e_{ij} = a\left(\mathbf{s}_{i-1}, \bar{\mathbf{h}}_j\right)
= \mathbf{v}_a^{\top}\tanh\left(\mathbf{p}_{ij}\right) \in \mathbb{R},
$$

with $\mathbf{v}_a \in \mathbb{R}^{d_a}$. This is a multilayer perceptron (MLP) from Block 2 at its
smallest size: one hidden layer of $d_a$ units with $\tanh$, and a linear output of a single neuron.
That neuron is the one from <Leccion slug="la-neurona">the lesson on the artificial neuron</Leccion>,
$z = \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b$: its input is the hidden layer, $\tanh(\mathbf{p}_{ij})$, and
its weights are $\mathbf{v}_a$, one per hidden unit. We call $\mathbf{v}_a$ the **read-out vector**,
because it turns the $d_a$ numbers of the hidden layer into the single number that is the score.

Neither layer has a bias, for different reasons. The output's would be redundant, because it would
add the same number to the whole row, and a constant per row cancels out in the softmax. The hidden
layer's wouldn't be: inside the $\tanh$ it shifts the point where each unit operates, closer to
saturation or further from it, and that does change the scores. Bahdanau leaves it out and many
implementations put it in; here I follow the paper's formula.

Count the weights: $2 d_a d_h + d_a$. Neither $T_x$ nor $T_y$ appears, and neither does the pair.
There's a single set of weights, and it scores the $T_x \cdot T_y$ pairs of this sentence and of every
other, which is the same economy as the previous block's recurrence (one $\mathbf{W}_{hh}$ for every
position), applied to a grid instead of a row.

### Without the non-linearity, every step would look at the same place

Remove the $\tanh$ and look at what's left. The read-out vector distributes over the sum:

$$
e_{ij} = \mathbf{v}_a^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{sa}\mathbf{s}_{i-1} + \mathbf{v}_a^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{ha}\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j,
$$

two numbers added together without either having looked at the other. The first,
$\mathbf{v}_a^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{sa}\mathbf{s}_{i-1}$, depends on step $i$ but not on position $j$. So
in row $i$, that is, among the $T_x$ scores $e_{i1}, \dots, e_{iT_x}$ that step $i$'s softmax turns
into weights, it's the same in every score: a constant added to the whole row. And a constant like
that doesn't change the softmax. It's the shift invariance from
<Leccion slug="funcion-de-perdida">the lesson on loss functions</Leccion>, the same property that
lets you subtract the maximum before exponentiating, and here it makes the first number cancel
between numerator and denominator:

$$
\alpha_{ij} = \frac{\exp\left(\mathbf{v}_a^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{ha}\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j\right)}{\sum_{k=1}^{T_x} \exp\left(\mathbf{v}_a^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{ha}\bar{\mathbf{h}}_k\right)}.
$$

The index $i$ has disappeared from the right-hand side. All $T_y$ steps receive the same row of
weights, and therefore the same context vector, so the model is back to being the fixed summary of
the earlier lessons: a learned alignment, yes, but still frozen, only now it sits wherever training
puts it instead of on the last token. The $\tanh$ is what prevents that split. It squashes the sum
*before* the read-out, and a non-linear function of a sum doesn't distribute over its terms, so the
score can't come apart again into one opinion about the decoder plus another about the encoder.

## How the gradient reaches it

Now there are parameters to train, and the question is whether the loss reaches them. The chain
starts at the mixture, where $\alpha_{ij}$ multiplies a known vector:

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \alpha_{ij}} = \left(\nabla_{\mathbf{c}_i}\ell\right)^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j,
$$

and continues through the softmax, which makes each score affect every weight in its row. Out the
other side comes the derivative with respect to a score:

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial e_{ij}} = \alpha_{ij}\left(\nabla_{\mathbf{c}_i}\ell\right)^{\top}\left(\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j - \mathbf{c}_i\right).
$$

<Details summary="The step through the softmax, with its two cases">

With $i$ fixed, each weight in the row depends on all $T_x$ scores, so the derivative of the loss
with respect to one score sums over the whole row. The softmax's derivative has two cases, the ones
from <Leccion slug="funcion-de-perdida">the lesson on loss functions</Leccion>: when the weight and
the score share an index it's $\alpha_{ij}\left(1 - \alpha_{ij}\right)$, and when they don't,
$-\alpha_{ik}\alpha_{ij}$. Separating the term $k = j$ from the rest gives

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial e_{ij}}
= \alpha_{ij}\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \alpha_{ij}} - \alpha_{ij}\sum_{k=1}^{T_x} \alpha_{ik}\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \alpha_{ik}}.
$$

The sum is $\left(\nabla_{\mathbf{c}_i}\ell\right)^{\top}\sum_k \alpha_{ik}\bar{\mathbf{h}}_k$, and
that weighted sum is $\mathbf{c}_i$. Substituting $\partial \ell / \partial \alpha_{ij}$ and factoring
out $\alpha_{ij}$ gives the derivative above.

</Details>

Read it slowly, because it says how attention learns where to look. It has two parts. The first is
what happens to the mixture when the score goes up: if $e_{ij}$ grows a little, position $j$ gains
weight at the expense of the others, and $\mathbf{c}_i$ shifts towards $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j$.
Precisely,
$\partial \mathbf{c}_i / \partial e_{ij} = \alpha_{ij}\left(\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j - \mathbf{c}_i\right)$,
which is the same calculation as in the drop-down, with $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_k$ in place of
$\partial \ell / \partial \alpha_{ik}$. The second part is $\nabla_{\mathbf{c}_i}\ell$,
whose opposite points to where the loss goes down, that is, where gradient descent would want to take
the mixture. The derivative is the dot product of the two, so it measures whether moving
$\mathbf{c}_i$ towards $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j$ takes it where it ought to go.

That tells us what training does with the score. If moving the mixture towards $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j$
makes the loss go down, the score goes up; if it makes the loss go up, the score goes down; and if
$\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j$ coincides with $\mathbf{c}_i$, raising the score doesn't move the mixture, so the
score gets no correction at all. A score, then, isn't corrected according to what its state is worth
on its own, but according to what that state adds to the mixture the row has already built. It makes
sense for this to be a comparison and not a grade: the weights sum to $1$, so raising one means
lowering the others, and there's no way to reward one position without saying which one pays for it.

From there back, it's the chain rule as you already know it. The read-out hands back
$\tanh(\mathbf{p}_{ij})$, the $\tanh$ contributes its derivative $1 - \tanh^{2}$, and each matrix
collects the outer product with the vector it multiplied:

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{v}_a}\ell = \sum_{i,j} \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial e_{ij}}\tanh\left(\mathbf{p}_{ij}\right),
\qquad
\nabla_{\mathbf{p}_{ij}}\ell = \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial e_{ij}}\,\mathbf{v}_a \odot \left(1 - \tanh^{2}\left(\mathbf{p}_{ij}\right)\right),
$$

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{ha}}\ell = \sum_{i,j} \left(\nabla_{\mathbf{p}_{ij}}\ell\right)\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j^{\top},
\qquad
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{sa}}\ell = \sum_{i,j} \left(\nabla_{\mathbf{p}_{ij}}\ell\right)\mathbf{s}_{i-1}^{\top}.
$$

All $T_x \cdot T_y$ pairs add into the same three arrays, which is what sharing weights means.

There's one more consequence, and it isn't in the parameters. Look at where $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j$
appears: in the mixture, as one of the terms, and in the $T_y$ scores, as an argument of $a$. That's
two places, so the gradient reaches it along two routes, and the two contributions have to be added:

$$
\nabla_{\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j}\ell = \sum_{i=1}^{T_y} \alpha_{ij}\nabla_{\mathbf{c}_i}\ell + \sum_{i=1}^{T_y} \mathbf{W}_{ha}^{\top}\nabla_{\mathbf{p}_{ij}}\ell.
$$

On top of those two comes the one the previous block already had, the gradient that comes down from
$\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{j+1}$ through the encoder's recurrence. And here is where it gets interesting: the
two routes above do *not* go through that recurrence. They run from step $i$ of the decoder straight
to position $j$ of the input, without travelling the stretch of recurrence that separates that
position from the end of the sentence. They don't multiply by $\mathbf{W}^{\text{enc}}_{hh}$ even
once, and their length doesn't grow as $j$ moves further away. The product of matrices that
<Leccion slug="gradiente-desvanecido">the lesson on the vanishing gradient</Leccion> identified as the
cause of the problem doesn't appear on either of them.

## The score, in NumPy

The first cell computes the whole grid. It stores the states by rows (one row per step, one row per
position), which is why the matrices appear transposed relative to the equations. There are two things
to look at: how closely the sharing-out that freshly initialised weights produce resembles a map, and
what happens when you ask for the version without the $\tanh$.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

T_y, T_x, d_h, d_a = 8, 6, 5, 4
rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
S = rng.normal(size=(T_y, d_h)) * 0.6      # row i: the state s_{i-1} that scores step i
H = rng.normal(size=(T_x, d_h)) * 0.6      # row j: the encoder state at position j
Wsa = rng.normal(size=(d_a, d_h)) * 0.5    # untrained: nobody has corrected this yet
Wha = rng.normal(size=(d_a, d_h)) * 0.5
va = rng.normal(size=d_a) * 0.5


def softmax_rows(E):
    Z = np.exp(E - E.max(axis=1, keepdims=True))     # shift invariance
    return Z / Z.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)


def score(S, H, with_tanh=True):
    Ps = S @ Wsa.T                                   # (T_y, d_a): one projection per step
    Ph = H @ Wha.T                                   # (T_x, d_a): one per position
    P = Ps[:, None, :] + Ph[None, :, :]              # (T_y, T_x, d_a): the sum, pair by pair
    return (np.tanh(P) if with_tanh else P) @ va     # (T_y, T_x)


alpha = softmax_rows(score(S, H))
assert np.allclose(alpha.sum(axis=1), 1.0), "some row doesn't sum to 1"

print("weights:", 2 * d_a * d_h + d_a, "  shapes:  alpha", alpha.shape)
print("row 1:", np.round(alpha[0], 3))
print("largest weight in each row:", np.round(alpha.max(axis=1), 3), " uniform:", round(1 / T_x, 3))

linear = softmax_rows(score(S, H, with_tanh=False))
print()
print("without tanh, row 1:", np.round(linear[0], 3))
print("without tanh, row 8:", np.round(linear[7], 3))
print("without tanh, all rows equal:", bool(np.allclose(linear, linear[0])))
`} />

The freshly initialised map says nothing: the largest weight in each row is around $0.18$ when an even
share would be $0.167$, so the eight rows are practically the mean of the sentence. The explorable's
interpretable alignment doesn't come from the architecture but from training; what the architecture
guarantees is a route along which to train it. And the output after the blank line is the previous
section checked by hand: without the $\tanh$, row $1$ and row $8$ come out equal to the last decimal.

The second cell follows the two routes separately. It needs a loss, so it uses the cheapest one
possible (one whose gradient with respect to each $\mathbf{c}_i$ is a given vector), which means the
mechanism is the only thing being measured.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

# Needs the previous cell: S, H, Wsa, Wha, va, score, softmax_rows.
# Toy loss: l = sum_i G[i] . c_i, so the gradient of l with respect to c_i
# is exactly G[i], without differentiating the decoder or the cross-entropy.
G = np.random.default_rng(9).normal(size=(T_y, d_h))


def mix(H):
    alpha = softmax_rows(score(S, H))
    return alpha @ H, alpha


def loss(H):
    C, _ = mix(H)
    return float((G * C).sum())


C, alpha = mix(H)
via_value = alpha.T @ G                                         # route 1: as a term of c_i
dl_de = alpha * (G @ H.T - (G * C).sum(axis=1, keepdims=True))   # alpha_ij * G_i . (h_j - c_i)
P = (S @ Wsa.T)[:, None, :] + (H @ Wha.T)[None, :, :]
D = dl_de[:, :, None] * va * (1 - np.tanh(P) ** 2)              # (T_y, T_x, d_a)
via_score = np.einsum("ijk,kl->jl", D, Wha)                     # route 2: as an argument of a

num = np.zeros_like(H)                                          # numerical probe, central difference
for j in range(T_x):
    for l in range(d_h):
        Hplus = H.copy(); Hplus[j, l] += 1e-6
        Hminus = H.copy(); Hminus[j, l] -= 1e-6
        num[j, l] = (loss(Hplus) - loss(Hminus)) / 2e-6

print("norm of the value route:      %.4f" % np.linalg.norm(via_value))
print("norm of the score route:      %.4f" % np.linalg.norm(via_score))
print("difference, each on its own:  %.1e   %.1e"
      % (np.abs(via_value - num).max(), np.abs(via_score - num).max()))
print("difference, the two added:    %.1e" % np.abs(via_value + via_score - num).max())
`} />

The last line is the check: added together, the two routes reproduce the numerical gradient to the
order of $10^{-10}$, while on its own each one is wrong in the first digit. The norms tell you
something more, and it's worth being honest about it: with untrained weights, the value route is
eleven times larger than the score route. At the start, almost all of the gradient arrives along the
route it would take even if the alignment were uniform, and the small route, the one through $a$, is
the only one that can pull the map out of that uniformity. Change the scale of `va` to $2.0$ and run
both cells again: the scores spread apart, the weights sharpen and the two norms draw closer.

The last question leaves a bill unsettled. The two projections come out of the loop because each
depends on a single index, but between the sum and the read-out vector there's a $\tanh$, and it
forces the grid of $T_x \cdot T_y$ vectors of $d_a$ coordinates to be built in full. A sixty-token
sentence translated into sixty tokens asks for $3\,600$ of those vectors, and none of the operations
that produce them is a large matrix product: they're sums and $\tanh$s over a three-dimensional
block. The hardware that trains these networks is far better at one thing than at anything else, and
this isn't it.

There's another way to score a pair that avoids the whole problem, and its formula fits in five
symbols: Luong's attention, the multiplicative kind, the subject of
<Leccion slug="luong">the next lesson</Leccion>. Instead of projecting, adding and squashing, it
multiplies the two states through a matrix, so the complete grid of scores becomes *one* matrix
product. That lesson compares the two scores (what each costs, what each can do), and that's where the
shape of Block 5 starts to be decided.
