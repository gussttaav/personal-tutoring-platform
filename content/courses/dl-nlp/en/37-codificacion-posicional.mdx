      import numpy as np


      def frequencies(d_model):
          i = np.arange(d_model // 2)
          return 1.0 / (10000 ** (2 * i / d_model))


      def positional_encoding(T, d_model):
          omega = frequencies(d_model)
          pos = np.arange(T)[:, None]              # (T, 1) against (d_model/2,) -> (T, d_model/2)
          PE = np.zeros((T, d_model))
          PE[:, 0::2] = np.sin(pos * omega)
          PE[:, 1::2] = np.cos(pos * omega)
          return PE


      def shift(k, d_model):
          omega = frequencies(d_model)
          c, s = np.cos(k * omega), np.sin(k * omega)
          M = np.zeros((d_model, d_model))
          M[0::2, 0::2] = np.diag(c)
          M[0::2, 1::2] = np.diag(s)
          M[1::2, 0::2] = np.diag(-s)
          M[1::2, 1::2] = np.diag(c)
          return M

{/* COURSE-P5-05: Block 5, Lesson 5. COURSE-P11-08: English translation. */}

Multi-head attention completed the layer: $h$ ways of looking at the sentence, concatenated and
projected back for the price of one. What no number of heads can give $\mathbf{X}$ is a first row
and a last, so the order has to be written into every row before the layer reads it. What gets
written there is this lesson's subject.

The easy answers fail in three ways, and they are worth looking at because between them they
describe what is needed. Writing the position number into a coordinate breaks the scale: at position
$300$ that coordinate is worth $300$ while the rest of the vector moves between $-1$ and $1$, and it
flattens any dot product it enters. Dividing it by the sentence length, $\text{pos}/T$, fixes the
scale and spoils something else: the $0.5$ of an eight-token sentence would point at the fourth
token and that of a three-hundred-token text at the hundred and fiftieth, and it would be the same
coordinate with the same value. And learning one vector per position, a table like the embeddings',
works (some models do it) at the price of the table having exactly as many rows as positions seen
in training, and of positions $7$ and $8$ being two rows with no relation between them: nothing says
they are neighbours.

That last flaw is the one that points at what is really wanted. Think of an analogue clock. The
second hand goes round once a minute: it tells two consecutive seconds apart without trouble, but at
10:00:05 and at 10:01:05 it is in the same place. The hour hand does the opposite: from one second to
the next it barely moves, so it doesn't tell neighbours apart, but it never mistakes ten o'clock for
four. No hand on its own will do, and the three together pick out an instant without ambiguity
within twelve hours.

The clock has a second property as well, and it is the one this lesson ends up proving: letting ten
minutes pass turns each hand through a fixed angle, whatever the time. From 3:00 to 3:10 the minute
hand covers a sixth of a turn, and from 8:40 to 8:50 it covers exactly the same. The paper's encoding
is a handful of clocks like that, each with a single hand: each pair of coordinates is a hand, each
turns at its own speed, and the token's position plays the part of the time. The sine and the cosine
are, literally, where the tip is: measuring the angle from twelve o'clock, the sine says how far the
tip lies to the right and the cosine how far up.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
Each position is written as a handful of clocks turning at different speeds, and moving forward $k$
positions is turning them all through a fixed angle: the same turn at the start of the sentence as
at the end.
</Callout>

## A ladder of wavelengths

The paper defines the positional encoding entry by entry, with $\text{pos}$ as the index of the
position (it is the $t$ of the rest of the course, written with the paper's letter) and with
$\text{PE}$ for positional encoding:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{PE}_{(\text{pos},\,2i)} &= \sin\left(\frac{\text{pos}}{10\,000^{2i/d_{\text{model}}}}\right), \\[4pt]
\text{PE}_{(\text{pos},\,2i+1)} &= \cos\left(\frac{\text{pos}}{10\,000^{2i/d_{\text{model}}}}\right)
\end{aligned}
$$

for $i = 0, 1, \dots, d_{\text{model}}/2 - 1$. Read it slowly, because the structure is in the
indices: the coordinates come *in pairs*, pair $i$ takes up coordinates $2i$ and $2i+1$, and the two
share the same argument. Let's call that divisor, inverted, $\omega_i$; it is how far the pair turns
for each position you move forward:

$$
\omega_i = \frac{1}{10\,000^{2i/d_{\text{model}}}}, \qquad \frac{2\pi}{\omega_i} = 2\pi \cdot 10\,000^{2i/d_{\text{model}}}.
$$

On the right is the wavelength: how many positions that pair takes to come back to the same place.
Pair $0$ has $\omega_0 = 1$ and comes back every $2\pi \approx 6.28$ positions; the last one stays
below $10\,000 \cdot 2\pi$, because its exponent never reaches $1$: the last pair is
$i = d_{\text{model}}/2 - 1$, so the exponent stops at $1 - 2/d_{\text{model}}$. With
$d_{\text{model}} = 512$ that is about $60\,600$ positions, which over any text is a band that barely
moves. Between the two, the wavelengths grow by multiplying by the same factor every time: they form
a geometric progression, and that is the ladder the section is named after. On the clock, pair $0$ is
the second hand and the last pair an hour hand that takes far longer to go round.

What comes out of this is a matrix $\text{PE} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d_{\text{model}}}$ whose row
$\text{pos}$ is $\text{PE}_{\text{pos}}^{\top}$, exactly the shape of the $\mathbf{X}$ of the last
three lessons. And the two are joined by adding:

$$
\mathbf{x}_t = \mathbf{e}_{w_t} + \text{PE}_t.
$$

Notice that the first flaw has gone away on its own: sine and cosine live in $[-1, 1]$ whatever
happens to $T$, so the encoding of position $3$ and that of position $3\,000$ are the same size. So
has the second, for a different reason: $T$ appears nowhere in the formula, so position $5$ is
encoded the same way in a short sentence as in a long text.

Adding instead of concatenating is a decision, and what it costs deserves saying out loud.
Concatenating would keep the coordinates of meaning and those of position apart by construction, and
it would be paid for in width: setting aside $64$ of the $512$ for position leaves $448$ for
everything else. Adding costs no width at all, and in exchange the two signals share the same
coordinates and nothing guarantees they can be pulled apart again. What there is in its favour is
that $\mathbf{W}^Q$ and $\mathbf{W}^K$ are learned and can keep some directions and not others, and
that the encoding has a structure very much its own that a trained embedding has no reason to
imitate. Plausible, then; not proven.

### Shifting by a fixed distance is turning through a fixed angle

Of the three flaws from the start, the third remains, and it was the one that really asked for
something: that two neighbouring positions look alike, and look alike to the same degree wherever
they are. Here it can be proved, and it takes three lines.

Fix a shift $k$ and keep a single pair. The angle-addition formulas say what happens to that pair
when it moves forward $k$ positions:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sin\left(\omega_i(\text{pos}+k)\right) &= \cos(\omega_i k)\,\sin(\omega_i \text{pos}) + \sin(\omega_i k)\,\cos(\omega_i \text{pos}), \\[2pt]
\cos\left(\omega_i(\text{pos}+k)\right) &= -\sin(\omega_i k)\,\sin(\omega_i \text{pos}) + \cos(\omega_i k)\,\cos(\omega_i \text{pos}).
\end{aligned}
$$

I have written the factors in that order on purpose, because that way the two lines are already a
matrix product. The new coordinates are a combination of the two old ones, and the coefficients of
that combination are four numbers that look only at $\omega_i$ and $k$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\text{PE}_{(\text{pos}+k,\,2i)} \\[2pt] \text{PE}_{(\text{pos}+k,\,2i+1)}\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}\cos(\omega_i k) & \sin(\omega_i k) \\[2pt] -\sin(\omega_i k) & \cos(\omega_i k)\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}\text{PE}_{(\text{pos},\,2i)} \\[2pt] \text{PE}_{(\text{pos},\,2i+1)}\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Look at what is *not* in that matrix: $\text{pos}$. The starting position has stayed whole inside the
vector on the right, and the expansion has left it there. Since each pair is transformed without
touching the others, stacking the $d_{\text{model}}/2$ matrices, each $2 \times 2$, along the
diagonal gives a single matrix for the whole vector:

$$
\text{PE}_{\text{pos}+k} = \mathbf{M}_k\,\text{PE}_{\text{pos}}, \qquad \mathbf{M}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{model}}}.
$$

That is the property, and it is worth reading carefully what it says: there is *one* matrix for each
distance $k$, and that matrix holds for every position in the sentence. Moving forward three
positions is applying $\mathbf{M}_3$, whether you start from the first word or from the last. Each
block is also a rotation (its two columns are perpendicular and of length $1$), so $\mathbf{M}_k$
stretches and shrinks nothing: every row of $\text{PE}$ has the same length.

### What a score can see of all this

$\mathbf{M}_k$ is a property of the encoding, and the attention layer doesn't look at matrices: it
looks at dot products. It is worth working one out and seeing what is left. With
$\cos(A - B) = \cos A \cos B + \sin A \sin B$ applied pair by pair:

$$
\text{PE}_{\text{pos}}^{\top}\text{PE}_{\text{pos}+k} = \sum_{i} \left[\sin(\omega_i \text{pos})\sin\left(\omega_i(\text{pos}+k)\right) + \cos(\omega_i \text{pos})\cos\left(\omega_i(\text{pos}+k)\right)\right] = \sum_{i=0}^{d_{\text{model}}/2 - 1} \cos(\omega_i k).
$$

$\text{pos}$ has disappeared again. Two positions $k$ apart score the same number against each other
at the start of the sentence and in the middle, and that number depends only on the distance. With
$k = 0$ each term is $1$ and the sum gives $d_{\text{model}}/2$, which is the maximum: no position
scores as highly against another as against itself.

And now the concession, because this is less than it looks. The layer doesn't compute
$\text{PE}_{\text{pos}}^{\top}\text{PE}_{\text{pos}+k}$: it computes $\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{k}$
with both projected from $\mathbf{e}_{w_t} + \text{PE}_t$, so expanding the product gives four terms
and only one of them is purely positional. What the sinusoidal form guarantees is that this term
exists and depends only on the distance; a head that wants to learn to look for the determiner two
positions back (<W>the</W> in <W>the yellow house</W>, seen from <W>house</W>) has the material to
build it, because the signal for two positions back is the same throughout the sentence. Whether it
builds it is for training to decide, not this lesson.

That concession is the weakness RoPE (*rotary position embedding*) corrects, the encoding Su and
colleagues proposed in 2021 and that most large language models use today. The idea comes straight
out of $\mathbf{M}_k$: instead of adding the position to the input, where it mixes with the meaning
before the projections, it is applied as a rotation after them. The query at position $m$ is rotated,
pair by pair and with the same frequencies $\omega_i$, through an angle $\omega_i m$, and the key at
position $n$ through an angle $\omega_i n$. Rotating both vectors by the same amount doesn't change
their dot product, so when they are compared only the difference counts, $\omega_i(n - m)$: the
whole score, and not one of its four terms, depends on the positions only through the distance
between them.

## The ladder in NumPy

The first cell builds $\text{PE}$ with two slices and not a single loop, and then prints the
wavelength of a few pairs.

import numpy as np

T, d_model = 24, 32

print("PE", PE.shape, " values between", round(PE.min(), 3), "and", round(PE.max(), 3))

print("row 1 :", np.round(PE[1, :8], 3))

print("\\n  pair   omega          wavelength")

Row $0$ comes out as $(0, 1, 0, 1, \dots)$, which is what it has to be: at position $0$ every angle
is $0$ and every hand points at twelve. The first three rows of the table are the ladder: pair $0$
comes back every $6.3$ positions, pair $1$ every $11.2$, pair $2$ every $19.9$ (each $1.78$ times the
one before), and pair $15$, the last for this $d_{\text{model}}$, every $35\,332.9$, which over $24$
positions is a straight line. The last line is the observation from the end of the previous section,
checked: every row has length $4$, which is $\sqrt{32/2}$.

The second cell checks the two properties, which is where the content of the lesson lies.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

T, d_model, k = 24, 32, 3
i = np.arange(d_model // 2)
omega = 1.0 / (10000 ** (2 * i / d_model))
pos = np.arange(T + k)[:, None]
PE = np.zeros((T + k, d_model))
PE[:, 0::2] = np.sin(pos * omega)
PE[:, 1::2] = np.cos(pos * omega)

c, s = np.cos(k * omega), np.sin(k * omega)  # the four numbers of each block
M = np.zeros((d_model, d_model))
M[0::2, 0::2] = np.diag(c)
M[0::2, 1::2] = np.diag(s)
M[1::2, 0::2] = np.diag(-s)
M[1::2, 1::2] = np.diag(c)

shifted = PE[:T] @ M.T                       # M times a column == the row times M transposed
print("a single M moves all", T, "rows at once; maximum error",
      f"{np.abs(shifted - PE[k:T + k]).max():.2e}")

print("\\nPE_pos . PE_pos+k, from four different positions:")
print(" ", np.round([float(PE[p] @ PE[p + k]) for p in (0, 5, 11, 19)], 6))
print("  sum of cosines:", round(float(np.cos(k * omega).sum()), 6))

print("\\nand how it changes with the distance:")
for kk in (0, 1, 2, 3, 8, 20):
    print(f"  k = {kk:2d} -> {np.cos(kk * omega).sum():7.3f}")
`} />

The first line is $\mathbf{M}_k$ applied to all twenty-four positions at once, and the maximum error
stays of the order of $10^{-15}$: the same matrix, every row. The second is the other property (four
starting positions and four times $12.272398$, which is exactly the sum of cosines), and the table at
the end says how that quantity behaves as the two positions move apart: $16$ against itself,
$15.314$ one step away, $12.272$ at three, and after that it falls slowly and not monotonically,
because it is a sum of cosines and not a distance.

What the paper draws around the attention (a connection that skips the whole layer, a normalisation
and a position-wise perceptron) looks like plumbing around the important part, and it isn't. It is
what decides whether a signal put in at the bottom is still there at the top, and Block 3 has already
