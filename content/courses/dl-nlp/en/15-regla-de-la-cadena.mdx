      import numpy as np


      def jacobian(f, x):
          x = np.asarray(x, dtype=float)
          h = 1e-6
          columns = []
          for j in range(x.size):
              step = np.zeros_like(x)
              step[j] = h
              columns.append((f(x + step) - f(x - step)) / (2 * h))
          return np.stack(columns, axis=1)

          return g

{/* COURSE-P5-02 — Block 2, Lesson 7. */}

Of the three prerequisites the course starts from, one is the chain rule, and up to here the course
has differentiated with the version everyone learns first: two one-variable functions chained, two
derivatives multiplied. That version produced the two calculations the block has needed, the
correction of the output layer and the gradient of a lone neuron. The previous lesson, on gradient
descent, left training solved except for one piece: the step knows what to do with the gradient, and
for a network with hidden layers there is still no way to compute it. This lesson is that piece, and
it is only calculus.

Look at where it gets complicated, because it is not where it seems. Take the review network with
three answers instead of two (positive, negative and neutral, the classes with which <Leccion slug="funcion-de-perdida">the lesson on loss functions</Leccion> arrived at the softmax): eight inputs, four hidden neurons and three
output ones. Pick any weight of the first layer, say the one the first hidden neuron gives
<W>fun</W>, and move it a thousandth. Its neuron changes value: so far, the usual calculation. What
comes next is not, because that neuron feeds the three output neurons, all three enter the loss, and
all three have moved. One number touched, three effects that come out through different places and
meet at the end. In the one-variable rule there is nowhere to put that.

What has to be counted is not the neurons: it is the paths from a weight to the loss.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
The derivative does not choose a path: it runs along every path there is and keeps what they add up
to. Everything else in this lesson is bookkeeping: what multiplies within a path, and how paths are
stacked so that nobody writes them out one by one.
</Callout>

## When one variable arrives by several paths

Let's take the network out of the way. Let $\mathcal{L}$ be a function of $m$ variables
$u_1, \dots, u_m$, and let each of them be, in turn, a function of one and the same variable $t$.
Then $\mathcal{L}$ depends on $t$ even though it does not mention it, and its derivative is

$$
\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{dt} = \sum_{k=1}^{m} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial u_k}\,\frac{\partial u_k}{\partial t},
$$

one summand per intermediate variable. Each summand has the shape of the usual rule (how much
$\mathcal{L}$ responds to $u_k$, times how much $u_k$ responds to $t$), and what is new is that there
are $m$ of them and that they add. Within a path you multiply; across paths you add.

That sum is not a convention, and where it comes from is the only thing in the lesson that is not
bookkeeping. That $\mathcal{L}$ is differentiable at a point means that near it it resembles a
linear function of the displacements $\Delta u_1, \dots, \Delta u_m$, and a linear function of $m$
numbers is a sum of $m$ terms, one per number. The paths do not compete: each contributes its share,
and the shares add because the approximation they come from was already a sum.

<Details summary="The derivation, with the displacement written out">

Move $t$ by a small amount $\Delta t$. Each intermediate variable moves with it, and being
differentiable with respect to $t$,

$$
\Delta u_k = \frac{\partial u_k}{\partial t}\,\Delta t + o(\Delta t),
$$

where $o(\Delta t)$ collects what is left over and which, divided by $\Delta t$, tends to zero. The
differentiability of $\mathcal{L}$ at the point $(u_1, \dots, u_m)$ says the same thing one level up:
the increment of $\mathcal{L}$ is linear in the $m$ displacements, plus a remainder negligible
against the size of the total displacement,

$$
\Delta\mathcal{L} = \sum_{k=1}^{m} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial u_k}\,\Delta u_k + o\!\left(\lVert \Delta \mathbf{u} \rVert\right).
$$

Substitute the first into the second, divide everything by $\Delta t$ and let $\Delta t$ tend to
zero. The two remainders go away (the second because $\lVert \Delta \mathbf{u} \rVert$ is of the
order of $\Delta t$) and what is left is the formula above. The sum was in the second equation from
the start: it came in with the definition of the derivative, not with the chain rule.

</Details>

Now go back to the network with that in hand. The weight you were moving, the one the first hidden
neuron gives <W>fun</W>, is $\mathbf{W}^{(1)}_{13}$ (row $1$, that neuron's; column $3$, that
vocabulary entry's), and the intermediate variables are the three pre-activations of the output
layer, $z^{(2)}_1$, $z^{(2)}_2$ and $z^{(2)}_3$. There are two more links along the way, so each
summand has four factors:

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{W}^{(1)}_{13}}
= \sum_{k=1}^{3}
\underbrace{\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z^{(2)}_k}}_{\hat{y}_k - y_k}\;
\underbrace{\frac{\partial z^{(2)}_k}{\partial h^{(1)}_1}}_{\mathbf{W}^{(2)}_{k1}}\;
\underbrace{\frac{\partial h^{(1)}_1}{\partial z^{(1)}_1}}_{\varphi^{\prime}\left(z^{(1)}_1\right)}\;
\underbrace{\frac{\partial z^{(1)}_1}{\partial \mathbf{W}^{(1)}_{13}}}_{x_3}.
$$

None of the four is new. The first is the result with which <Leccion slug="funcion-de-perdida">the lesson on loss functions</Leccion> closed the output layer by putting softmax and cross-entropy together. The second
comes from $z^{(2)}_k$ being a sum in which $h^{(1)}_1$ appears once, multiplied by its weight. The
third is the derivative of the activation, tabulated for the three candidates in <Leccion slug="funciones-activacion">the lesson on activation functions and non-linearity</Leccion>. And the fourth is the previous lesson's: the
pre-activation is linear in its weights, so the derivative with respect to one of them is the input
that goes with it. The only thing missing was knowing that the three paths add.

## The object that replaces the derivative

That formula serves one weight. The network has $51$, and fifty-one sums with indices are not an
answer: we need to go up a level.

Let $\mathbf{u} : \mathbb{R}^{n} \to \mathbb{R}^{m}$. Its derivative at a point is the **Jacobian**,
the matrix that gathers every partial derivative of every output with respect to every input,

$$
\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial \mathbf{x}} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n},
\qquad
\left[\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial \mathbf{x}}\right]_{ij} = \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j},
$$

with one row per output coordinate and one column per input coordinate. That order, the output
first, is the whole course's, and from it come the two things this block does with a Jacobian.

The first is that composing is multiplying again. If $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ produces
$\mathbf{u} \in \mathbb{R}^{m}$ and this in turn produces $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^{p}$, then

$$
\frac{\partial \mathbf{v}}{\partial \mathbf{x}} = \frac{\partial \mathbf{v}}{\partial \mathbf{u}}\,\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial \mathbf{x}},
$$

a product of a $p \times m$ matrix by an $m \times n$ one that delivers the $p \times n$ that is
needed. The dimension that is run over and disappears is $m$, that of the intermediate variables,
exactly as in <Leccion slug="forward-pass">the forward pass</Leccion> the width shared by two layers disappeared.

And that product is not a new object, it is the previous one stacked. Expand the entry $(i, j)$ with
the definition of the matrix product:

$$
\left[\frac{\partial \mathbf{v}}{\partial \mathbf{x}}\right]_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{m} \frac{\partial v_i}{\partial u_k}\,\frac{\partial u_k}{\partial x_j},
$$

which is the sum over paths from the previous section, written for output $i$ and input $j$: the $m$
routes from $x_j$ to $v_i$, one per intermediate variable you can pass through. The sum over paths
and the matrix product are the same calculation, and the second one the computer does without
anyone writing an index.

### The loss is a scalar, and that is where the transpose comes from

The second thing comes from looking at the end of the chain. $\mathcal{L}$ delivers a single number,
so in its case $m = 1$ and its Jacobian is a one-row matrix,
$\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times n}$. The gradients of this course
are columns, like all its vectors, so one is the transpose of the other,
$\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L} = \left(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{x}\right)^{\top}$.
Transposing the composition above (and remembering that transposing a product reverses the order of
the factors),

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L} = \left(\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial \mathbf{x}}\right)^{\top} \nabla_{\mathbf{u}}\mathcal{L},
$$

an $n \times m$ matrix by a vector of $m$ coordinates that returns the vector of $n$ that was
wanted. That is the shape of everything left in the block: a gradient comes in on the right, leaves
converted into the gradient of the layer before, and the transposed Jacobian is what does the
conversion.

Keep hold of an observation that is not used here. A chain of Jacobians can be evaluated by first
multiplying the matrices among themselves, or by carrying the output's gradient backwards through
one transpose after another. They give the same, because the matrix product is associative; they do
not cost the same, because the first multiplies matrices by matrices and the second only matrices by
vectors.

### A coordinate-wise activation has a diagonal Jacobian

One concrete Jacobian is missing, the activation's, and it comes out far cheaper than its size
suggests. With $\mathbf{h} = \varphi(\mathbf{z})$ applied coordinate by coordinate, each output looks
at a single input: $h_i$ depends on $z_i$ and on nothing else, so $\partial h_i / \partial z_j = 0$
as soon as $i \neq j$. Of the $m \times m$ matrix the diagonal survives:

$$
\frac{\partial \mathbf{h}}{\partial \mathbf{z}} = \text{diag}\left(\varphi^{\prime}(\mathbf{z})\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m},
$$

where $\text{diag}(\mathbf{v})$ is the square matrix carrying $\mathbf{v}$ on its diagonal and zeros
everywhere else. Multiplying by it is multiplying coordinate by coordinate, which is written with a
symbol of its own, the **Hadamard product**:

$$
\text{diag}(\mathbf{v})\,\mathbf{g} = \mathbf{v} \odot \mathbf{g}.
$$

From there on nobody builds the matrix: it is $m$ products instead of $m^{2}$, and $m$ numbers
stored instead of $m^{2}$ of which $m^{2} - m$ are zeros. With ReLU (rectified linear unit) the
diagonal is $0$ or $1$ and it is a mask: a hidden neuron with a negative pre-activation puts a zero
in its entry, and that zero cuts every path that went through it. It is the dying ReLU of <Leccion slug="funciones-activacion">the lesson on activation functions</Leccion>, seen from the other side.

The softmax is the exception, and it has to be said: it mixes every coordinate, so its Jacobian has
not a single zero. It is not needed here, because <Leccion slug="funcion-de-perdida">the lesson on loss functions</Leccion> already differentiated it together with
cross-entropy, and out of the two together came $\hat{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbf{y}$, the gradient the
chain starts from.

## One weight, three paths and three ways of measuring them

The cell puts the review <W>the movie is fun and i recommend it</W> into the network of eight
inputs, four hidden neurons and three outputs, and computes
$\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{W}^{(1)}_{13}$ (the same weight as in the previous section) in
three ways: a numerical probe, the sum of the three paths and the product of the Jacobians. I set
the weights by hand, so you will see these numbers and no others. Run it and compare the three
figures, and then look at the fourth.

Go back to the previous section's formula, but with the four factors stacked into their objects: the
summation sign disappears inside the matrix product.

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{W}^{(1)}_{13}}
= \underbrace{(\hat{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbf{y})^{\top}}_{(1,\ 3)}\;
\underbrace{\mathbf{W}^{(2)}}_{(3,\ 4)}\;
\underbrace{\text{diag}\!\left(\varphi^{\prime}(\mathbf{z}^{(1)})\right)}_{(4,\ 4)}\;
\underbrace{x_3\,\mathbf{e}_1}_{(4,\ 1)}.
$$

The diagonal is written out in full (not as the previous section's cheap Hadamard product) because
here the aim is to show the Jacobian as it is, not to save arithmetic; $\mathbf{e}_1 \in
\mathbb{R}^{4}$ is $1$ at hidden neuron $1$ and $0$ at the other three, the only one that
$\mathbf{W}^{(1)}_{13}$ moves. What does save is the order: evaluated from left to right (row times
matrix, three times) nothing larger than a row of four numbers ever appears; it is the observation
the transpose section set aside, applied here. It is step 3 of the cell.

All three agree at $-0.09208080$, and the probe is the one with the least right to: it knows nothing
about the network, it only evaluates the loss with the weight moved a hundred-thousandth up and a
hundred-thousandth down. That it gives the same as the chain is the check worth keeping to hand when
a derivative written by hand does not add up.

The fourth figure is the one that teaches what the lesson is about. Forgetting a path (the third,
the one through the output neuron of the neutral class) leaves the derivative at $-0.16541038$,
almost double what it is worth. And look at the three summands, because the error is not one of
size: the first two are negative and the third is **positive**. That path was pulling the other way,
and removing it does not trim the correction, it enlarges it. A forgotten path does not give an
approximate derivative: it gives the derivative of another function.

Everything needed to differentiate any network is now here, and put like this it is no use. The cell
has computed the derivative of **one** weight, and to get it, it has walked the whole chain: the
error of the output layer, the second layer's matrix, the diagonal of the activation. The weight next
to it needs that same walk with a single different factor at the end, and so do the other forty-nine
of this network. With a vocabulary of twenty thousand entries and a hundred and twenty-eight hidden
neurons that is upwards of two and a half million walks, identical except for the last step, and not
even the fastest computer in the world has time for that.

The waste is plain as soon as it is named: those walks share almost everything, and what is shared
is recomputed once per parameter. What is needed is to reverse the direction of travel: compute
once, starting from the last layer, how much the loss owes each pre-activation of the network, and
let each weight read its derivative off a quantity that is already there. That procedure has a name
of its own, it is derived layer by layer and with the indices in view, and it is <Leccion slug="backpropagation">the next lesson, on backpropagation</Leccion>.
