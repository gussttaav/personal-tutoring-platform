{/* ── 1. MOTIVATION — no heading ── */}

A neural network cannot be handed the word <W>hello</W>. At bottom, a neural network is a mathematical function that operates on vectors of real numbers. Its computations assume that the inputs belong to a space where notions like "distance", "direction" and "closeness" are well defined, and text, on its own, does not live in a space like that. Between <W>apple</W> and <W>cat</W> there is no intermediate point, no direction that leads from one word to the other, no step halfway between the two.

And yet language models work. That is the challenge in one line: to close the gap between a text and a function over real numbers. Solving it is what this block is for, because the decisions taken here shape everything that comes afterwards: tokenisation, the vocabulary, embeddings and, in the end, the architecture of the model itself.

{/* ── 2. INTUITION — no heading ── */}

So you need a translator: a function that turns text into real vectors, placed *before* the network, so that the network only ever sees numerical representations. The uncomfortable part is that this translator is not neutral. The moment you put words into a numerical space, that space starts making claims about them: which are close, which are far, which lie halfway between. You cannot avoid making those claims. You can only choose to make them on purpose or by accident.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
Representing text is not encoding it: it is choosing a geometry for meaning. The question is not whether that geometry expresses relations, but whether it expresses the right ones.
</Callout>

This is what makes the problem interesting rather than a formality. Take any translator you like: numbering the words alphabetically, say. It produces perfectly valid numbers, and it destroys every notion of meaning.

{/* ── 3. FORMALISATION — heading, by content ── */}

## What exactly you have to hand a network

Fix the vocabulary, the set of units the model recognises, and call it $V$. For now "unit" means
"word"; <Leccion slug="tokenizacion">the lesson on tokenisation</Leccion> will show that this decision is more delicate than it
looks. A text is then a finite sequence of elements of $V$:

$$
s = (w_1, w_2, \dots, w_T), \qquad w_t \in V, \quad T \in \mathbb{N}.
$$

A network, by contrast, is a function between spaces of real numbers. We can write down its type
without yet knowing anything about what it does inside: it takes a vector of a fixed size and returns
another,

$$
f : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^k,
$$

where $d$ is the dimension of the input space and $k$ that of the output space. What happens between
input and output (the layers, the weights, the training) is the subject of the next block. Here, all
that matters is that the network has to be handed vectors from $\mathbb{R}^d$.

The problem shows up at once: a text is a sequence of symbols, and what $f$ asks for is a vector
in $\mathbb{R}^d$. Nothing turns an element of $V$ into an element of $\mathbb{R}^d$ on its own. You
need a function to close that gap:

$$
r : V \to \mathbb{R}^d,
$$

and with it every word of the sequence becomes a vector, so the whole text turns into a matrix of
numbers, one row per position and $d$ columns. The vectors in this course are columns, so stacking
them lays each one on its side, as a row:

$$
\mathbf{X} =
\begin{pmatrix}
r(w_1) \\
r(w_2) \\
\vdots \\
r(w_T)
\end{pmatrix}
\in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d}.
$$

This whole block is about how to build $r$. Before proposing one, settle what you are going to demand
of it. Three things.

**Totality.** $r$ has to be defined for any word that arrives in production, not only for the ones
you saw when you built it. The day somebody writes <W>cryptocurrency</W> and that word was not in $V$,
the function still has to return something. This is the demand that sounds bureaucratic and turns out
to be the most expensive of the three: it is the problem of words outside the vocabulary
(*out-of-vocabulary*, OOV), and further on it gets <Leccion slug="vocabulario-oov">a whole lesson of its own, on the OOV problem</Leccion>.

**Fixed dimension.** $r(w) \in \mathbb{R}^d$, with the same $d$ for every $w$. The network is designed
to expect vectors of a definite size; a representation that sometimes returned a vector of five
numbers and sometimes of nine would not fit through the front door.

**Determinism.** The same word always produces the same vector. If $r$ changed between two runs, what
the model learned would stop matching what it receives.

<Callout type="note" title="What is NOT demanded">
$r$ does not have to be injective. It is tempting to ask that two distinct words never share a
vector, but you will give that up on purpose more than once: in <Leccion slug="vocabulario-oov">the lesson on the OOV problem</Leccion>, every rare
word will share a single vector, and subword representations will make <W>cat</W> and <W>cats</W>
share almost everything. Losing some information is often what makes a model generalise.
</Callout>

### The representation that looks like it works and doesn't

With those three conditions on the table, the shortest proposal is to sort the vocabulary and keep
the index. With $V = \{\textit{apple}, \textit{cat}, \textit{dog}\}$ in alphabetical order:

$$
r_{\text{ord}}(\textit{apple}) = 1, \qquad r_{\text{ord}}(\textit{cat}) = 2, \qquad r_{\text{ord}}(\textit{dog}) = 3.
$$

Suppose for a moment that $V$ is closed, that no new word will ever arrive. With that concession, and
only with it, $r_{\text{ord}}$ is total; it also has fixed dimension $d = 1$ and is deterministic. The
concession is a big one: the moment somebody writes <W>cryptocurrency</W>, $r_{\text{ord}}$ has no
index to return, and that is exactly the fault line <Leccion slug="vocabulario-oov">the lesson on the OOV problem</Leccion> runs along. We park it
here on purpose, because what we want to see is what happens *even when* the three conditions hold.
And what happens is that the representation is bad anyway, because satisfying them was never all it
took. Putting the words into $\mathbb{R}$ makes them inherit its structure, and that structure asserts
things:

$$
\lvert\, r_{\text{ord}}(\textit{apple}) - r_{\text{ord}}(\textit{cat}) \,\rvert = 1 \;<\; \lvert\, r_{\text{ord}}(\textit{apple}) - r_{\text{ord}}(\textit{dog}) \,\rvert = 2.
$$

In words: <W>apple</W> is more like <W>cat</W> than like <W>dog</W>. It also says that <W>cat</W> lies
between the other two, and that the exact midpoint of the road from <W>apple</W> to <W>dog</W> is
<W>cat</W>. You made none of those three claims. They come out of the alphabet, which is an order
invented for looking things up in a dictionary and knows nothing about meaning.

What matters is not that the claims are false, but that the network has no way of knowing they
are. All it receives are the numbers; to the network, an invented geometry looks exactly like a true
one, and it cannot tell them apart. You have handed it false information dressed up as good
information.

And the flaw is not specific to this representation. You could rescale the indices by
dividing by $\lvert V \rvert$, and nothing would change: dividing by a constant preserves the order
and preserves the ratios between distances, so <W>cat</W> would still lie between <W>apple</W> and
<W>dog</W>. Any $r$ with $d = 1$ carries the same underlying problem, because $\mathbb{R}$ is
totally ordered and words are not. Getting out of it means going up in dimension, and that is the
argument that leads to <Leccion slug="one-hot">the lesson on one-hot encoding</Leccion>.

<Details summary="How many dimensions it takes not to lie">
Ask for the least you possibly can: that the representation assert *nothing*, that no word sit closer
to one word than to any other. In other words, that every pairwise distance be equal.

In $\mathbb{R}$ that is already impossible with three words. If $a < b < c$, then
$\lvert a - c \rvert > \lvert a - b \rvert$: there is always one that ends up in the middle. In the
plane it can be done, with an equilateral triangle, but only with three. Four equidistant points
force you out into space, and in general $n$ mutually equidistant points need $n - 1$ dimensions.
</Details>

## Check it yourself, on one sentence

Take the sentence <W>the cat drinks milk</W> and the alphabetically ordered vocabulary
$V = \{\textit{cat}, \textit{drinks}, \textit{milk}, \textit{the}\}$, with indices $1$ to $4$. The
sentence becomes the sequence $(4, 1, 2, 3)$, and with $d = 1$ the matrix $\mathbf{X}$ from the
previous section has shape $4 \times 1$. Now answer three questions looking *only* at those numbers,
the way the network looks at them:

1. By the numbers, which two words of the sentence are most alike? Compare $\lvert 1 - 2 \rvert,
   \lvert 2 - 3 \rvert$ and the rest of the distances, and do not look for a single winner: three
   pairs are tied at distance $1$ (<W>cat</W> and <W>drinks</W>, <W>drinks</W> and <W>milk</W>,
   <W>milk</W> and <W>the</W>), and you have not miscounted. Does that tie match what you know about
   those words?
2. Which word falls at the midpoint between <W>cat</W> $(1)$ and <W>milk</W> $(3)$? Does that question
   mean anything outside the numbers?
3. Add <W>bird</W> to the vocabulary. Sort alphabetically again and reassign the indices. How many of
   the original words change number, even though the sentence has not changed at all?

The third is the most uncomfortable: a new word rewrites the representation of words that have
nothing to do with it, and a model trained on the earlier $V$ is left useless. Notice that this is a
different flaw from the other two. It is not the invented geometry, and it is not the OOV problem
either: <W>bird</W> does end up with an index, and the vocabulary grows without difficulty. What
fails is that the representation is not **stable**: it depends on the whole of $V$ at once, so
touching one entry moves every other. Hold on to that observation. In <Leccion slug="one-hot">the lesson on one-hot encoding</Leccion> it is
what makes the jump to a far larger space look reasonable despite the cost.

The problem the rest of the block lives on is now stated: we need a function $r : V \to \mathbb{R}^d$
that is total, of fixed dimension and deterministic, and that, on top of all that, carries a geometry
which does not accidentally assert false things about words. We already know that $d = 1$ will not do.

But there is an earlier question we have been treating as settled every time we write $V$ as though
it were obvious: before deciding which vector belongs to <W>unhappiness</W>, you have to decide
whether <W>unhappiness</W> is one unit of the vocabulary, or three (<W>un</W>, <W>happi</W>,
<W>ness</W>), or eleven letters. That decision fixes the size of $V$, defines what it means for a word
to be "new" and, in the end, bounds what the model can learn. That is <Leccion slug="tokenizacion">the next lesson, on tokenisation</Leccion>.
