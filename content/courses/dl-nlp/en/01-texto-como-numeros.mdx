{/* ── 1. MOTIVATION — no heading ── */}

A neural network cannot be handed the word <W>hello</W>. In essence, a neural network is a mathematical function that operates on vectors of real numbers. Its computations presuppose that the inputs belong to a space where notions like "distance", "direction" and "closeness" are well defined. Text, on its own, doesn't live in a space of that kind. Between <W>apple</W> and <W>cat</W> there is no intermediate point, no direction leading from one word to the other, no step halfway between the two.

And yet language models work. The challenge is exactly this: to close the gap between a text and a function over real numbers. Solving that problem is what this block is for, because the decisions taken here determine everything that comes afterwards — tokenisation, the vocabulary, embeddings and, ultimately, the architecture of the model itself.

{/* ── 2. INTUITION — no heading ── */}

So you need a translator: a function that turns text into real vectors, placed *before* the network, so that the network only ever processes numerical representations. The uncomfortable part is that this
translator isn't neutral. The moment you put words into a numerical space, that space starts making
claims about them: which are close, which are far, which lie halfway between. You can't avoid making
those claims. You can only choose to make them on purpose or by accident.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
Representing text isn't encoding it: it's choosing a geometry for meaning. The question isn't whether that geometry expresses relations, but whether it expresses the right ones.
</Callout>

This is what makes the problem interesting rather than a formality. Any old translator — numbering
the words alphabetically, say — produces perfectly valid numbers, and destroys every notion of
meaning.

{/* ── 3. FORMALISATION — heading, by content ── */}

## What exactly you have to hand a network

Fix the vocabulary, the set of units the model recognises, and call it $V$. For now "unit" means
"word"; <Leccion slug="tokenizacion">the lesson on tokenisation</Leccion> will show that the decision is more delicate than it
looks. A text is then a finite sequence of elements of $V$:

$$
s = (w_1, w_2, \dots, w_T), \qquad w_t \in V, \quad T \in \mathbb{N}.
$$

A network, by contrast, is a function between spaces of real numbers. We can write down its type
without yet knowing anything about what it does inside: it takes a vector of a fixed size and returns
another,

$$
f : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^k,
$$

where $d$ is the dimension of the input space and $k$ that of the output space. What happens between
the input and the output — the layers, the weights, the training — is the subject of the next block; here all
that matters is that the network needs to be handed vectors from $\mathbb{R}^d$.

The problem shows up immediately: a text is a sequence of symbols, and what $f$ asks for is a vector
in $\mathbb{R}^d$. Nothing turns an element of $V$ into an element of $\mathbb{R}^d$ on its own. You
need a function to close that gap:

$$
r : V \to \mathbb{R}^d,
$$

and with it every word of the sequence becomes a vector, so the whole text turns into a matrix of
numbers, one row per position and $d$ columns. The vectors in this course are columns, so stacking
them writes each one lying down, as a row:

$$
\mathbf{X} =
\begin{pmatrix}
r(w_1) \\
r(w_2) \\
\vdots \\
r(w_T)
\end{pmatrix}
\in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d}.
$$

This whole block is about how to build $r$. Before proposing one, settle what you're going to demand
of it. Three things.

**Totality.** $r$ has to be defined for any word that arrives in production, not only for the ones
you saw when you built it. The day somebody writes <W>cryptocurrency</W> and that word wasn't in $V$,
the function still has to return something. This is the demand that looks bureaucratic and turns out
to be the most expensive of the three: it is the problem of words outside the vocabulary
(*out-of-vocabulary*, OOV), and further on it gets <Leccion slug="vocabulario-oov">a whole lesson of its own, on the OOV problem</Leccion>.

**Fixed dimension.** $r(w) \in \mathbb{R}^d$, with the same $d$ for every $w$. The network is designed
expecting vectors of a definite size; a representation that sometimes returned a vector of five
numbers and sometimes of nine wouldn't fit through the front door.

**Determinism.** The same word always produces the same vector. If $r$ changed between two runs, what
the model learned would stop matching what it receives.

<Callout type="note" title="What is NOT demanded">
$r$ doesn't have to be injective. It's tempting to ask that two distinct words never share a
vector, but you will give that up on purpose more than once: in <Leccion slug="vocabulario-oov">the lesson on the OOV problem</Leccion>, every rare
word will share a single vector, and subword representations will make <W>cat</W> and <W>cats</W>
share almost everything. Losing some information is often what makes a model generalise.
</Callout>

### The representation that looks like it works and doesn't

With those three conditions on the table, the shortest proposal is to sort the vocabulary and keep
the index. With $V = \{\textit{apple}, \textit{cat}, \textit{dog}\}$ in alphabetical order:

$$
r_{\text{ord}}(\textit{apple}) = 1, \qquad r_{\text{ord}}(\textit{cat}) = 2, \qquad r_{\text{ord}}(\textit{dog}) = 3.
$$

Suppose for a moment that $V$ is closed, that no new word will ever arrive. With that concession, and
only with it, $r_{\text{ord}}$ is total; it also has fixed dimension $d = 1$ and is deterministic. The
concession is a big one: the moment somebody writes <W>cryptocurrency</W>, $r_{\text{ord}}$ has no
index to return, and that's exactly the crack <Leccion slug="vocabulario-oov">the lesson on the OOV problem</Leccion> lives on. We park it
here deliberately, because what we want to see is what happens *even when* the three conditions hold.
And what happens is that the representation is bad anyway, because satisfying them was never all it
took. Putting the words into $\mathbb{R}$ makes them inherit its structure, and that structure asserts
things:

$$
\lvert\, r_{\text{ord}}(\textit{apple}) - r_{\text{ord}}(\textit{cat}) \,\rvert = 1 \;<\; \lvert\, r_{\text{ord}}(\textit{apple}) - r_{\text{ord}}(\textit{dog}) \,\rvert = 2.
$$

Read aloud: <W>apple</W> is more like <W>cat</W> than like <W>dog</W>. And also that <W>cat</W> lies
between the other two, and that the midpoint of the road from <W>apple</W> to <W>dog</W> is exactly
<W>cat</W>. You made none of those three claims. They come out of the alphabet, which is an order
invented for looking things up in a dictionary and which knows nothing about meaning.

What matters is not that the claims are false, but that the network has no way of knowing that they
are. All it receives are the numbers; to the network, an invented geometry looks exactly like a true
one, and it can't tell them apart. You've handed it false information dressed as good information.

And the flaw doesn't belong to this representation in particular. You could rescale the indices by
dividing by $\lvert V \rvert$, and nothing would change: dividing by a constant preserves the order
and preserves the ratios between distances, so <W>cat</W> would still lie between <W>apple</W> and
<W>dog</W>. Any $r$ with $d = 1$ drags the same underlying problem behind it, because $\mathbb{R}$ is
totally ordered and words are not. Getting out of it means going up in dimension, and that's the
argument that leads to <Leccion slug="one-hot">the lesson on one-hot encoding</Leccion>.

<Details summary="How many dimensions it takes not to lie">
Ask for the least you can ask for: that the representation assert *nothing*, that no word be closer
to one word than to any other. That is, that all the pairwise distances be equal.

In $\mathbb{R}$ that's already impossible with three words. If $a < b < c$, then
$\lvert a - c \rvert > \lvert a - b \rvert$: there's always one that ends up in the middle. In the
plane it can be done — an equilateral triangle — but only with three. Four equidistant points demand
going out into space, and in general $n$ mutually equidistant points need $n - 1$ dimensions.
</Details>

{/* ── 4. CONCRETE — by hand, heading by content ── */}

## Check it yourself, on one sentence

Take the sentence <W>the cat drinks milk</W> and the alphabetically ordered vocabulary
$V = \{\textit{cat}, \textit{drinks}, \textit{milk}, \textit{the}\}$, with indices $1$ to $4$. The
sentence becomes the sequence $(4, 1, 2, 3)$, and with $d = 1$ the matrix $\mathbf{X}$ from the
previous section has shape $4 \times 1$. Now answer three questions looking *only* at those numbers,
the way the network looks at them:

1. By the numbers, which two words of the sentence are most alike? Compare $\lvert 1 - 2 \rvert,
   \lvert 2 - 3 \rvert$ and the rest of the distances, and don't look for a single winner: three
   pairs are tied at distance $1$ — <W>cat</W> and <W>drinks</W>, <W>drinks</W> and <W>milk</W>,
   <W>milk</W> and <W>the</W> — and you haven't miscounted. Does that tie match what you know about
   those words?
2. Which word falls at the midpoint between <W>cat</W> $(1)$ and <W>milk</W> $(3)$? Does that question
   mean anything outside the numbers?
3. Add <W>bird</W> to the vocabulary. Sort alphabetically again and reassign the indices. How many of
   the original words change number, even though the sentence hasn't changed at all?

The third is the most uncomfortable: a new word rewrites the representation of words that have
nothing to do with it. A model trained on the earlier $V$ is left useless. Notice that this is a
different flaw from the other two: it isn't the invented geometry, and it isn't the OOV problem
either — <W>bird</W> does end up with an index, the vocabulary grows without difficulty. What fails is
that the representation is not **stable**: it depends on the whole of $V$ at once, so touching one
entry moves every other. Hold on to that observation, because it's what will make the jump to a far
larger space look reasonable despite its cost in <Leccion slug="one-hot">the lesson on one-hot encoding</Leccion>.

The problem the rest of the block lives on is now stated: we need a function $r : V \to \mathbb{R}^d$
that is total, of fixed dimension and deterministic, and that on top of all that carries a geometry
which doesn't assert false things about words by accident. We already know that $d = 1$ won't do.

But there's an earlier question we've taken as settled by writing $V$ as though it were obvious:
before deciding which vector belongs to <W>unhappiness</W>, you have to decide whether
<W>unhappiness</W> is one unit of the vocabulary, or three (<W>un</W>, <W>happi</W>, <W>ness</W>), or
eleven letters. That decision fixes the size of $V$, defines what it means for a word to be "new"
and, ultimately, bounds what the model can end up learning. That is <Leccion slug="tokenizacion">the next lesson, on tokenisation</Leccion>.
