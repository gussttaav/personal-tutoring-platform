The block has built a complete mechanism and two different ways of filling its one empty slot: the
additive score and the multiplicative one. What it hasn't yet done is stop to look at the vocabulary
all of that is written in. Every equation of the last three lessons talks about an encoder that
reads, a decoder that writes, states with a bar and states without one. That vocabulary comes from
one particular machine (two recurrent networks chained together to translate), and this lesson asks
how much of it attention actually needs.

Do it the blunt way: cross out of the equations everything that talks about recurrence and see
what's left standing. What remains is a vector that asks, a list of vectors it's compared against,
and a list of vectors that get mixed, with a softmax between the two lists and a weighted sum at the
end. Not one of those pieces mentions a recurrence, a language or a translation. They're three roles
and one operation, each with a name that was fixed years ago and an initial that the next block puts
in every one of its equations, starting from the first line of the paper that introduced them.

You've already written an operation that does almost exactly this. In the challenges of
<Leccion slug="lstm">the lesson on the long short-term memory (LSTM)</Leccion> and
<Leccion slug="gru">the lesson on the gated recurrent unit (GRU)</Leccion>, the weights arrived in a
dictionary `P`, and you took each piece out with something like `P["z"]`. You give a **query**,
Python compares it with the stored **keys** and hands back the **value** of the one that matches. Get
one letter wrong and there's no answer.

Attention is that lookup with the requirement to match taken away. The query doesn't have to equal
any key: it's compared with all of them, each comparison becomes a weight, and what comes back isn't
one value but a mixture of all of them, each in the proportion its weight gives it. A dictionary
returns one of its values; attention returns a combination of all of them. So far in this block, the
three roles have been dealt out like this: the decoder state asks, and each source position supplies
the other two, what the query is compared with and what gets poured into the mixture.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
Attention is a dictionary lookup with the obligation to get the key right taken away: the query is
compared with every key, and what comes back is the mixture of all the values.
</Callout>

## Three roles, three arguments

Take the three roles as three objects, without saying where they come from: a **query**
$\mathbf{q}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d_k}$, and $T_x$ pairs of **key** and **value**,
$\mathbf{k}_j \in \mathbb{R}^{d_k}$ and $\mathbf{v}_j \in \mathbb{R}^{d_v}$, one pair per position.
The query and the keys have the same width because they're going to be compared with each other; the
values don't have to match that width. With that, an attention call is three steps:

$$
e_{ij} = a\left(\mathbf{q}_i, \mathbf{k}_j\right),
\qquad
\alpha_{ij} = \frac{\exp(e_{ij})}{\sum_{m=1}^{T_x} \exp(e_{im})},
\qquad
\mathbf{c}_i = \sum_{j=1}^{T_x} \alpha_{ij}\,\mathbf{v}_j \in \mathbb{R}^{d_v}.
$$

This is the mechanism from <Leccion slug="la-idea-de-atencion">the lesson on the idea of attention</Leccion>
under other names: it scores each pair, normalises the row with a softmax over the positions, and
mixes the values with those weights. The index in the denominator changes letter ($m$ where $k$ used
to be) because from here on $k$ belongs to the keys.

Look at the shapes, because that's where the rewrite starts to pay off. The output has $d_v$
coordinates: the size of a *value*, not that of the query and not the number of pairs. $d_k$ lives
entirely inside the comparison and dies there, in the number $e_{ij}$; $T_x$ lives in the row of
weights and dies in the sum. So the list of pairs can grow as long as it likes without the slot the
result lands in noticing. This is the shape property from <Leccion slug="la-idea-de-atencion">that
lesson</Leccion>, now stated without naming any recurrence.

The last three lessons are a special case of this, with no equation changed:
$\mathbf{q}_i = \mathbf{s}_{i-1}$, and $\mathbf{k}_j = \mathbf{v}_j = \bar{\mathbf{h}}_j$, with
$d_k = d_v = d_h$. The function $a$ is whichever was chosen (Bahdanau's additive score or Luong's
multiplicative one), and the softmax and the weighted sum are the same as before. All that has
happened is that each piece now has a name that doesn't mention what produced it.

### The key and the value are the same vector, and no equation asks for it

Tying the two roles to one vector has a price: it forces the $d_h$ coordinates of
$\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j$ to serve both purposes at once, to be good for being compared and good for
being read.

The gradient's bookkeeping shows it better than the formula does. Untied, each role appears in a
single place, so <Leccion slug="regla-de-la-cadena">the chain rule</Leccion> follows a single route
for each, summing over the $T_y$ steps because both lists are used in all of them:

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{v}_j}\ell = \sum_{i=1}^{T_y} \alpha_{ij}\,\nabla_{\mathbf{c}_i}\ell,
\qquad
\nabla_{\mathbf{k}_j}\ell = \sum_{i=1}^{T_y} \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial e_{ij}}\,\nabla_{\mathbf{k}_j} e_{ij},
$$

and the step through the softmax is the one from
<Leccion slug="bahdanau">the lesson on additive attention</Leccion>, with one difference: it now says
precisely what is compared with what:

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial e_{ij}} = \alpha_{ij}\left(\nabla_{\mathbf{c}_i}\ell\right)^{\top}\left(\mathbf{v}_j - \mathbf{c}_i\right).
$$

What gets compared with the mixture is that position's *value*; what receives the correction is its
*key*. With one vector playing both roles, that sentence couldn't even be written.

<Details summary="And tying them back together gives the two-route formula again">

Setting $\mathbf{k}_j = \mathbf{v}_j = \bar{\mathbf{h}}_j$ isn't a new case for the gradient: it's
a shared weight, like the $\mathbf{W}_{hh}$ that runs across every position in the previous block's
recurrence, or the $\mathbf{W}_a$ that scores every pair. An object that appears in two places
receives the sum of what reaches it through each, so the two routes above add up:

$$
\nabla_{\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j}\ell = \sum_{i=1}^{T_y} \alpha_{ij}\,\nabla_{\mathbf{c}_i}\ell + \sum_{i=1}^{T_y} \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial e_{ij}}\,\nabla_{\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j} e_{ij},
$$

which is, term by term, the two-route line from
<Leccion slug="bahdanau">the lesson on additive attention</Leccion>. There, the second term was
expanded for the additive score as $\mathbf{W}_{ha}^{\top}\nabla_{\mathbf{p}_{ij}}\ell$, which is this
same term with $\nabla_{\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j} e_{ij}$ written out. What looked like a quirk of the
architecture in that lesson (the gradient reaches this state along two routes) turns out to be a
consequence of having tied two roles together.

</Details>

## The whole grid with three matrices

Stack the three roles by rows, as the previous lesson stacked the states:
$\mathbf{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{T_y \times d_k}$ with step $i$'s query in row $i$, and
$\mathbf{K} \in \mathbb{R}^{T_x \times d_k}$ and $\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{T_x \times d_v}$ with
position $j$'s key and value in row $j$ of each (transposed, because in this course vectors are
columns). With the score in its cheapest form, the dot product, the three operations above fit in
one line:

$$
\text{Attention}\left(\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V}\right) = \text{softmax}\left(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}\right)\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{T_y \times d_v},
$$

with the softmax taken by rows, so that each row of $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}$ (one query against
the $T_x$ keys) comes out summing to $1$. The function's name is the one the paper that fixed this
form gave it, and it's how you'll find it written everywhere.

Now for the question that decides the next block: where has $\mathbf{W}_a$ gone? The previous
lesson's grid was $\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_a\bar{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}$, so group the first two factors and
compare with the line above. Out come $\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_a$ (that is,
$\mathbf{q}_i = \mathbf{W}_a^{\top}\mathbf{s}_{i-1}$) and $\mathbf{K} = \mathbf{V} = \bar{\mathbf{H}}$.
The matrix hasn't disappeared: it has stopped being part of the comparison and become part of what
is compared. And with it out of the way, the function $a$ is left as the bare dot product for good.
Everything a model wants to learn about how to compare has to be built into how $\mathbf{Q}$ and
$\mathbf{K}$ are made, a matter this line is silent about and the next block is almost entirely
about.

The additive score can't be moved like that, and the argument is the one from
<Leccion slug="bahdanau">the lesson on additive attention</Leccion>: between the two projections and
the read-out there's a $\tanh$, and a non-linear function of a sum doesn't split among its terms. So
this three-matrix form, the one the next block inherits, exists thanks to the choice the previous
lesson made, not in spite of it.

One debt is left, and it's worth stating out loud because you're going to see it written. The next
block's form carries one more factor:

$$
\text{Attention}\left(\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V}\right) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)\mathbf{V}.
$$

That divisor answers what the previous lesson measured without fixing: with freshly initialised
weights, the largest weight in one row reached $0.851$ against the $0.167$ of an even share, because
an unbounded dot product grows with the size of the vectors it multiplies. In this notation that size
has a name, $d_k$, which is why the fix fits in a single division. Where the root comes from (and why
$\sqrt{d_k}$ and not something else) is the subject of
<Leccion slug="scaled-dot-product">a lesson in the next block, on the *scaled dot-product*</Leccion>;
for now, all you need is to know that the factor is there and which problem it answers.

## The three arguments, in NumPy

The whole function is two lines, and what the cell checks is that nothing changed when the names did.
First it recovers the previous lesson's grid by handing out the roles (the query is the decoder state
rewritten by $\mathbf{W}_a$, and the key and the value are the same encoder state). Then it unties
them: one projection to compare with, $d_k = 3$ coordinates wide, and another to mix, $d_v = 7$ wide.
Pay particular attention to the last three lines.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

Replacing the values wholesale doesn't move a single weight of the map, while replacing the keys moves
all of it. That's the separation the last three lessons couldn't show, because there was one vector

With the names in place, what's missing looks different. $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ exist in full
as soon as the encoder finishes reading: they depend only on the source, and none of their rows waits
for any other. $\mathbf{Q}$ is different. Its rows are made one at a time, because each query is a
decoder state and each decoder state needs the previous step's context. Two of the three matrices are
complete in one go while the third still moves in single file, which is the previous lesson's
outstanding debt, told in this lesson's vocabulary.

And that's where the course turns. <Leccion slug="adios-recurrencia">The first lesson of the next
block, on removing the recurrence</Leccion>, takes the one remaining question seriously: if what
strangles the parallelism is the recurrence, and attention already knows how to read a whole sequence
without walking through it step by step, what's left once you take the recurrence out? The answer
fills the rest of the course, and you reach it with the three words and the three letters already in
place, which is the one thing this block had to accomplish.
