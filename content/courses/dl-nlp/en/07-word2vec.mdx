{/* COURSE-P5-01 — Block 1, Lesson 7. */}

The previous lesson, on dense representations, built the table and left it empty: the embedding
matrix $\mathbf{E}$ has a shape, a size and a geometry you can now read, and not one number in it.
The distributional hypothesis asserts where meaning lives and does not say how to compute it.
Word2Vec was the first to close that gap at real scale, in 2013, and it arrives from where you would
not expect: it computes no one's coordinates, it lets them fall out as the residue of another task.

The task is a guessing game. Take <W>the dog barks in the yard of the school</W>, cover everything
but <W>dog</W> and ask what was around it; a model that answers <W>barks</W> before <W>school</W> has
learned something, and to answer it needs numbers to score candidates with. Those numbers are in the
matrix $\mathbf{E}$, which enters a model with random values and gets corrected until it gets them
right — that is what **training** is — after which the model is thrown away and $\mathbf{E}$ is kept.
Word2Vec is that idea turned into a **technique**, and it is three pieces: a prediction task over
windows of text, two ways of solving it — *skip-gram* and CBOW (*continuous bag of words*) — and a
simplification that makes the computation viable: negative sampling. What is left to decide is which
parameters do the scoring, and what "getting it right" means once it is measured by a count that,
written out with care, comes out unaffordable.

Slide a window of $m$ tokens on each side along the corpus: the middle one is the **centre**, the
ones at the sides are its **context**, and one pair comes out for each neighbour. With $m = 2$, the
centre <W>yard</W> of the sentence above produces four: with <W>in</W>, <W>the</W>, <W>of</W> and
<W>the</W> again. The raw corpus already carries the pairs inside it: the distributional
hypothesis's promise, collected.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
Prediction is the pretext. What we are after are the parameters that make it possible, and they end
up resembling each other exactly when the entries keep the same company.
</Callout>

## Predicting the context: the skip-gram objective

Fix the window at $m$ and walk the corpus. The skip-gram takes each position $t$, with its central
token $w_t$, and asks to predict each of its neighbours separately. Getting it right is written as a
**loss**: a number, computed from the parameters and the whole corpus, that measures how badly the
model does; correcting them until it gets it right is making that number small. The skip-gram's is
the mean negative log-likelihood of the pairs:

$$
\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \; \sum_{\substack{-m \leq j \leq m \\ j \neq 0}} \log P(w_{t+j} \mid w_t),
$$

where $t$ runs over the $T$ positions of the corpus and the minus sign turns the criterion around:
the probability of the real pair has to go up, so $\mathcal{L}$ has to go down. The essential thing
is missing: what $P(c \mid w)$ is.

Predicting is choosing among candidates, and choosing requires scoring them. We need a number that
says how well the centre $w$ and a candidate context $c$ fit together. Word2Vec takes the simplest
one between two vectors, their dot product: it grows when they point in similar directions and when
they are long. We call it the pair's **affinity**.

For now we have only the table $\mathbf{E}$ with one vector per entry; the affinity of a pair comes
from reading two of its rows. That forces every entry to use the same vector as centre and as
candidate, and the flaw shows in the pair of an entry with itself:
$\mathbf{e}_w^{\top}\mathbf{e}_w = \lVert\mathbf{e}_w\rVert^2$, positive and larger the longer the
vector is. The cosine of the previous lesson cancelled it by dividing by the norms; in the affinity
there is no division. And the corpus asks for the opposite: <W>dog</W> almost never falls inside its
own window, and it has to be possible to score that pair low. But lowering $\lVert\mathbf{e}_w\rVert^2$
forces $\mathbf{e}_w$ to shrink, and that lowers at the same time all the affinities in which $w$ is
the centre.

Hence the design decision that defines Word2Vec: each entry receives *two* vectors of
$d_{\text{model}}$ coordinates, the $\mathbf{e}_w$ for when it acts as centre and a new one,
$\mathbf{u}_c$, for when it acts as context. They live in two tables of the same shape,
$\mathbf{E}, \mathbf{U} \in \mathbb{R}^{\lvert V \rvert \times d_{\text{model}}}$, with row $i$ of
$\mathbf{U}$ equal to $\mathbf{u}_{w_i}^{\top}$ exactly as that of $\mathbf{E}$ is $\mathbf{e}_{w_i}^{\top}$.
The affinity becomes $\mathbf{u}_c^{\top}\mathbf{e}_w$, and that of an entry with itself,
$\mathbf{u}_w^{\top}\mathbf{e}_w$, can go down without $\mathbf{e}_w$ shrinking.

Reading affinity as a probability asks for it to be made positive and for all of them to sum to 1;
the softmax does both:

$$
P(c \mid w) = \frac{\exp\!\left(\mathbf{u}_c^{\top}\mathbf{e}_w\right)}{\sum_{w' \in V} \exp\!\left(\mathbf{u}_{w'}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_w\right)}.
$$

The exponential function preserves order, so the candidate with the most affinity takes the highest
probability. And keep this about the denominator: it is there so that the $\lvert V \rvert$
affinities are a probability distribution over $V$, and for nothing else.

The parameters are $\theta = (\mathbf{E}, \mathbf{U})$, and $\mathcal{L}$ depends on nothing else.
To minimise it we use **gradient descent**, which we will see in detail in Block 2, on the
multilayer perceptron.

The gradient $\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}$ points in the direction of the loss's steepest increase.
So, at each step $t$, we update the parameters in the opposite direction,

$$
\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta\,\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta_t),
$$

where the subscript $t$ counts descent steps, not corpus positions, and $\eta$ is the **learning
rate**, the size of the step. Choosing it is that block's business: too large and the descent
diverges.

### CBOW: the same idea with the roles swapped

CBOW inverts the question: it guesses the centre from the context. The $2m$ neighbours enter
averaged into a single vector,

$$
\bar{\mathbf{e}}_t = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{\substack{-m \leq j \leq m \\ j \neq 0}} \mathbf{e}_{w_{t+j}},
$$

and the probability of the centre is the same softmax with $\bar{\mathbf{e}}_t$ in place of
$\mathbf{e}_w$. The average loses the order: the bag of words of
<Leccion slug="bolsa-de-palabras">the lesson on TF-IDF</Leccion> in miniature, and hence the name.

Both are used: CBOW is faster — one prediction per position instead of $2m$ — and skip-gram makes
more of the rare entries, which lose out when they are averaged in. What they share is what matters
now: a softmax over $V$.

### The sum that makes the objective unaffordable

Count the operations of a single probability. The numerator is a dot product, $d_{\text{model}}$
multiplications; the denominator, one per vocabulary entry:

$$
\lvert V \rvert \cdot d_{\text{model}} = 50\,000 \cdot 300 = 1.5 \times 10^{7} \quad \text{operations}.
$$

Fifteen million for a single pair. With $2m = 4$ pairs per token and a corpus of $T = 10^{10}$, that
gives $4 \times 10^{10}$ pairs, and a whole pass costs $6 \times 10^{17}$ operations, and training
does several. No machine was going to pay that for an embedding, not in 2013 and not now.

Look at where the cost is, because that is where the way out comes from. Making <W>barks</W> score
high next to <W>dog</W> costs 300 multiplications; the other 14,999,700 go on a sum whose only job is
to make the probabilities sum to one. You pay for a distribution over 50,000 entries you are not
going to use: when it ends you throw the model away and keep $\mathbf{E}$.

So the exact objective is abandoned, and it is worth saying so in full, because it is a move the
course will make again — in Block 5, with attention: when the right quantity is unaffordable, you
swap it for one you can afford with similar optima, and then check whether the swap broke anything.
What you do not do is pretend the second is the first.

## Negative sampling: from sharing probability out to telling pairs apart

The substitution changes the question. In place of — given <W>dog</W>, what probability does each of
the 50,000 entries have of going with it? — it asks a different one: is this pair, drawn at random,
from the corpus, or did I make it up?

False pairs are needed, and they are manufactured: to the real pair $(w, c)$ we add $n_{\text{neg}}$
pairs $(w, c_j)$ with the context drawn at random. The original paper fixed the number by trying:
from $5$ to $20$ with small corpora, from $2$ to $5$ with large ones. Take $n_{\text{neg}} = 5$. Each
pair's probability is computed by squashing its affinity into the interval $(0,1)$ with the logistic
function

$$
\sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}.
$$

The loss of a real pair and its negatives is

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{par}} = -\log \sigma\!\left(\mathbf{u}_c^{\top}\mathbf{e}_w\right) - \sum_{j=1}^{n_{\text{neg}}} \log \sigma\!\left(-\mathbf{u}_{c_j}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_w\right),
$$

and it reads left to right. The first term goes down when $\sigma(\mathbf{u}_c^{\top}\mathbf{e}_w)$
rises towards $1$: when the centre moves closer to the real context. In the second, $\sigma(-x)$ is
the probability of "no", and each summand goes down when the affinity with an invented context turns
negative. Move closer to one, away from the other five.

The negatives are not drawn uniformly at random, but from

$$
P_{\text{neg}}(w) = \frac{f_w^{3/4}}{\sum_{w' \in V} f_{w'}^{3/4}},
$$

with $f_w$ the frequency of the type $w$ in the corpus. The exponent $3/4$ compresses the
distribution without flattening it, between two bad extremes: with $1$ the negatives would be the
function words of <Leccion slug="bolsa-de-palabras">the lesson on the bag of words</Leccion>, with
$0$ the rarest entries, and neither costs anything to get right. There is no derivation behind it; it
is the value that worked.

Now the count, the reason for all of this. A step touches the centre, the real context and the
$n_{\text{neg}}$ negatives, one dot product per vector:

$$
(n_{\text{neg}} + 1) \cdot d_{\text{model}} = (5 + 1) \cdot 300 = 1\,800 \quad \text{operations},
$$

against the 15 million of before. The factor is $\lvert V \rvert / (n_{\text{neg}} + 1) \approx
8\,333$, and the whole pass drops from $6 \times 10^{17}$ to $7.2 \times 10^{13}$: from impossible to
an afternoon. Notice what has disappeared and what has not: the cost per pair stops depending on
$\lvert V \rvert$, and the $4 \times 10^{10}$ pairs are still there.

The concession, out loud: this is not the softmax computed faster, it is a different loss, and
raising $n_{\text{neg}}$ does not make it converge to $\mathcal{L}$. Whether its optima place the
vectors where they serve us is checked by looking at the neighbours that come out. The paper brings a
second shortcut with the same end, *hierarchical softmax*, which this lesson does not go through.

The step needs $\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{par}} / \partial \mathbf{e}_w$, and it comes from two
derivatives that say, in passing, where each term pushes.

<Details summary="Where the step comes from: the gradient of a pair's loss with respect to the centre vector">

The derivative of the logistic is $\sigma'(x) = \sigma(x)\left(1 - \sigma(x)\right)$, from which

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial x} \log \sigma(x) = \frac{\sigma(x)\left(1 - \sigma(x)\right)}{\sigma(x)} = 1 - \sigma(x).
$$

Write $s = \mathbf{u}_c^{\top}\mathbf{e}_w$ and $s_j = \mathbf{u}_{w_j}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_w$, with
$\partial s / \partial \mathbf{e}_w = \mathbf{u}_c$ and $\partial(-s_j) / \partial \mathbf{e}_w =
-\mathbf{u}_{w_j}$. By the chain rule, each term contributes

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf{e}_w}\left(-\log \sigma(s)\right) = -\left(1 - \sigma(s)\right)\mathbf{u}_c = \left(\sigma(s) - 1\right)\mathbf{u}_c,
$$

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf{e}_w}\left(-\log \sigma(-s_j)\right) = \left(1 - \sigma(-s_j)\right)\mathbf{u}_{w_j} = \sigma(s_j)\,\mathbf{u}_{w_j},
$$

using $1 - \sigma(-x) = \sigma(x)$ in the second. Summing:

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{par}}}{\partial \mathbf{e}_w} = \left(\sigma(s) - 1\right)\mathbf{u}_c + \sum_{j=1}^{n_{\text{neg}}} \sigma(s_j)\,\mathbf{u}_{w_j}.
$$

The coefficient of $\mathbf{u}_c$ is negative, so the step moves $\mathbf{e}_w$ towards the real
context; those of the negatives are positive and push against it. And they switch themselves off: if
the real pair already scores high, $\sigma(s) \approx 1$ and its coefficient goes to zero.

</Details>

## Training a tiny skip-gram in NumPy

The cell trains for real, in miniature: 800 tokens of a toy corpus from templates, 20 entries,
$d_{\text{model}} = 12$, $n_{\text{neg}} = 5$, four passes. The update is the gradient from the
derivation above: `g = s - labels`, where `s` is the output of the sigmoid applied to each pair's
affinity and `labels` is the vector with 1 for the real pair and 0 for the negatives. Subtracting it,
scaled, from each vector is gradient descent. The learning rate starts at $0.05$ and decays to
almost zero: the last steps refine instead of jumping. Run it and look at the neighbours of
<W>dog</W> before and after.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)

# --- A toy corpus, written by templates so there is something to learn.
animals = ["dog", "cat", "horse", "mouse"]
foods = ["meat", "fish", "grass", "cheese"]
places = ["yard", "kitchen", "stable", "barn"]
drinks = ["water", "milk"]

sentences = []
for a in animals:
    for l in places:
        for c in foods:
            sentences.append(f"the {a} eats {c} in the {l}")
        for b in drinks:
            sentences.append(f"the {a} drinks {b} in the {l}")
        sentences.append(f"the girl sees the {a} in the {l}")

corpus = " ".join(sentences).split()
V = sorted(set(corpus))
pos = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(V)}
ids = np.array([pos[w] for w in corpus])

d_model, m, n_neg, eta0, epochs = 12, 2, 5, 0.05, 4

# The (centre, context) pairs: the raw corpus, with no one annotating anything.
centres, contexts = [], []
for t, w in enumerate(ids):
    for j in range(max(0, t - m), min(len(ids), t + m + 1)):
        if j != t:
            centres.append(w)
            contexts.append(ids[j])
centres, contexts = np.array(centres), np.array(contexts)
print("T =", len(corpus), "tokens |", len(V), "entries |", len(centres), "pairs")

# P_neg(w) proportional to f_w^0.75, and all the negatives at once.
f = np.bincount(ids, minlength=len(V)).astype(float)
P_neg = f ** 0.75
P_neg = P_neg / P_neg.sum()
n_steps = epochs * len(centres)
negatives = rng.choice(len(V), size=(n_steps, n_neg), p=P_neg)

E = 0.1 * rng.standard_normal((len(V), d_model))
U = 0.1 * rng.standard_normal((len(V), d_model))

# A 1 for the real pair and a 0 for each negative: that is all the supervision.
labels = np.zeros(n_neg + 1)
labels[0] = 1.0


def neighbours(w, k=3):
    N = E / np.linalg.norm(E, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    s = N @ N[pos[w]]
    return [(V[i], round(float(s[i]), 2)) for i in np.argsort(-s) if i != pos[w]][:k]


print("before   dog  ->", neighbours("dog"))
print("         meat ->", neighbours("meat"))
print()

step = 0
for epoch in range(epochs):
    loss = 0.0
    for idx in rng.permutation(len(centres)):
        w = centres[idx]
        rows = np.concatenate(([contexts[idx]], negatives[step]))
        eta = eta0 * max(1e-4, 1.0 - step / n_steps)   # decays to almost zero
        step += 1

        v = E[w]
        # Affinity of the real pair and of the negatives, squashed by the logistic.
        s = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-np.clip(U[rows] @ v, -6.0, 6.0)))
        loss += -np.log(np.where(labels == 1.0, s, 1.0 - s) + 1e-10).sum()

        # The gradient derived above: (sigma - label) multiplies the other vector.
        g = s - labels
        E[w] = v - eta * (g @ U[rows])
        np.add.at(U, rows, -eta * g[:, None] * v)
    print("epoch", epoch + 1, " mean loss per pair", round(loss / len(centres), 3))

print()
print("after    dog  ->", neighbours("dog"))
print("         meat ->", neighbours("meat"))
print("         yard ->", neighbours("yard"))
print("         girl ->", neighbours("girl"))
print()

# What it cost, and what the exact objective would have cost.
print("dot products in", n_steps, "steps")
print("  with negatives ", n_steps * (n_neg + 1))
print("  exact softmax  ", n_steps * len(V), " (|V| =", len(V), ")")
print("  at |V| = 50,000 the ratio would be", 50000 // (n_neg + 1))
`} />

Before training, the nearest neighbour to <W>dog</W> is <W>kitchen</W> at $0.5$: pure noise. Four
passes later they are <W>cat</W>, <W>mouse</W> and <W>horse</W>, all at least $0.96$; <W>meat</W>'s are
the other three foods and <W>yard</W>'s the other three places. No one has said they resemble each
other: only window pairs have gone in, and a $1$ or a $0$ per pair.

Two things with no make-up on them. The corpus is artificial and the animals are interchangeable by
construction: hence cosines that graze $1$ and do not come out with real text. And <W>girl</W> lands
among the animals, next to <W>dog</W> and <W>cat</W>: it appears in a single mould — the subject of
<W>sees</W>, the same slot the animals fill — and it is the mould that got learned, not the word.

Set `n_neg = 0` and run it again. The loss collapses to $0.019$ — with no negatives, saying yes to
everything is perfect — and the neighbours turn to junk: cosines pinned near $0.99$ across the board,
<W>dog</W> now as close to <W>the</W> as to another animal. The negatives were not only the saving:
with nothing to push against, the loss goes down and the representation does not exist.

The matrix $\mathbf{E}$ is no longer empty; the debt the previous lesson ended on is paid. One
peculiarity remains. Word2Vec does not see the corpus, it sees one window at a time, and that
<W>dog</W> and <W>cat</W> resemble each other it concludes by stumbling into thousands of them. But
that is already told before any training: a single pass noting how many times each entry fell inside
another's window gives a $\lvert V \rvert \times \lvert V \rvert$ table of **co-occurrences** where
the row of <W>dog</W> and that of <W>cat</W> already resemble each other. Word2Vec never gets around
to writing it.

Making use of it is <Leccion slug="glove-y-limites">the lesson on GloVe and the limits of static
embeddings</Leccion>: GloVe (*global vectors*) builds that table and looks for the vectors that
explain it, another objective with results of the same family. And with the two techniques on the
table, that lesson closes the block by charging what the word "static" hides: each entry has one
vector, a single one, for ever.
