      def self_attention(X, Wq, Wk, Wv):
          Q, K, V = X @ Wq, X @ Wk, X @ Wv     # three readings of the same X
          E = Q @ K.T / np.sqrt(Wq.shape[1])   # (T, T): one score per pair

{/* COURSE-P5-05: Block 5, Lesson 2. COURSE-P11-08: English translation. */}

The previous lesson left the architecture without a single recurrent piece and with one operation
at its centre, attention, which has to be handed three things: queries, keys and values. Nothing is
left to make any of them, because both networks that did were recurrent. The only thing that still
has a row for every position is the sentence, already turned into vectors, so there is one candidate
for all three roles and no second one.

Using it as it is doesn't work, though, and the reason fits in a line: what a vector most resembles
is itself. Every position would give itself the highest weight, in every row at once, and the layer
would hand each one back what it already had, without a single parameter to do anything else with.
And it needs to do something else: in <W>the keys to this car are here</W>, getting <W>are</W>
right means having looked at <W>keys</W>, four positions back, and not at <W>car</W>, which sits
right next to it and is singular. This lesson builds the layer that can do that, and what it costs
is three learnt matrices, one per role. Multiplying a vector by one of them is *projecting* it: out
comes another vector, usually shorter, whose coordinates are combinations of the original's, and
the matrix decides which combinations. Each position is projected three times, and those give its
query, its key and its value.

The grid below is a sentence set against itself: the row is the one asking, the column is the one
answering, and each cell is how much of that column goes into what that row reads. Type any
sentence you like and, above all, switch the projections off and on. Off, the queries, the keys and
the values are the sentence's vectors as they are, and the highest cell in each row is the one that
position gives itself, in all seven rows at once. On, the row of <W>are</W> goes looking for
<W>keys</W> above everything else.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
Self-attention is the previous block's attention with the queries, the keys and the values taken
from the same sentence: each position asks every position of its own sentence, itself included, and
takes away a mixture of what they answer.
</Callout>

## Three readings of the same matrix

Stack the sentence by rows, the way the previous block stacked the encoder's states:

$$
\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d_{\text{model}}},
$$

where row $t$ is $\mathbf{x}_t^{\top}$, the vector of the token at position $t$ (transposed,
because in this course vectors are columns). That is everything that goes into the layer: one
matrix, and no second network.

The queries, the keys and the values come out of it, each through its own matrix:

$$
\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^Q, \qquad \mathbf{K} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^K, \qquad \mathbf{V} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^V,
$$

with $\mathbf{W}^Q, \mathbf{W}^K \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times d_k}$ and
$\mathbf{W}^V \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times d_v}$, so that
$\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d_k}$ and $\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d_v}$.
The superscripts on these three matrices are *labels for the role each one plays*, not layer
indices: it's the only place in the course where a superscript doesn't count layers, and it comes
from the paper's notation. There's a single set of these three projections here; a real layer
carries several in parallel, and that is <Leccion slug="multi-head">a lesson further on, about
multi-head attention</Leccion>.

That is where the <W>self</W> in the name comes from, and it's what separates this lesson from
<Leccion slug="atencion-como-consulta">the lesson on attention as query, key and value</Leccion>.
There the query was the state of one network and the keys and the values were the states of
another: two sources for three roles. Here all three come out of the same $\mathbf{X}$. The
operation that receives them is that lesson's with one addition, the divisor the paper puts in the
score:

$$
\text{Attention}\left(\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V}\right) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d_v},
$$

with the softmax taken by rows. That divisor, $\sqrt{d_k}$, is the only thing in the line nobody
has justified yet; it's noted, and I come back to it at the end.

That $\mathbf{Q}$ comes whole out of $\mathbf{X}$ doesn't contradict the previous lesson's caveat,
that when generating text the decoder still writes one token per step. Here $\mathbf{X}$ is a
sentence that is already complete (the source sentence, or the target one during training), and
then all the queries come out at once. When generating, the decoder's $\mathbf{X}$ is what it has
written so far and gains a row per step, and $\mathbf{Q}$ with it. But the new query is that row
multiplied by $\mathbf{W}^Q$, not the state of a network that needs the previous step's: the only
thing that forces it to go one token at a time is that the next token hasn't been written yet. How
that decoder is put together is <Leccion slug="encoder-decoder-masking">the lesson on masks</Leccion>.

Now look at the shapes, which is where you can tell that the input and the output are the same
sentence. $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}$ multiplies a $T \times d_k$ matrix by a $d_k \times T$ one,
so the product is *square*, $T \times T$: one row and one column per position. The softmax doesn't
change that shape, so the weight map $\mathbf{A}$ that comes out of it is $T \times T$ as well, with
the weight $\alpha_{ij}$ in cell $(i, j)$: how much of position $j$ goes into what position $i$
reads. It's the grid from the start of the lesson, and the literature calls it the *attention
weights* or the *attention map*. In the previous block it was $T_y \times T_x$ and its two axes were
two different sentences; here both axes are the same one, and that's why the cell $\alpha_{ii}$
exists and means something: how much of itself a position keeps. Each row still sums to $1$,
because the softmax still normalises one row at a time, and no position is left out of any row:
every position looks at every position.

And count the parameters, which is the other property of shape that decides the rest of the block.
The three matrices hold between them

$$
d_{\text{model}}\left(2d_k + d_v\right) \quad \text{parameters},
$$

and $T$ does not appear in that count. They multiply rows of $\mathbf{X}$, and it makes no
difference to them how many rows there are. Set that beside <Leccion slug="por-que-falla-el-mlp">the
lesson on why the MLP fails on sequences</Leccion>: there each position had its own block of columns
$\mathbf{W}^{(1)}_t$, so lengthening the input meant adding parameters, and each position learnt on
its own. These three matrices are the same for every position and for every length.

### Without projections, the layer copies itself

What's left to defend is what looks like a detour: if $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$
are readings of $\mathbf{X}$, why not use it as it is and save the three projections? Set
$\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{K} = \mathbf{V} = \mathbf{X}$ and work it through. The score of the pair
$(i,j)$ becomes the dot product of the two token vectors,

$$
e_{ij} = \frac{\mathbf{x}_i^{\top}\mathbf{x}_j}{\sqrt{d_{\text{model}}}},
$$

and two things follow from that, neither of them good. The first:
$\mathbf{x}_i^{\top}\mathbf{x}_j = \mathbf{x}_j^{\top}\mathbf{x}_i$, so the grid of scores is
*symmetric*. The weights aren't (each row is divided by its own sum, and those sums have no reason to
match), but the order within each row is fixed by a number that can't tell the one asking from the
one answering. A language can: <W>are</W> needs <W>keys</W> far more than <W>keys</W> needs
<W>are</W>.

The second is worse. Suppose all the token vectors have the same length,
$\lVert \mathbf{x}_t \rVert = r$. I'm assuming it here so that the calculation comes out exact,
and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality does the rest:

$$
\mathbf{x}_i^{\top}\mathbf{x}_j \le \lVert \mathbf{x}_i \rVert \lVert \mathbf{x}_j \rVert = r^{2} = \mathbf{x}_i^{\top}\mathbf{x}_i,
$$

with equality only when $\mathbf{x}_j$ points in the direction of $\mathbf{x}_i$. The diagonal cell
is the largest in its row, always, in every row at once, so the softmax gives the highest weight to
the position itself and the mixture leans towards $\mathbf{x}_i$: the layer hands back a faded
version of what came in. How far it leans depends on how long the vectors are: the larger $r$, the
more tightly the row closes on its diagonal. With lengths that differ from one another the
conclusion keeps its form: the row then goes to whichever position has the longest vector, which is
still a property of the vectors and not of what the sentence needs.

What settles the argument isn't either of the two, but what's missing from both. That layer *has
not a single parameter*. There's nothing to adjust, so there's no way for it to learn that a plural
verb has to go and find a plural noun. The three matrices are what separates the three roles
(comparing isn't being compared, and neither of them is contributing content) and, on top of that,
the only thing the layer can learn.

## The sequence in all three roles, in NumPy

The whole layer is five lines. Read them with the shapes in front of you, which is where you see
the sentence go in once and come out through three places.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

T, d_model, d_k, d_v = 6, 8, 4, 5
rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
X = rng.normal(size=(T, d_model))          # row t: the vector of the token at position t
Wq = rng.normal(size=(d_model, d_k)) * 0.6
Wk = rng.normal(size=(d_model, d_k)) * 0.6
Wv = rng.normal(size=(d_model, d_v)) * 0.6

Q, K, V = X @ Wq, X @ Wk, X @ Wv           # three readings of the SAME X

E = Q @ K.T / np.sqrt(d_k)                 # (T, T): one score per pair of positions
Z = np.exp(E - E.max(axis=1, keepdims=True))
A = Z / Z.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)       # softmax by rows
Y = A @ V

print("X", X.shape, " Q", Q.shape, " K", K.shape, " V", V.shape)
print("A", A.shape, " square:", A.shape[0] == A.shape[1])
print("every row sums to 1:", bool(np.allclose(A.sum(axis=1), 1.0)))
print("A symmetric:", bool(np.allclose(A, A.T)))
print("output", Y.shape, "->", d_v, "coordinates per position")
print("parameters:", d_model * (2 * d_k + d_v), " and T does not appear in the count")
print("row 0 of A:", np.round(A[0], 3))
`} />

Change `T` to $9$ and run it again: the map becomes $9 \times 9$, the output gains three rows and
the parameter line doesn't move. That's this layer's property of shape in one sentence.

Now the check that holds up the previous section. The cell below removes the projections, measures
what happens and puts them back.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)

def softmax_rows(E):

That leaves the divisor, and it can't be let slide any longer. The third part of the last cell
measures it without touching anything else: with queries and keys of $d_k = 8$ the largest weight
in a row stays at $0.558$, and with random vectors drawn the same way at $d_k = 512$ it climbs to
$0.983$. A row like that has stopped being a mixture: it keeps one position and throws away the
other five. That has had a name since <Leccion slug="funciones-activacion">the lesson on activation
functions</Leccion>: it's saturated. Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$, the maximum grows too, but slowly,
from $0.330$ to $0.486$, and the row goes on sharing its weight out.

That root isn't a constant someone kept trying until things came out right: it's calculated, and
what has to be calculated is how much a dot product grows as coordinates are added to it. The
calculation takes up the whole of <Leccion slug="scaled-dot-product">the next lesson, on the
*scaled dot-product*</Leccion>, and it deserves to be done slowly, because it's where two things
this course left in different blocks meet again: the saturation that switches off a share-out and
the gradient that vanishes on its way through one. Neither of them needed a sigmoid or a recurrence
to appear.
