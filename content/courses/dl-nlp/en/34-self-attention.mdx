{/* COURSE-P5-05: Block 5, Lesson 2. COURSE-P11-08: English translation. */}

The previous lesson left the architecture without a single recurrent piece and with one operation at
its centre, attention, which has to be handed three things: queries, keys and values. Nothing is
left to produce any of them, because both of the networks that did were recurrent. The only thing
that still has a row for every position is the sentence itself, already turned into vectors, so
there is one candidate for all three roles and no second one.

Using it as it is does not work, though, and the reason fits in a line: a vector resembles itself
more than anything else. Every position would give itself the highest weight, in every row at once,
and the layer would hand each one back what it already had, with no parameter to do anything else.
And it does need to do something else: in <W>the keys to this car are here</W>, getting <W>are</W>
right means having looked at <W>keys</W>, four positions back, and not at <W>car</W>, which sits
right next to it and is singular. This lesson builds the layer that can do that, and the price is
three learnt matrices, one per role. Multiplying a vector by one of them is *projecting* it: out
comes another vector, usually shorter, whose coordinates are combinations of the original's, and the
matrix decides which combinations. Each position is projected three times, giving its query, its key
and its value.

The grid below sets a sentence against itself: the row is the position asking, the column is the
position answering, and each cell shows how much of that column goes into what that row reads. Type
any sentence you like and, above all, switch the projections off and on. With them off, the queries,
keys and values are the sentence's vectors as they are, and the highest cell in each row is the one
that position gives itself, in all seven rows at once. With them on, the row for <W>are</W> goes
looking for <W>keys</W> above everything else.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
Self-attention is the previous block's attention with the queries, keys and values all taken from
the same sentence: each position asks every position in its own sentence, itself included, and takes
away a mixture of what they answer.
</Callout>

## Three readings of the same matrix

Stack the sentence by rows, the way the previous block stacked the encoder's states:

$$
\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d_{\text{model}}},
$$

where row $t$ is $\mathbf{x}_t^{\top}$, the vector of the token at position $t$ (transposed, because
in this course vectors are columns). That is everything that goes into the layer: one matrix, and no
second network.

The queries, keys and values all come out of it, each through its own matrix:

$$
\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^Q, \qquad \mathbf{K} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^K, \qquad \mathbf{V} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^V,
$$

with $\mathbf{W}^Q, \mathbf{W}^K \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times d_k}$ and
$\mathbf{W}^V \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times d_v}$, so that
$\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d_k}$ and $\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d_v}$.
The superscripts on these three matrices are *labels for the role each one plays*, not layer
indices. This is the only place in the course where a superscript does not count layers, and it
comes from the paper's notation. There is a single set of these three projections here; a real layer
carries several in parallel, which is the subject of <Leccion slug="multi-head">a later lesson on
multi-head attention</Leccion>.

That is where the <W>self</W> in the name comes from, and it is what separates this lesson from
<Leccion slug="atencion-como-consulta">the lesson on attention as query, key and value</Leccion>.
There, the query was the state of one network and the keys and values were the states of another:
two sources for three roles. Here all three come out of the same $\mathbf{X}$. The operation that
receives them is the same as in that lesson, with one addition: the divisor the paper puts in the
score.

$$
\text{Attention}\left(\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V}\right) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d_v},
$$

with the softmax taken by rows. That divisor, $\sqrt{d_k}$, is the only thing in the line nobody has
justified yet. For now, take note of it; I will come back to it at the end.

That $\mathbf{Q}$ comes whole out of $\mathbf{X}$ does not contradict the previous lesson's caveat
that, when generating text, the decoder still writes one token per step. Here $\mathbf{X}$ is a
sentence that is already complete (the source sentence, or the target one during training), so all
the queries come out at once. When generating, the decoder's $\mathbf{X}$ is what it has written so
far and gains a row at each step, and $\mathbf{Q}$ gains one with it. But the new query is that row
multiplied by $\mathbf{W}^Q$, not the state of a network that needs the previous step's: the only
thing forcing the decoder to go one token at a time is that the next token has not been written yet.
How that decoder is put together is the subject of <Leccion slug="encoder-decoder-masking">the lesson
on masks</Leccion>.

Now look at the shapes, which is where you can see that the input and the output are the same
sentence. $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}$ multiplies a $T \times d_k$ matrix by a $d_k \times T$ one,
so the product is *square*, $T \times T$: one row and one column per position. The softmax does not
change that shape, so the weight map $\mathbf{A}$ that comes out of it is $T \times T$ as well, with
the weight $\alpha_{ij}$ in cell $(i, j)$: how much of position $j$ goes into what position $i$
reads. This is the grid from the start of the lesson, and the literature calls it the *attention
weights* or the *attention map*. In the previous block it was $T_y \times T_x$ and its two axes were
two different sentences; here both axes are the same one, which is why the cell $\alpha_{ii}$ exists
and means something: how much of itself a position keeps. Each row still sums to $1$, because the
softmax still normalises one row at a time, and no position is left out of any row: every position
looks at every position.

And count the parameters, which is the other property of shape that decides what follows in the
block. The three matrices hold between them

$$
d_{\text{model}}\left(2d_k + d_v\right) \quad \text{parameters},
$$

and $T$ does not appear in that count. They multiply rows of $\mathbf{X}$, and it makes no
difference to them how many rows there are. Compare that with <Leccion slug="por-que-falla-el-mlp">the
lesson on why the MLP fails on sequences</Leccion>: there, each position had its own block of
columns $\mathbf{W}^{(1)}_t$, so lengthening the input meant adding parameters, and each position
learnt on its own. These three matrices are the same for every position and every length.

### Without projections, the layer copies itself

What remains to be defended is what looks like a detour: if $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$ and
$\mathbf{V}$ are readings of $\mathbf{X}$, why not use it as it is and save the three projections?
Set $\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{K} = \mathbf{V} = \mathbf{X}$ and work it through. The score of the pair
$(i,j)$ becomes the dot product of the two token vectors,

$$
e_{ij} = \frac{\mathbf{x}_i^{\top}\mathbf{x}_j}{\sqrt{d_{\text{model}}}},
$$

and two things follow, neither of them good. First, $\mathbf{x}_i^{\top}\mathbf{x}_j =
\mathbf{x}_j^{\top}\mathbf{x}_i$, so the grid of scores is *symmetric*. The weights are not (each row
is divided by its own sum, and those sums have no reason to match), but the ordering within each row
is fixed by a number that cannot tell the position asking from the position answering. A language
can: <W>are</W> needs <W>keys</W> far more than <W>keys</W> needs <W>are</W>.

The second is worse. Suppose all the token vectors have the same length,
$\lVert \mathbf{x}_t \rVert = r$. I am assuming this so that the calculation comes out exact, and
the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality does the rest:

$$
\mathbf{x}_i^{\top}\mathbf{x}_j \le \lVert \mathbf{x}_i \rVert \lVert \mathbf{x}_j \rVert = r^{2} = \mathbf{x}_i^{\top}\mathbf{x}_i,
$$

with equality only when $\mathbf{x}_j$ points in the direction of $\mathbf{x}_i$. The diagonal cell is
the largest in its row, always, in every row at once, so the softmax gives the highest weight to the
position itself and the mixture leans towards $\mathbf{x}_i$: the layer hands back a faded version of
what came in. How far it leans depends on how long the vectors are: the larger $r$ is, the more
tightly the row closes on its diagonal. If the vectors have different lengths, the conclusion keeps
its shape: the row then goes to whichever position has the longest vector, which is still a property
of the vectors and not of what the sentence needs.

What settles the argument is neither of these, but what is missing from both. That layer *has not a
single parameter*. There is nothing to adjust, so there is no way for it to learn that a plural verb
has to go and find a plural noun. The three matrices are what separate the three roles (asking is
not being asked, and neither is contributing content) and, on top of that, they are the only thing
the layer can learn.

## The sequence in all three roles, in NumPy

The whole layer is five lines. Read them with the shapes in front of you, which is where you see the
sentence go in once and come out as three different readings.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

T, d_model, d_k, d_v = 6, 8, 4, 5
rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
X = rng.normal(size=(T, d_model))          # row t: the vector of the token at position t
Wq = rng.normal(size=(d_model, d_k)) * 0.6
Wk = rng.normal(size=(d_model, d_k)) * 0.6
Wv = rng.normal(size=(d_model, d_v)) * 0.6

Q, K, V = X @ Wq, X @ Wk, X @ Wv           # three readings of the SAME X

E = Q @ K.T / np.sqrt(d_k)                 # (T, T): one score per pair of positions
Z = np.exp(E - E.max(axis=1, keepdims=True))
A = Z / Z.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)       # softmax by rows
Y = A @ V

print("X", X.shape, " Q", Q.shape, " K", K.shape, " V", V.shape)
print("A", A.shape, " square:", A.shape[0] == A.shape[1])
print("every row sums to 1:", bool(np.allclose(A.sum(axis=1), 1.0)))
print("A symmetric:", bool(np.allclose(A, A.T)))
print("output", Y.shape, "->", d_v, "coordinates per position")
print("parameters:", d_model * (2 * d_k + d_v), " and T does not appear in the count")
print("row 0 of A:", np.round(A[0], 3))
`} />

Change `T` to $9$ and run it again: the map becomes $9 \times 9$, the output gains three rows and the
parameter line does not move. That is the layer's shape property in a nutshell.

what happens, and then puts them back.

def softmax_rows(E):
    Z = np.exp(E - E.max(axis=1, keepdims=True))

That leaves the divisor, and it cannot be put off any longer. The third part of the last cell
measures it without touching anything else: with queries and keys of $d_k = 8$, the largest weight
in a row averages $0.558$, and with random vectors drawn the same way at $d_k = 512$ it climbs to
$0.983$. A row like that has stopped being a mixture: it keeps one position and throws away the
other five. This has had a name since <Leccion slug="funciones-activacion">the lesson on activation
functions</Leccion>: the row is saturated. Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$, the maximum grows too, but
slowly, from $0.330$ to $0.486$, and the row goes on sharing its weight out.

That root is not a constant someone kept tweaking until things came out right: it is calculated, and
what has to be calculated is how much a dot product grows as coordinates are added to it. That
calculation takes up the whole of <Leccion slug="scaled-dot-product">the next lesson, on the *scaled
dot-product*</Leccion>, and it deserves to be done slowly, because it is where two things this
course left in different blocks meet again: the saturation that switches off a share-out and the
gradient that vanishes on its way through one. Neither of them needed a sigmoid or a recurrence to
appear.
