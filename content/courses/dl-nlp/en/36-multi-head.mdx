      import numpy as np


      def multi_head(X, Wq, Wk, Wv, Wo, h):
          T, d_model = X.shape
          d_k = d_model // h
          # (T, d_model) -> (h, T, d_k): block of columns i becomes head i.
          Q = (X @ Wq).reshape(T, h, d_k).transpose(1, 0, 2)
          K = (X @ Wk).reshape(T, h, d_k).transpose(1, 0, 2)
          V = (X @ Wv).reshape(T, h, d_k).transpose(1, 0, 2)
          E = Q @ K.transpose(0, 2, 1) / np.sqrt(d_k)      # (h, T, T)
          Z = np.exp(E - E.max(axis=2, keepdims=True))
          A = Z / Z.sum(axis=2, keepdims=True)
          concat = (A @ V).transpose(1, 0, 2).reshape(T, d_model)
          return concat @ Wo, A

The lesson on the square root in the scores left two things unsettled. A self-attention layer gives
each position one row of $\mathbf{A}$, produced by a single pair of matrices, $\mathbf{W}^Q$ and
$\mathbf{W}^K$; and $d_k$ sits in the formula with nothing to say how large it ought to be.

Take the sentence <W>this small mouse which the cats chase sleeps</W>. <W>sleeps</W> has to find
its subject, <W>mouse</W>, and one criterion isn't enough for that: asking whether a word is a noun
doesn't tell <W>mouse</W> from <W>cats</W>, and asking whether it agrees in number doesn't tell a
subject from a determiner. I'll call each criterion of that kind a rule. The row of <W>sleeps</W>
needs both at once, and it combines them by adding: each rule gives each word a score, the dot
product adds them up and the softmax shares out the row according to the total. The word that meets
both rules wins.

Adding works when what you're after is one word that meets every rule, but a position may need two
different words. <W>chase</W> needs whoever does the chasing, <W>cats</W>, and whoever is chased,
<W>mouse</W>, and its row is a single share-out that sums to $1$: either it keeps one and loses the
other, or it mixes the two. What's more, once the rules have been added they can't be pulled apart
again: the row doesn't say how much each one put in.

The alternative is not to add before sharing out: split the rules into groups, each with its own
attention and its own softmax, and join what each one reads afterwards. Each of those attentions is
a head, and the layer that runs several of them is multi-head attention, the subject of this lesson.

The explorable below puts one rule in each head: head 1 is the noun rule, head 2 is agreement, and
heads 3 and 4 relate nouns to their determiners and modifiers. The fifth button doesn't join the
heads: it is the layer from <Leccion slug="self-attention">the lesson on self-attention</Leccion>,
with the four rules added before a single softmax. The row is the position asking and the column the
position answering, and the panel underneath sums up, for the row you pick, which word each map
pulls towards.

In the row of <W>sleeps</W>, head 1 ties <W>mouse</W> with <W>cats</W>, at $0.30$ each; head 2

In the row of <W>chase</W>, which needed both <W>cats</W> and <W>mouse</W>, the sum keeps
<W>cats</W>, with $0.40$, and leaves <W>mouse</W> $0.07$. Head 1, by contrast, still gives it

doesn't know <W>mouse</W> is the one being chased, because none of these rules looks for objects
and it reads both nouns alike; what the separation prevents is the sum erasing it. That is why a real
head carries several rules: the ones that have to hold at the same time are added inside it, and the
ones that look for different things can end up in different heads.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
Multi-head doesn't change the scores: it changes the moment the softmax comes in. A single head adds
up every rule and applies one softmax to the total; several heads apply a softmax to each group of
rules separately and combine what each one has read afterwards.

## The same matrix read by several attentions at once

Each head is the layer from <Leccion slug="self-attention">the lesson on self-attention</Leccion>,
whole: its three projections over the same matrix and its own map. All that has to be added is the
subscript there was no need to write there, because there was only one.

$$
\text{head}_i = \text{Attention}\left(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^Q_i,\; \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^K_i,\; \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}^V_i\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d_v},
$$

with $i = 1, \dots, h$, and with $\mathbf{W}^Q_i, \mathbf{W}^K_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times
d_k}$ and $\mathbf{W}^V_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times d_v}$. Notice that the $h$ heads
read *the same* $\mathbf{X}$: they don't divide the sentence among themselves, and they don't divide
the input coordinates either. Each one draws its own queries, keys and values from it, and with them
its own map $\mathbf{A}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times T}$, square and with rows summing to $1$ like
the one in <Leccion slug="self-attention">the lesson on self-attention</Leccion>.

That leaves $h$ outputs of $T \times d_v$, and the layer has to return one. Lay them side by side and
project the result:

$$
\text{MultiHead}(\mathbf{X}) = \text{Concat}\left(\text{head}_1, \dots, \text{head}_h\right)\mathbf{W}^O \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d_{\text{model}}},
$$

where $\text{Concat}$ joins the $h$ matrices *by columns* (one row per position, with the $h$ pieces
one after another inside it, so the result is $T \times \left(h \cdot d_v\right)$), and
$\mathbf{W}^O \in \mathbb{R}^{\left(h \cdot d_v\right) \times d_{\text{model}}}$ is the only matrix
in the layer that belongs to no head. Its superscript is a label for its role, like those of the
other three.

### Adding before or after the softmax

A head's score was already a sum, and the previous lesson wrote it that way precisely so that it
could count it:

$$
e_{ij} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d_k}}\sum_{m=1}^{d_k} q_{im}k_{jm},
$$

where $q_{im}$ is coordinate $m$ of the query of position $i$. Each coordinate of the comparison
contributes its term, and the softmax doesn't see the terms: it sees the total.

The rules from the start of the lesson are those terms. The explorable above is built that way on
purpose: each rule is one column of $\mathbf{W}^Q$ and one of $\mathbf{W}^K$, so coordinate $m$ of
the query times coordinate $m$ of the key is the score of rule $m$. The single-head layer carries all
four columns, so it compares in $d_k = 4$ coordinates (and divides their sum by $\sqrt{4} = 2$); each
of the four heads keeps a single column, $d_k = 1$, and its score is one rule and nothing more. In a
trained layer nobody writes the rules, and a coordinate need not mean anything on its own, but the
count is the same: terms that reach the softmax already added up.

Adding inside the softmax has a consequence that fits in one line. If $a_j$ and $b_j$ are what two
rules give word $j$, the exponential turns their sum into a product:

$$
\text{softmax}_j\left(a_j + b_j\right) = \frac{e^{a_j}\,e^{b_j}}{\sum_{l} e^{a_l}\,e^{b_l}}.
$$

Sharing out the total means multiplying, word by word, what each rule would give on its own, and
rescaling so that the row sums to $1$ again. A product behaves like a logical AND: if one rule scores
a word low, that word is left with no weight, whatever the other rule says. That is what happens to
<W>mouse</W> in the row of <W>chase</W>: agreement sinks it, because the number doesn't match, and
the noun rule, which scored it the same as <W>cats</W>, can't pull it back up. And since what comes
out is a single share-out, there's no longer any telling how much each rule put in.

That is the whole operation. Splitting the coordinates into $h$ groups and giving each group its own
softmax is *sharing out before combining*, instead of combining before sharing out. Out come $h$
share-outs instead of one, each with its own denominator, and they reach the output without touching
each other; what combines them is $\mathbf{W}^O$, a matrix that is learnt and that receives them
already shared out. The sum the single-head layer did inside the softmax could not be undone; done
outside, how much each part weighs can still be decided.

### Splitting the width among the heads

That leaves choosing $d_k$, the other open question, and it is chosen by counting. Each head costs
what the whole layer from <Leccion slug="self-attention">the lesson on self-attention</Leccion>
cost:

$$
d_{\text{model}}\left(2d_k + d_v\right) \quad \text{parameters},
$$

there are $h$ of them, and on top of them sits $\mathbf{W}^O$ with $h \cdot d_v \cdot d_{\text{model}}$
more, so the complete layer asks for

$$
h \cdot d_{\text{model}}\left(2d_k + d_v\right) + h \cdot d_v \cdot d_{\text{model}}.
$$

Now assign $d_k$ and $d_v$ the value $d_{\text{model}}/h$ (splitting the model's width among the
heads, instead of giving each one the whole width) and watch what happens:

$$
h \cdot d_{\text{model}} \cdot \frac{3d_{\text{model}}}{h} + h \cdot \frac{d_{\text{model}}}{h} \cdot d_{\text{model}} = 3d_{\text{model}}^{2} + d_{\text{model}}^{2} = 4d_{\text{model}}^{2}.
$$

The $h$ has gone. Eight heads cost the same as one, and sixteen the same as eight. The
multiplications behave the same way: <Leccion slug="adios-recurrencia">the lesson that said goodbye to recurrence</Leccion>
measured one attention at $T^{2}\left(d_k + d_v\right)$, and $h$ of them give
$h \cdot T^{2}\left(d_k + d_v\right) = 2T^{2}d_{\text{model}}$, which is exactly what a single one of
width $d_{\text{model}}$ cost.

So $h$ is chosen for what it does and not for what it costs, and $d_k$ stops being a loose number:
it is what is left of $d_{\text{model}}$ once it is split. With the paper's figures,
$d_{\text{model}} = 512$ and $h = 8$, that gives $d_k = 64$ (the number the previous lesson did its
sums with), and the whole layer weighs $4 \cdot 512^{2} = 1\,048\,576$ parameters.

What it costs is worth saying, because it isn't nothing. Each head compares in $64$ coordinates
where a single one would have compared in $512$: these are not eight comparisons as fine as the one
there was, they are eight comparisons eight times narrower. That is the deal, and it is a bet about
language (that several coarse relations are worth more than one very fine one), not a theorem.

### Concatenate and project, don't average

The obvious alternative to concatenating is averaging: give each head the whole width,
$d_v = d_{\text{model}}$, and return $\frac{1}{h}\sum_i \text{head}_i$, which already has the right
shape without any further matrix. It is worth seeing it for what it is, a special case of what the
layer already does. Take the $\mathbf{W}^O$ that stacks $h$ copies of $\frac{1}{h}\mathbf{I}$:

$$
\mathbf{W}^O = \frac{1}{h}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I} \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{I}\end{bmatrix}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{Concat}\left(\text{head}_1, \dots, \text{head}_h\right)\mathbf{W}^O = \frac{1}{h}\sum_{i=1}^{h}\text{head}_i.
$$

Concatenating with a $\mathbf{W}^O$ that is learnt can therefore give the average and anything else;
averaging is fixing one point of all that in advance. And fixing it has a very specific cost: it
forces the $h$ heads to write into the same $d_{\text{model}}$ coordinates, so two heads that have
found two different things can cancel each other out before anything reads them. Concatenated, each
has its $d_v$ coordinates to itself, and $\mathbf{W}^O$ decides afterwards, one output coordinate at
a time, which of them to listen to.

That matrix does a second thing as well, which is only pointed out here: it brings the output back to
$d_{\text{model}}$, the width it came in with. That is what lets these layers be stacked one on top
of another, and what really needs it is <Leccion slug="bloque-transformer">a lesson further on, on
the full block with residual connections and layer norm</Leccion>.

It remains to say what this construction does *not* do, because the drawings in papers invite you to
believe the opposite. Nothing in the loss assigns a role to a head: there is no term saying that head
2 takes care of agreement. Heads do different things because they start out different, and for no
other reason. The names in the explorable above are mine, from writing the four rules by hand; in a
trained layer nobody names them, and you have to go looking for them afterwards, when they are there
at all. Not even the order means anything: swap two heads together with their two blocks of rows of
$\mathbf{W}^O$ and not one number of the output changes, so head 3 does not name the same thing in two
trainings of the same model.

## The heads in NumPy

The first cell builds the layer with the projections whole and one block of columns per head, which
is how it is really implemented, and then runs the parameter count through five values of $h$.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

T, d_model, h = 6, 8, 4
d_k = d_v = d_model // h
rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
X = rng.normal(size=(T, d_model))
Wq = rng.normal(size=(d_model, d_model)) * 0.6   # the h heads, side by side
Wk = rng.normal(size=(d_model, d_model)) * 0.6
Wv = rng.normal(size=(d_model, d_model)) * 0.6
Wo = rng.normal(size=(h * d_v, d_model)) * 0.6


def softmax_rows(E):
    Z = np.exp(E - E.max(axis=1, keepdims=True))
    return Z / Z.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)

    A = softmax_rows(Q @ K.T / np.sqrt(d_k))

    maps.append(A)

print("row 0 of head 2:", np.round(maps[1][0], 3))

The second cell measures the concession. If the $h$ heads start out with the same parameters, there
are not $h$ heads.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

T, d_model, h = 6, 8, 4
d_k = d_model // h
rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
X = rng.normal(size=(T, d_model))


def softmax_rows(E):
    Z = np.exp(E - E.max(axis=1, keepdims=True))
    return Z / Z.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)


def head_maps(Wq, Wk):
    out = []
    for i in range(h):
        cols = slice(i * d_k, (i + 1) * d_k)
        Q, K = X @ Wq[:, cols], X @ Wk[:, cols]
        out.append(softmax_rows(Q @ K.T / np.sqrt(d_k)))
    return np.stack(out)                          # (h, T, T)


block = rng.normal(size=(d_model, d_k)) * 0.6
equal = np.tile(block, (1, h))                    # the h heads, with the same matrices
A = head_maps(equal, equal)
print("h identical maps:", bool(np.allclose(A, A[0])))
print("largest difference from head 1:", np.abs(A - A[0]).max(axis=(1, 2)))

different = equal.copy()
different[:, :d_k] += 0.4 * rng.normal(size=(d_model, d_k))   # head 1 only
B = head_maps(different, different)
print("after moving head 1 only:")
print("  how far each one is from head 1:", np.round(np.abs(B - B[0]).max(axis=(1, 2)), 3))
print("  and how far each one is from head 2:", np.round(np.abs(B - B[1]).max(axis=(1, 2)), 3))
print("row 0, head 1:", np.round(B[0][0], 3))
print("row 0, head 2:", np.round(B[1][0], 3))
`} />

With the four blocks of columns equal, the four distances come out exactly $0$: the four maps are
the same, and $\mathbf{W}^O$ receives four copies of one vector per position. Moving a single block
separates that head from the other three ($0.458$ in the last three cells of the first line) and
leaves those three identical to one another, which is what the three zeros of the second line say.
The asymmetry doesn't appear by itself: it comes in through the initialisation, and in training it
lasts because the heads and the blocks of rows of $\mathbf{W}^O$ all start out different. With
everything symmetric, the gradient would have no way of breaking it.

With $h$ heads, a position has $h$ answers where it had one, and $\mathbf{W}^O$ decides what to do
with them. But look at where the $h$ come from: the same $\mathbf{X}$, the same formula, and
neither $i$ nor $j$ appears anywhere in $e_{ij}$. Multiplying the heads multiplies *what* is
compared, and adds not a single coordinate of *where*. None of the $h$ can learn to look for the
noun on its left, however many of them you put in, because in what the layer reads there is no left.

The missing piece isn't in the layer: it is in the input, and it amounts to two different positions
reaching $\mathbf{X}$ as two different rows even when the token is the same. That is
<Leccion slug="codificacion-posicional">the next lesson, on positional encoding</Leccion>, and what
makes it interesting is not that it is needed (that has been known since the first lesson of this
block) but the particular form the paper chooses: sines and cosines with which the distance between
two positions can be written as a linear operation on what has been added. That property is shown,
not asserted, and it takes up the whole lesson.
