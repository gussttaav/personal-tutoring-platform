The previous lesson, on Word2Vec, ended by pointing at a table it never wrote down, and said the
corpus carries it for free. That table is where GloVe (*global vectors*), published in 2014, begins —
and it does not arrive with the one thing that matters decided: what to ask of it. A raw count on its
own says little. <W>bank</W> sits beside <W>the</W> a great many times, and nothing follows from that.

What tells one entry from another is not who it appears with, but in what proportion. That proportion
sets the whole shape of the objective, and it settles, in passing, something the block has been
showing for two lessons without charging for it: what happens when you press the button on the map
from <Leccion slug="embeddings-densos">the lesson on dense representations</Leccion>.

Subtract <W>man</W> from <W>king</W> and add the result to <W>woman</W>, and you land on <W>queen</W>.
What the picture claims is not that <W>king</W> and <W>queen</W> are close — that neighbourhood was
already there in that lesson — but something stronger: that the *difference* between two vectors means
something in itself and reappears intact in another pair. The warning is the same as then: I placed
the coordinates by hand, so the map shows the shape of the claim, not its proof.

That the differences mean something cannot be an accident: if subtracting vectors gives something
sensible, the objective must have asked them to reproduce something that already works by subtraction.
Subtraction cancels what is shared and leaves what separates; in the counts, the operation that does
that is division. Take <W>ice</W> and <W>steam</W> and, instead of looking at what each appears with,
divide one frequency by the other, entry by entry. These are the figures the authors of GloVe
published over a corpus of 6 billion tokens:

| $k$ | $P(k \mid \textit{ice})$ | $P(k \mid \textit{steam})$ | ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| <W>solid</W> | $1.9 \times 10^{-4}$ | $2.2 \times 10^{-5}$ | $8.9$ |
| <W>gas</W> | $6.6 \times 10^{-5}$ | $7.8 \times 10^{-4}$ | $0.085$ |
| <W>water</W> | $3.0 \times 10^{-3}$ | $2.2 \times 10^{-3}$ | $1.36$ |
| <W>fashion</W> | $1.7 \times 10^{-5}$ | $1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ | $0.96$ |

The bare probabilities confuse frequent with characteristic: <W>water</W> appears with <W>ice</W> more
than <W>solid</W> does. The ratio does not: it measures the contrast with <W>steam</W>, not the
frequency. It is far more than $1$ when $k$ belongs to the world of the first, far less when it belongs
to the second's, and it sticks to $1$ when it does not tell them apart: <W>fashion</W>, which appears
with neither, and <W>water</W>, which appears with both alike. The base frequency cancels in the
division.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
The counts say who each entry appears with; their ratios say what separates it from the rest, and that
is where the information is. GloVe comes from demanding that vector differences reproduce those ratios.
</Callout>

## What the co-occurrence ratios ask of the vectors

Write $X_{ik}$ for the cell of the co-occurrence table: the count of entry $k$ inside the window of
entry $i$, with the same $m$ as the previous lesson. It is symmetric and has $\lvert V \rvert^{2}$
cells. Write too $\mathbf{e}_i$ and $\mathbf{u}_k$ for $\mathbf{e}_{w_i}$ and $\mathbf{u}_{w_k}$, the
centre and context vectors of that lesson.

Summing a whole row gives the total number of $i$'s neighbours, and with it the probability that,
looking at a window centred on $i$, $k$ appears:

$$
X_i = \sum_{k} X_{ik}, \qquad P(k \mid i) = \frac{X_{ik}}{X_i}.
$$

The table of <W>ice</W> and <W>steam</W> pointed at the ratio between two of these probabilities.
Asking the vectors for it is written with an $F$ still to be decided:

$$
F\!\left(\mathbf{e}_i,\, \mathbf{e}_j,\, \mathbf{u}_k\right) = \frac{P(k \mid i)}{P(k \mid j)}.
$$

Two decisions leave it almost fixed. What separates $i$ from $j$ on the right is a quotient, and on
the left what the function receives of the two is their difference $\mathbf{e}_i - \mathbf{e}_j$, the
one from the map above. And to turn that, together with $\mathbf{u}_k$, into a single number there is
the dot product from <Leccion slug="bolsa-de-palabras">the lesson on the bag of words</Leccion>. With
the two, $F$ is a function of one real variable:

$$
F\!\left(\mathbf{u}_k^{\top}\left(\mathbf{e}_i - \mathbf{e}_j\right)\right) = \frac{P(k \mid i)}{P(k \mid j)}.
$$

What remains is an $F$ that turns a subtraction in its argument into a division in its value, and that
property determines it: the exponential.

<Details summary="Why the exponential is the only way out">

We want a continuous function $F : \mathbb{R} \to (0, \infty)$ with $F(a - b) = F(a) / F(b)$ for any
$a$ and $b$. With $a = b$ this gives $F(0) = 1$; with $a = x + y$ and $b = y$, the condition
rearranges into

$$
F(x + y) = F(x)\,F(y),
$$

Cauchy's functional equation in its multiplicative form, whose only continuous, not identically zero
solutions are $F(x) = e^{cx}$. The constant is absorbed into the scale of the vectors — if $e^{cx}$
serves for some $\mathbf{e}_i$, then $e^{x}$ serves for $c\,\mathbf{e}_i$ — so taking $c = 1$ loses
nothing.

</Details>

With $F = \exp$ the equality holds as soon as each probability is matched with its exponential,

$$
\exp\!\left(\mathbf{u}_k^{\top}\mathbf{e}_i\right) = P(k \mid i),
$$

because the quotient on the right becomes the quotient of two exponentials. Taking logarithms,
$\mathbf{u}_k^{\top}\mathbf{e}_i = \log P(k \mid i)$, and subtracting that equality for $i$ and for $j$
brings out what the intuition promised:

$$
\mathbf{u}_k^{\top}\left(\mathbf{e}_i - \mathbf{e}_j\right) = \log \frac{P(k \mid i)}{P(k \mid j)}.
$$

The difference of two vectors, projected onto a third, is the logarithm of the table's ratio. That is
where directions start to mean something: the analogy on the map is this line read backwards.

It remains to put it in a form that can be fitted. Substituting $P(k \mid i) = X_{ik} / X_i$,

$$
\mathbf{u}_k^{\top}\mathbf{e}_i = \log X_{ik} - \log X_i,
$$

and that $\log X_i$ has a cheap fix: it does not depend on $k$, so it is a constant for each entry
$i$. Give it a name, $b_i$, and move it to the other side. With that the equality stops being
symmetric — $X_{ik} = X_{ki}$, but the left side no longer is — so a second scalar $\tilde{b}_k$ for
the entry playing the context role restores it:

$$
\mathbf{u}_k^{\top}\mathbf{e}_i + b_i + \tilde{b}_k = \log X_{ik}.
$$

That is two more real numbers per entry, one in each role, and what is gained is not cosmetic: the
starting condition spoke of triples $(i, j, k)$ and this one, of a pair. One equation per cell of the
table.

### The weighting that stops the most frequent pairs deciding for everyone

That system has no exact solution: it is $\lvert V \rvert^{2}$ equations — 2.5 billion with the
block's vocabulary of 50,000 entries — against some 30 million unknowns. So we ask for what is
possible, in the least-squares sense, and first two things have to be fixed.

The first is that most of the table is zeros — any two given entries never once share a window — and
$\log 0$ does not exist. The empty cells are left out, which is also what makes the fit cheap: you pay
for the pairs seen and not for $\lvert V \rvert^{2}$.

The second is that the cells are not worth the same. One with $X_{ik} = 1$ is almost noise, and one
with $X_{ik} = 10^{6}$ — two function words from <Leccion slug="bolsa-de-palabras">the lesson on the
bag of words</Leccion> — should not decide on its own where the vectors go. The fix is a weighting
that depends on the count itself:

$$
g(x) = \begin{cases}
  \left(x / x_{\max}\right)^{\alpha} & \text{if } x < x_{\max} \\
  1 & \text{if } x \geq x_{\max}
\end{cases}.
$$

It grows while the count is small, so a pair seen once weighs little, and it flattens from $x_{\max}$
on, so the most frequent ones do not drag the fit. The paper takes $x_{\max} = 100$ and $\alpha = 3/4$,
the same exponent as the previous lesson's negative sampling and found the same way: by trying.
GloVe's loss is then

$$
\mathcal{L} = \sum_{i,\, k \;:\; X_{ik} > 0} g(X_{ik}) \left(\mathbf{u}_k^{\top}\mathbf{e}_i + b_i + \tilde{b}_k - \log X_{ik}\right)^{2},
$$

with parameters $\theta = (\mathbf{E}, \mathbf{U}, b, \tilde{b})$ and nothing else. Minimising it is
gradient descent, and each step needs the gradient of $\mathcal{L}$.

<Details summary="The gradient of the loss used at each step">

The weighting $g(X_{ik})$ and the target $\log X_{ik}$ come from the co-occurrence table: in the
derivative they are constants. Writing $r_{ik}$ for the cell's error,

$$
\ell_{ik} = g(X_{ik})\,r_{ik}^{2}, \qquad r_{ik} = \mathbf{u}_k^{\top}\mathbf{e}_i + b_i + \tilde{b}_k - \log X_{ik},
$$

the chain rule on the square leaves $2r_{ik}$ multiplying four immediate derivatives:

$$
\frac{\partial \ell_{ik}}{\partial \mathbf{e}_i} = 2\,g(X_{ik})\,r_{ik}\,\mathbf{u}_k, \qquad
\frac{\partial \ell_{ik}}{\partial \mathbf{u}_k} = 2\,g(X_{ik})\,r_{ik}\,\mathbf{e}_i, \qquad
\frac{\partial \ell_{ik}}{\partial b_i} = \frac{\partial \ell_{ik}}{\partial \tilde{b}_k} = 2\,g(X_{ik})\,r_{ik}.
$$

The four share a coefficient, and each vector receives the other. A cell already explained, with
$r_{ik} \approx 0$, moves nothing.

</Details>

The difference from the previous lesson is not where it seems: both fit a function of
$\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{e}$ to a co-occurrence statistic, and what changes is when the counting
happens. Word2Vec counts while it learns; GloVe counts first and then fits without opening the corpus
again.

## One vector, a single one, for ever

The block now has two ways of filling $\mathbf{E}$, and they share a trait neither of them mentions.
Look at the subject of that sum: $i$ runs over the vocabulary entries. There is one row of
$\mathbf{E}$ per entry, and only one.

<W>bank</W> is a vocabulary entry. In an English corpus it appears in <W>I drew the money from the
bank</W> and in <W>I sat on the river bank</W>, and the co-occurrence table does not separate the two:
it piles the neighbours of one and of the other into the same row. Nothing in $X_{ik}$ remembers which
occurrence each count came from, because the counts are made over the type, and the type is the same
string. The fit is left with a compromise, and the loss says which: the row that minimises it explains
the *sum* of the two profiles, which resembles neither.

This is not a defect of GloVe. Word2Vec has it for the same reason, and the one-hot of
<Leccion slug="one-hot">the lesson on the curse of dimensionality</Leccion> and the TF-IDF
(*term frequency–inverse document frequency*) of <Leccion slug="bolsa-de-palabras">the lesson on the
bag of words</Leccion> had it before. And it does not come alone:
<Leccion slug="embeddings-densos">the lesson on dense representations</Leccion> noted another limit of
the same family — that <W>cold</W> and <W>hot</W> appear in almost identical contexts, so the
distributional hypothesis places them close and the representation has nothing to tell them apart with.
The third is the usual one: an entry that was not in the corpus has no row, and it has to be handed
<W>\<UNK></W>'s, as in <Leccion slug="vocabulario-oov">the lesson on out-of-vocabulary (OOV)
words</Leccion>.

The three are the same fact written three ways, and it is there at the start of the block:
<Leccion slug="texto-como-numeros">the lesson on the problem of representing language</Leccion> asked
for a function

$$
r : V \to \mathbb{R}^{d},
$$

and discussed slowly what it had to satisfy. What binds here is none of those conditions: it is the
domain. If the argument of $r$ is a vocabulary entry, its value cannot depend on the sentence it
appears in, because the sentence does not enter the function. That is what **static** means.

## Counting the pairs and fitting GloVe in NumPy

The cell counts and fits. The corpus is 680 tokens of templates, with <W>bank</W> planted in two
worlds; I have left $d_{\text{model}} = 3$ deliberately: with more coordinates the table fits almost
exactly and the loss is not seen to work. The learning rate decays to almost zero, as in the previous
lesson. Run it and look at three things: the column of ratios, the neighbours from the fit, and the
three rows at the end.

import numpy as np

# --- A toy corpus where <bank> appears in both of its senses.
finance = ["money", "mortgage", "payroll", "savings", "loan"]
river = ["reed", "willow", "heron", "boat", "pebble"]


def write(bank_f, bank_r):
    sentences = []
    for f in finance:
        for v in ["deposited", "withdrew", "checked"]:
            sentences.append(f"the client {v} her {f} at the {bank_f}")
        for f2 in finance:
            if f2 != f:
                sentences.append(f"the {bank_f} recorded her {f} and her {f2}")
        sentences.append(f"the girl deposited her {f} at the {bank_f}")
    for p in river:
        for v in ["rested", "waited", "read"]:
            sentences.append(f"the girl {v} beside the {p} at the {bank_r}")
        for p2 in river:
            if p2 != p:
                sentences.append(f"the {bank_r} lay between the {p} and the {p2}")
        sentences.append(f"the client rested beside the {p} at the {bank_r}")
    return [s.split() for s in sentences]


# X_ik: how many times k falls in a window of m tokens around i. ONE pass.
def cooccurrences(docs, m=4):
    V = sorted({w for d in docs for w in d})
    pos = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(V)}
    X = np.zeros((len(V), len(V)))
    for d in docs:
        ids = [pos[w] for w in d]
        for t, i in enumerate(ids):
            for j in range(max(0, t - m), min(len(ids), t + m + 1)):
                if j != t:
                    X[i, ids[j]] += 1.0
    return V, pos, X


docs = write("bank", "bank")
V, pos, X = cooccurrences(docs)
T = sum(len(d) for d in docs)
print("T =", T, "tokens |", len(V), "entries |", int((X > 0).sum()), "nonzero cells of", X.size)
print()

# The ratio from the derivation, measured here: P(k|i) = X_ik / X_i.
X_i = X.sum(axis=1)
print("k          P(k|mortgage)  P(k|willow)     ratio")
for k in ["client", "girl", "bank", "at"]:
    a = X[pos["mortgage"], pos[k]] / X_i[pos["mortgage"]]
    b = X[pos["willow"], pos[k]] / X_i[pos["willow"]]
    print("  ", k.ljust(9), ("%.4f" % a).rjust(9), ("%.4f" % b).rjust(14), ("%.2f" % (a / b)).rjust(9))
print()

d_model, x_max, alpha, eta0, epochs = 3, 10.0, 0.75, 0.1, 30


def fit(X, seed=3):
    """Minimise sum g(X_ik) (u_k . e_i + b_i + b_k - log X_ik)^2 over the NONZERO cells."""
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n = len(X)
    row, col = np.nonzero(X)
    g = np.minimum((X[row, col] / x_max) ** alpha, 1.0)
    target = np.log(X[row, col])

    E = 0.1 * r.standard_normal((n, d_model))
    U = 0.1 * r.standard_normal((n, d_model))
    bE, bU = np.zeros(n), np.zeros(n)

    n_steps = epochs * len(row)
    step = 0
    for epoch in range(1, epochs + 1):
        loss = 0.0
        for idx in r.permutation(len(row)):
            i, k = row[idx], col[idx]
            eta = eta0 * max(1e-4, 1.0 - step / n_steps)   # decays to almost zero
            step += 1

            e, u = E[i].copy(), U[k]
            error = e @ u + bE[i] + bU[k] - target[idx]
            loss += g[idx] * error ** 2

            # The gradient derived above: 2 g(X_ik) times the error, each vector receives the other.
            coef = 2.0 * g[idx] * error
            E[i] -= eta * coef * u
            U[k] -= eta * coef * e
            bE[i] -= eta * coef
            bU[k] -= eta * coef
        if epoch in (1, 10, 20, epochs):
            print("   epoch", str(epoch).rjust(2), " mean loss per cell",
                  round(float(loss / len(row)), 4))
    return E + U        # the implementations keep the sum of the two tables


W = fit(X)
N = W / np.linalg.norm(W, axis=1, keepdims=True)
S = N @ N.T

# Neighbours among content words: in 680 tokens the function words fall in every window.
content = finance + river + ["client", "girl", "bank"]


def neighbours(w, k=3):
    i = pos[w]
    order = sorted((pos[c] for c in content if c != w), key=lambda j: -S[i, j])
    return [(V[j], round(float(S[i, j]), 2)) for j in order[:k]]
