A decoder free to read the whole input sounds like a fix, and it isn't an architecture yet. The
previous lesson, on the context-vector bottleneck, measured what summarising costs and left the
unused material within reach: the $T_x$ states the encoder computes and nothing touches again.
Everything else is still missing. What exactly does the decoder receive at step $i$, who builds it,
and out of what?

Before the first question there's an obstacle of shape, and it's the one that decides the answer.
There are as many encoder states as the sentence has tokens: six for one, thirty for another. The
decoder, on the other hand, has a size decided before it sees any sentence (its weight matrices have
a fixed number of columns), so laying the states out in a row and multiplying them by a matrix runs
into the same wall as <Leccion slug="por-que-falla-el-mlp">the lesson on why the multilayer
perceptron fails on sequences</Leccion>: an input whose length changes doesn't fit in a matrix whose
shape doesn't. Reading them all will have to mean something else. It will have to mean combining
them into a single vector of the size the decoder already expected, and then the only thing left to
decide is how much of each.

Take the translation of <W>ayer leí un libro muy bueno</W> as <W>yesterday i read a very good
book</W>, whose words line up: <W>ayer</W> is <W>yesterday</W>, <W>leí</W> is <W>i read</W>, <W>un
libro</W> is <W>a book</W> and <W>muy bueno</W> is <W>very good</W>. What each output step decides is
how much it takes from each encoder state, not which one it keeps. That difference is what makes the
idea work: if step $i$ had to point at *one* state, choosing would be a discrete decision, and
further down that turns out to carry a price nobody can pay. Sharing out, on the other hand, is done
with numbers. The step that writes <W>book</W> concentrates nearly all its weight on <W>libro</W>
and shares the little left over among the other tokens; the one that writes <W>very</W>
concentrates on <W>muy</W>. Each step gets its own mixture, and that sharing out (one amount per
input position, different at every output step) is what we call **attention**.

The explorable brings that pair from <Leccion slug="encoder-decoder">the lesson on the
encoder-decoder architecture</Leccion>, with one row per output step. Go through the rows and look at
two places. <W>i</W> and <W>read</W> pull from the same token, <W>leí</W>, because Spanish carries
the pronoun inside the verb's ending and English has to write it separately. And <W>book</W>, at
step 7, goes back to <W>libro</W>, which is at position 4, after steps 5 and 6 have sat on <W>muy</W>
and <W>bueno</W>: the lines cross, which is the reordering the fixed summary had to hold in memory.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
Each output step builds its own context vector as a weighted sum of all the encoder states, and the
only thing that distinguishes one step from another is the weights of that sum.
</Callout>

## A different context at every step

Fix what step $i$ receives. It's $T_x$ numbers, one per input position, and we call them
$\alpha_{i1}, \dots, \alpha_{iT_x}$. With them, the context of that step is

$$
\mathbf{c}_i = \sum_{j=1}^{T_x} \alpha_{ij}\,\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h},
\qquad
\alpha_{ij} \geq 0,
\qquad
\sum_{j=1}^{T_x} \alpha_{ij} = 1.
$$

Look at the two shapes, because the whole point is that they don't match. The list of weights
measures $T_x$: it grows with the input, one more weight for every new token, and that's where the
information that didn't fit before comes in. The result measures $d_h$ whatever happens, which is
exactly what lets it take the place of the usual context vector without touching the rest of the
decoder. The input grew; the slot didn't.

The two conditions on the weights turn that sum into a **mixture**, and it's worth seeing what they
rule out. Since no weight is negative and they all sum to one, each coordinate of $\mathbf{c}_i$
falls between the smallest and the largest value of that same coordinate across the
$\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j$: the mixture shares out what's there, it doesn't invent new values or amplify
any. The two extremes of what it can do write themselves. If one weight is $1$ and the rest are $0$,
then $\mathbf{c}_i = \bar{\mathbf{h}}_{j}$ and the mixture has become a hard choice of one state. If
they're all $1/T_x$, then $\mathbf{c}_i$ is the mean of the whole sentence, which looks at everything
and tells nothing apart. The mechanism lives between those two cases.

### The fixed summary was a frozen alignment

There's a third set of weights you already know, though it was never written as one. Take
$\alpha_{ij} = 1$ when $j = T_x$ and $\alpha_{ij} = 0$ everywhere else, for every step alike. In the
explorable's map, that would mean switching it all off except the column of <W>bueno</W>, lit in all
eight rows:

$$
\mathbf{c}_i = \sum_{j=1}^{T_x} \alpha_{ij}\,\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j = \bar{\mathbf{h}}_{T_x} = \mathbf{c}.
$$

That's the encoder-decoder of the previous two lessons, whole, with nothing taken away and nothing
added. And it's a perfectly legal alignment: the weights aren't negative and they sum to one. So
attention doesn't replace that architecture or compete with it: it contains it. What it does is
unfreeze some weights that had been set by hand, on the last token, and that didn't depend on $i$.
The bottleneck didn't come from $d_h$ being small. It came from that row being unable to move.

### The context enters the decoder as one more input

The figure puts the two halves together at an arbitrary step, step $i$. At the top is the encoder,
with its $T_x$ states. From each one a thread runs to $\mathbf{c}_i$, and each thread's thickness is
its weight $\alpha_{ij}$: here the thickest is the one from $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_2$, so $\mathbf{c}_i$
mostly resembles that state and the other two contribute less. On the right is the decoder cell,
which receives three things: from above, its previous state, $\mathbf{s}_{i-1}$; from below, the
token written at the previous step, $y_{i-1}$; and from the left, $\mathbf{c}_i$, which is the only
new thing.

The context reaches the decoder's recurrence as one more term, with its own weight matrix
$\mathbf{W}_{ch} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h \times d_h}$:

$$
\mathbf{s}_i = \tanh\left(\mathbf{W}^{\text{dec}}_{xh}\mathbf{o}_{y_{i-1}} + \mathbf{W}^{\text{dec}}_{hh}\mathbf{s}_{i-1} + \mathbf{W}_{ch}\mathbf{c}_i + \mathbf{b}^{\text{dec}}_h\right),
$$

where $\mathbf{o}_{y_{i-1}}$ is the one-hot vector of $y_{i-1}$, which in training is the correct
token (*teacher forcing*) and when generating is the one the model itself has just written. What
comes out on the right of the figure is the same as in <Leccion slug="encoder-decoder">the
encoder-decoder architecture</Leccion>:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{y}}_i = \text{softmax}\left(\mathbf{W}_{hy}\mathbf{s}_i + \mathbf{b}_y\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{\lvert V_y \rvert},
$$

one probability for each entry of the output vocabulary. When generating, the step writes the most
probable entry, and that token is the $y_i$ that comes in from below at the next step. The context
doesn't reach $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_i$ directly: it goes through $\mathbf{s}_i$ first.

The starting state, on the other hand, does change. In <Leccion slug="encoder-decoder">the
encoder-decoder lesson</Leccion> the decoder started at $\mathbf{s}_0 = \mathbf{c}$, because there
was a $\mathbf{c}$ and only one; now there isn't, so we take $\mathbf{s}_0 = \mathbf{0}$, like the
recurrence of the previous block. It's a choice, not a necessity (the usual thing is to start from a
learned projection of $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{T_x}$), and it changes nothing that follows.

With that, we can rewrite the uncomfortable line from <Leccion slug="encoder-decoder">the lesson on
the encoder-decoder architecture</Leccion>:

$$
P\left(y_i \mid y_{<i},\, \mathbf{c}\right)
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
P\left(y_i \mid y_{<i},\, \mathbf{c}_i\right).
$$

The change looks like a subscript, and it's a change in what each step conditions on. That equality
claimed that everything the input had to say about *any* position of the output fitted in $d_h$
numbers fixed once and for all; this one claims something much weaker, that for *each* position
there's a mixture of the states that will do. And since $\mathbf{c}_i$ is built from the $T_x$
states, step $i$ is conditioning on the complete source again. The assumption hasn't gone away. It's
become cheap.

## Where the weights come from

That leaves the question everything rests on: who decides the $\alpha_{ij}$. They can't be fixed in
advance (that's exactly what has just been unfrozen), nor can they depend only on $j$, the input
position and, with it, the encoder state $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j$ being weighted, because then every step
would look at the same place. They have to depend on the pair: on the step $i$ the decoder is at and
on what's at position $j$. So at step $i$ the mechanism scores $T_x$ pairs, the decoder's state
against each of the encoder's states:

$$
e_{ij} = a\left(\mathbf{s}_{i-1},\, \bar{\mathbf{h}}_j\right), \qquad j = 1, \dots, T_x,
$$

where $a$ is a function that returns a number (how compatible that decoder state and that encoder
state are), and $\mathbf{s}_{i-1}$ is the previous state for a reason of order, not taste: computing
$\mathbf{s}_i$ requires already having $\mathbf{c}_i$, which requires the weights, which require the
scores. The last thing that exists when it's time to score is $\mathbf{s}_{i-1}$. That's why the
scores come out row by row: those of step $i$ can't be computed until step $i-1$ has finished.

The scores are loose numbers and can be negative, so they have to be turned into a mixture. A
softmax does that, and here it's worth looking at what it runs over:

$$
\alpha_{ij} = \frac{\exp(e_{ij})}{\sum_{k=1}^{T_x} \exp(e_{ik})}.
$$

It comes out positive because of the exponential and sums to one because of the denominator, which
are the mixture's two conditions, and as a bonus it shares out smoothly: a slightly larger score
takes a bit more of the weight, not all of it.

<Callout type="warning" title="This softmax doesn't share out over the vocabulary">
Every softmax in the course until now shared probability out over the entries of a vocabulary. This
one shares out over the *input positions*: the index in the denominator runs over $k = 1, \dots,
T_x$, with step $i$ held still. The two live in the same decoder step and over different axes
($\hat{\mathbf{y}}_i$ decides what to write, $\alpha_{i\cdot}$ decides what to look at), and mixing
them up is the fastest way to misread any implementation of this.
</Callout>

Two consequences, and the first is a bill. There's one score for each pair of step and position, so
translating a sentence costs $T_x \cdot T_y$ evaluations of $a$: the price grows with the *product*
of the two lengths. The fixed summary paid none, and that's precisely what was choking it; it's worth
knowing from the start that looking back doesn't come free.

The second explains the softmax instead of something more direct. The natural thing would be to keep
the best-scored state, $\arg\max_j e_{ij}$, and save the whole sum. But that returns a position, and a
position is a whole number: move the parameters of $a$ by a millionth and the index doesn't budge,
until one day it jumps. The derivative of $\mathbf{c}_i$ with respect to those parameters is zero
wherever it exists, so the loss would have no way of teaching $a$ to score better. With the softmax,
$\mathbf{c}_i$ depends on the scores continuously, and <Leccion slug="regla-de-la-cadena">the chain
rule</Leccion> passes through the weights the same way it passes through anything else. Choosing
smoothly is what makes choosing learnable.

The function $a$ is still missing, and it isn't defined here. All this lesson needs from it is that
it returns a number and is differentiable; what form it takes, how many parameters it carries and
how they're trained is the next lesson's business.

## The per-step context, in NumPy

The cell takes the explorable's alignment (my numbers, again) and one state per input token, and
does three things. It checks the one thing you can't see by looking at the map, that every row sums
to one. It builds the eight contexts in one go, because mixing every row at once is a matrix product.
And it repeats the calculation with the frozen alignment, so you can compare the two.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

# The explorable's alignment: row = output step, column = input position.
SOURCE = ["ayer", "leí", "un", "libro", "muy", "bueno"]
TARGET = ["yesterday", "i", "read", "a", "very", "good", "book", "<EOS>"]
alpha = np.array([[0.86, 0.06, 0.03, 0.02, 0.02, 0.01],    # yesterday
                  [0.09, 0.74, 0.05, 0.07, 0.03, 0.02],    # i      <- from "leí"
                  [0.04, 0.81, 0.04, 0.06, 0.03, 0.02],    # read   <- from "leí" again
                  [0.02, 0.05, 0.72, 0.15, 0.03, 0.03],    # a
                  [0.02, 0.03, 0.04, 0.06, 0.79, 0.06],    # very
                  [0.02, 0.02, 0.03, 0.05, 0.10, 0.78],    # good
                  [0.02, 0.03, 0.06, 0.76, 0.05, 0.08],    # book   <- back to "libro"
                  [0.02, 0.03, 0.03, 0.12, 0.07, 0.73]])   # <EOS>
# One state per input token: d_h = 5 coordinates in (-1, 1), like a tanh.
H = np.array([[ 0.82, -0.31,  0.10,  0.45, -0.62], [-0.55,  0.71,  0.28, -0.14,  0.36],
              [ 0.12,  0.44, -0.77,  0.21,  0.08], [ 0.39, -0.68,  0.52,  0.77, -0.11],
              [-0.24,  0.15,  0.63, -0.58,  0.49], [ 0.66,  0.29, -0.35,  0.18,  0.74]])
T_y, T_x = alpha.shape

# What the map doesn't let you see, and without which this wouldn't be a mixture:
assert np.allclose(alpha.sum(axis=1), 1.0), "some row doesn't sum to 1"

C = alpha @ H                                     # c_i = sum_j alpha_ij * h_j, all T_y at once
fixed = np.zeros_like(alpha); fixed[:, -1] = 1.0  # the fixed summary, written as an alignment
C_fixed = fixed @ H

print("step  writes       pulls mostly from  weight  distance to the fixed summary")
for i in range(T_y):
    j = int(alpha[i].argmax())
    print("  %d   %-11s  %-18s  %.2f   %.2f"
          % (i + 1, TARGET[i], SOURCE[j], alpha[i, j], np.linalg.norm(C[i] - C_fixed[i])))

print()
print("distinct contexts:  with attention %d of %d,  with the fixed summary %d of %d"
      % (len(np.unique(C.round(9), axis=0)), T_y,
         len(np.unique(C_fixed.round(9), axis=0)), T_y))
print("and that one is the state of '%s':" % SOURCE[-1], np.allclose(C_fixed[0], H[-1]))
`} />

The middle column is the map read out loud, and the last two lines are the finding: with the
alignment above, the eight steps receive eight different vectors, and with the frozen one they
receive the same vector eight times, the state of the input's last token. The last column says how
much each step was missing, and there's something there to look at slowly, because not every step
misses the same amount. The two small values, $0.25$ and $0.32$, belong to <W>good</W> and to
<W>\<EOS></W>, the end-of-sequence token, which are the only two steps that pull from <W>bueno</W>,
the very token the fixed summary carried inside it. Those of <W>yesterday</W> and <W>book</W> are
above $1.35$. The fixed summary wasn't equally bad everywhere: it got things right where the
sentence ends and wrong everywhere else, which is where it broke down in the previous lesson. Change
one weight in the `alpha` matrix and run the cell again: the `assert` complains before you can look
at anything, which is exactly its job.

The mechanism is complete except for one piece, and it's the piece that decides everything else.
Nobody computed this lesson's weights: I wrote them into a matrix, choosing them so that the map
would show a reordering and a shared token. A real model has nobody to write them for it. It has to
score each pair $(\mathbf{s}_{i-1}, \bar{\mathbf{h}}_j)$ on its own, with a function that starts out
knowing as little as the rest of the network and learns at the same time as it does.

That function is $a$, and <Leccion slug="bahdanau">the next lesson, on Bahdanau's attention</Leccion>,
is the one that writes it: it gives it its own parameters (a small layer, with its matrix and its
vector), puts it into the same gradient descent that already trains the encoder and the decoder, and
derives how the gradient reaches it, which turns out to be along two routes at once. That there's
more than one reasonable way to score a pair, and that the choice between them ends up deciding what
Block 5 looks like, takes up the rest of this block.
