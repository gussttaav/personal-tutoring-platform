Letting the decoder read the whole input sounds like a fix, but it isn't an architecture yet. The
previous lesson, on the context-vector bottleneck, measured what summarising costs and left the
unused material within reach: the $T_x$ states the encoder computes and nothing touches again.
Everything else is still missing. What exactly does the decoder receive at step $i$, who builds it,
and out of what?

Before any of that, there's an obstacle of shape, and it decides the answer. There are as many encoder
states as the sentence has tokens: six for one sentence, thirty for another. The decoder, on the other
hand, has a size fixed before it sees any sentence (its weight matrices have a set number of
columns), so laying the states out in a row and multiplying them by a matrix runs into the same wall
as <Leccion slug="por-que-falla-el-mlp">the lesson on why the multilayer perceptron fails on
sequences</Leccion>: an input whose length changes doesn't fit a matrix whose shape doesn't. Reading
all the states will have to mean something else: combining them into a single vector of the size the
decoder already expects. Then the only thing left to decide is how much of each to take.

Take the translation of <W>ayer leí un libro muy bueno</W> as <W>yesterday i read a very good
book</W>, whose words line up: <W>ayer</W> is <W>yesterday</W>, <W>leí</W> is <W>i read</W>, <W>un
libro</W> is <W>a book</W> and <W>muy bueno</W> is <W>very good</W>. What each output step decides is
how much to take from each encoder state, not which single one to keep. That difference is what makes
the idea work. If step $i$ had to point at *one* state, choosing would be a discrete decision, and
further down that turns out to carry a price nobody can pay. Sharing out, on the other hand, is done
with numbers. The step that writes <W>book</W> puts nearly all its weight on <W>libro</W> and divides
the little that's left among the other tokens; the step that writes <W>very</W> concentrates on
<W>muy</W>. Each step gets its own mixture, and that sharing out (one amount per input position,
different at every output step) is what we call **attention**.

The explorable brings back that pair from <Leccion slug="encoder-decoder">the lesson on the
encoder-decoder architecture</Leccion>, with one row per output step. Go through the rows and look at
two places. <W>i</W> and <W>read</W> both pull from the same token, <W>leí</W>, because Spanish
carries the pronoun in the verb's ending while English has to write it separately. And <W>book</W>,
at step 7, goes back to <W>libro</W> at position 4, after steps 5 and 6 have sat on <W>muy</W> and
<W>bueno</W>: the lines cross, which is the reordering the fixed summary had to hold in memory.

Each output step builds its own context vector as a weighted sum of all the encoder states, and the
only thing that sets one step apart from another is the weights of that sum.
</Callout>

## A different context at every step

Start with what step $i$ receives: $T_x$ numbers, one per input position, which we call
$\alpha_{i1}, \dots, \alpha_{iT_x}$. With them, the context for that step is

$$
\mathbf{c}_i = \sum_{j=1}^{T_x} \alpha_{ij}\,\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h},
\qquad
\alpha_{ij} \geq 0,
\qquad
\sum_{j=1}^{T_x} \alpha_{ij} = 1.
$$

Look at the two shapes, because the whole point is that they don't match. The list of weights has
length $T_x$: it grows with the input, one more weight for every new token, and that's where the
information that didn't fit before gets in. The result always has size $d_h$, which is exactly what
lets it take the place of the usual context vector without touching the rest of the decoder. The
input grew; the slot didn't.

The two conditions on the weights turn that sum into a **mixture**, and it's worth seeing what they
rule out. Since no weight is negative and they all sum to one, each coordinate of $\mathbf{c}_i$ falls
between the smallest and largest value of that same coordinate across the $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j$: the
mixture blends what's already there, and doesn't invent new values or amplify any. The two extremes
are easy to write down. If one weight is $1$ and the rest are $0$, then $\mathbf{c}_i = \bar{\mathbf{h}}_{j}$
and the mixture has become a hard choice of one state. If they're all $1/T_x$, then $\mathbf{c}_i$ is
the mean of the whole sentence, which looks at everything and tells nothing apart. The mechanism lives
between those two cases.

### The fixed summary was a frozen alignment

There's a third set of weights you already know, though it was never written as one. Take
$\alpha_{ij} = 1$ when $j = T_x$ and $\alpha_{ij} = 0$ everywhere else, for every step alike. In the
explorable's map, that would switch everything off except the column for <W>bueno</W>, lit in all
eight rows:

$$
\mathbf{c}_i = \sum_{j=1}^{T_x} \alpha_{ij}\,\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j = \bar{\mathbf{h}}_{T_x} = \mathbf{c}.
$$

That's the encoder-decoder of the previous two lessons, whole, with nothing taken away and nothing
added. And it's a perfectly legal alignment: the weights are non-negative and they sum to one. So
attention doesn't replace that architecture or compete with it: it contains it. What attention does is
unfreeze weights that had been fixed by hand on the last token, the same for every $i$. The
bottleneck didn't come from $d_h$ being small. It came from that row being unable to move.

### The context enters the decoder as one more input

The figure puts the two halves together at an arbitrary step, step $i$. At the top is the encoder,
with its $T_x$ states. A thread runs from each one to $\mathbf{c}_i$, and each thread's thickness is
its weight $\alpha_{ij}$: here the thickest is the one from $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_2$, so $\mathbf{c}_i$
mostly resembles that state, with smaller contributions from the other two. On the right is the
decoder cell, which receives three things: its previous state, $\mathbf{s}_{i-1}$, from above; the
token written at the previous step, $y_{i-1}$, from below; and $\mathbf{c}_i$ from the left, which is
the only new one.

The context enters the decoder's recurrence as one more term, with its own weight matrix
$\mathbf{W}_{ch} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h \times d_h}$:

$$
\mathbf{s}_i = \tanh\left(\mathbf{W}^{\text{dec}}_{xh}\mathbf{o}_{y_{i-1}} + \mathbf{W}^{\text{dec}}_{hh}\mathbf{s}_{i-1} + \mathbf{W}_{ch}\mathbf{c}_i + \mathbf{b}^{\text{dec}}_h\right),
$$

where $\mathbf{o}_{y_{i-1}}$ is the one-hot vector of $y_{i-1}$, which in training is the correct
token (*teacher forcing*) and when generating is the one the model itself wrote at the previous step. What
comes out on the right of the figure is the same as in
<Leccion slug="encoder-decoder">the encoder-decoder architecture</Leccion>:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{y}}_i = \text{softmax}\left(\mathbf{W}_{hy}\mathbf{s}_i + \mathbf{b}_y\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{\lvert V_y \rvert},
$$

one probability for each entry of the output vocabulary. When generating, the step writes the most
probable entry, and that token becomes the $y_i$ that enters from below at the next step. The context
doesn't reach $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_i$ directly: it goes through $\mathbf{s}_i$ first.

The starting state, on the other hand, does change. In
<Leccion slug="encoder-decoder">the encoder-decoder lesson</Leccion> the decoder started at
$\mathbf{s}_0 = \mathbf{c}$, because there was one $\mathbf{c}$ and only one. Now there isn't, so we
take $\mathbf{s}_0 = \mathbf{0}$, like the recurrence of the previous block. That's a choice, not a
necessity (the usual alternative is to start from a learned projection of $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{T_x}$),
and nothing that follows depends on it.

With that, we can rewrite the uncomfortable line from
<Leccion slug="encoder-decoder">the lesson on the encoder-decoder architecture</Leccion>:

$$
P\left(y_i \mid y_{<i},\, \mathbf{c}\right)
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
P\left(y_i \mid y_{<i},\, \mathbf{c}_i\right).
$$

The change looks like a subscript, but it changes what each step conditions on. The old equality
claimed that everything the input had to say about *any* position of the output fitted in $d_h$
numbers fixed once and for all; this one claims something much weaker: that for *each* position,
some mixture of the states will do. And since $\mathbf{c}_i$ is built from all $T_x$ states, step $i$
is conditioning on the complete source again. The assumption hasn't gone away. It has become cheap.

## Where the weights come from

That leaves the question everything rests on: who decides the $\alpha_{ij}$? They can't be fixed in
advance (that's exactly what was unfrozen above). Nor can they depend only on $j$, the input position
(and with it the encoder state $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j$ being weighted), because then every step would
look at the same place. They have to depend on the pair: on the step $i$ the decoder has reached and
on what sits at position $j$. So at step $i$ the mechanism scores $T_x$ pairs, the decoder's state
against each of the encoder's states:

$$
e_{ij} = a\left(\mathbf{s}_{i-1},\, \bar{\mathbf{h}}_j\right), \qquad j = 1, \dots, T_x,
$$

where $a$ is a function that returns a number (how compatible that decoder state and that encoder
state are), and it uses the previous state $\mathbf{s}_{i-1}$ for a reason of order, not taste:
computing $\mathbf{s}_i$ requires already having $\mathbf{c}_i$, which requires the weights, which
require the scores. The latest state that exists when it's time to score is $\mathbf{s}_{i-1}$. That's
why the scores come out row by row: those of step $i$ can't be computed until step $i-1$ has
finished.

The scores are raw numbers that can be negative, so they have to be turned into a mixture. A softmax
does that, and it's worth noticing what it runs over:

$$
\alpha_{ij} = \frac{\exp(e_{ij})}{\sum_{k=1}^{T_x} \exp(e_{ik})}.
$$

The weights come out positive because of the exponential and sum to one because of the denominator,
which are the mixture's two conditions. As a bonus, the sharing is smooth: a slightly larger score
takes a bit more of the weight, not all of it.

<Callout type="warning" title="This softmax doesn't share out over the vocabulary">
Every softmax in the course until now shared probability out over the entries of a vocabulary. This
one shares it out over the *input positions*: the index in the denominator runs over
$k = 1, \dots, T_x$, with step $i$ held fixed. The two live in the same decoder step but over
different axes ($\hat{\mathbf{y}}_i$ decides what to write, $\alpha_{i\cdot}$ decides what to look
at), and confusing them is the quickest way to misread any implementation of this.
</Callout>

Two consequences follow, and the first is a bill. There's one score for every pair of step and
position, so translating a sentence costs $T_x \cdot T_y$ evaluations of $a$: the price grows with the
*product* of the two lengths. The fixed summary paid nothing, and that's exactly what was choking it.
Still, it's worth knowing from the start that looking back doesn't come free.

The second explains why the softmax is used instead of something more direct. The natural move would
be to keep the best-scored state, $\arg\max_j e_{ij}$, and save the whole sum. But that returns a
position, and a position is a whole number: nudge the parameters of $a$ by a millionth and the index
doesn't budge, until one day it jumps. The derivative of $\mathbf{c}_i$ with respect to those
parameters is zero wherever it exists, so the loss would have no way of teaching $a$ to score better.
With the softmax, $\mathbf{c}_i$ depends continuously on the scores, and
<Leccion slug="regla-de-la-cadena">the chain rule</Leccion> passes through the weights the same way it
passes through anything else. Choosing smoothly is what makes choosing learnable.

The function $a$ is still missing, and this lesson doesn't define it. All it needs from $a$ is that it
returns a number and is differentiable; what form it takes, how many parameters it carries and how
those parameters are trained are the next lesson's business.

## The per-step context, in NumPy

The cell takes the explorable's alignment (my numbers again) and one state per input token, and does
three things. It checks the one property you can't see by looking at the map: that every row sums to
one. It builds all eight contexts in one go, because mixing every row at once is a matrix product. And
it repeats the calculation with the frozen alignment, so you can compare the two.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

# The explorable's alignment: row = output step, column = input position.
SOURCE = ["ayer", "leí", "un", "libro", "muy", "bueno"]
TARGET = ["yesterday", "i", "read", "a", "very", "good", "book", "<EOS>"]
alpha = np.array([[0.86, 0.06, 0.03, 0.02, 0.02, 0.01],    # yesterday
                  [0.09, 0.74, 0.05, 0.07, 0.03, 0.02],    # i      <- from "leí"
                  [0.04, 0.81, 0.04, 0.06, 0.03, 0.02],    # read   <- from "leí" again
                  [0.02, 0.05, 0.72, 0.15, 0.03, 0.03],    # a
                  [0.02, 0.03, 0.04, 0.06, 0.79, 0.06],    # very
                  [0.02, 0.02, 0.03, 0.05, 0.10, 0.78],    # good
                  [0.02, 0.03, 0.06, 0.76, 0.05, 0.08],    # book   <- back to "libro"
                  [0.02, 0.03, 0.03, 0.12, 0.07, 0.73]])   # <EOS>
# One state per input token: d_h = 5 coordinates in (-1, 1), like a tanh.
H = np.array([[ 0.82, -0.31,  0.10,  0.45, -0.62], [-0.55,  0.71,  0.28, -0.14,  0.36],
              [ 0.12,  0.44, -0.77,  0.21,  0.08], [ 0.39, -0.68,  0.52,  0.77, -0.11],
              [-0.24,  0.15,  0.63, -0.58,  0.49], [ 0.66,  0.29, -0.35,  0.18,  0.74]])
T_y, T_x = alpha.shape

# What the map doesn't let you see, and without which this wouldn't be a mixture:
assert np.allclose(alpha.sum(axis=1), 1.0), "some row doesn't sum to 1"

C = alpha @ H                                     # c_i = sum_j alpha_ij * h_j, all T_y at once
fixed = np.zeros_like(alpha); fixed[:, -1] = 1.0  # the fixed summary, written as an alignment
C_fixed = fixed @ H

print("step  writes       pulls mostly from  weight  distance to the fixed summary")
for i in range(T_y):
    j = int(alpha[i].argmax())
    print("  %d   %-11s  %-18s  %.2f   %.2f"
          % (i + 1, TARGET[i], SOURCE[j], alpha[i, j], np.linalg.norm(C[i] - C_fixed[i])))

print()
print("distinct contexts:  with attention %d of %d,  with the fixed summary %d of %d"
      % (len(np.unique(C.round(9), axis=0)), T_y,
         len(np.unique(C_fixed.round(9), axis=0)), T_y))
print("and that one is the state of '%s':" % SOURCE[-1], np.allclose(C_fixed[0], H[-1]))
`} />

The middle column is the map read out loud, and the last two lines are the finding: with the
alignment above, the eight steps receive eight different vectors, and with the frozen one they
receive the same vector eight times, the state of the input's last token. The last column shows how
much each step was missing, and it deserves a slow look, because not every step misses the same
amount. The two small values, $0.25$ and $0.32$, belong to <W>good</W> and to <W>\<EOS></W>, the
end-of-sequence token, which are the only two steps that pull from <W>bueno</W>, the very token the
fixed summary carried inside it. The values for <W>yesterday</W> and <W>book</W> are above $1.35$.
The fixed summary wasn't equally bad everywhere: it got things right where the sentence ends and
wrong everywhere else, which is where it broke down in the previous lesson. Change one weight in the
`alpha` matrix and run the cell again: the `assert` complains before you can look at anything, which
is exactly its job.

The mechanism is complete except for one piece, and it's the piece that decides everything else.
Nobody computed this lesson's weights: I wrote them into a matrix, choosing them so that the map
would show a reordering and a shared token. A real model has no one to write them for it. It has to
score each pair $(\mathbf{s}_{i-1}, \bar{\mathbf{h}}_j)$ on its own, with a function that starts out
knowing as little as the rest of the network and learns alongside it.

That function is $a$, and <Leccion slug="bahdanau">the next lesson, on Bahdanau's attention</Leccion>,
is the one that writes it. It gives $a$ its own parameters (a small layer, with its matrix and its
vector), puts it into the same gradient descent that already trains the encoder and the decoder, and
derives how the gradient reaches it, which turns out to be along two routes at once. The rest of the
block then shows that there's more than one reasonable way to score a pair, and that the choice
between them ends up deciding what Block 5 looks like.
