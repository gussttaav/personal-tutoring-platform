    solution: |
      import numpy as np


      def gradients(X, Y, W1, b1, W2, b2):
          B = len(X)
          H1 = np.tanh(X @ W1.T + b1)
          Y_hat = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-(H1 @ W2.T + b2)))
          D2 = Y_hat - Y
          D1 = (D2 @ W2) * (1.0 - H1 ** 2)
          return (D1.T @ X) / B, D1.sum(0) / B, (D2.T @ H1) / B, D2.sum(0) / B


      def train(X, Y, W1, b1, W2, b2, eta, steps):
          W1, b1, W2, b2 = W1.copy(), b1.copy(), W2.copy(), b2.copy()
          curve = []
          for t in range(steps + 1):
              H1 = np.tanh(X @ W1.T + b1)
              Y_hat = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-(H1 @ W2.T + b2)))
              p = np.clip(Y_hat, 1e-12, 1.0 - 1e-12)
              curve.append(-np.mean(Y * np.log(p) + (1.0 - Y) * np.log(1.0 - p)))
              if t == steps:
                  break
              gW1, gb1, gW2, gb2 = gradients(X, Y, W1, b1, W2, b2)
              W1 -= eta * gW1
              b1 -= eta * gb1
              W2 -= eta * gW2
              b2 -= eta * gb2
          return curve, W1, b1, W2, b2
    explanation: |
      `gradients` is this lesson's backward pass written out exactly as derived, and the two
      real decisions sit in the second-to-last line. `D2 @ W2` carries no transpose (the
      forward pass already carries it, in `X @ W1.T`), because here the examples go by rows
      on both sides of the product; and the factor `(1.0 - H1 ** 2)` is the derivative of
      $\tanh$ written over the activation instead of the pre-activation, which is exactly
      what lets you skip keeping $\mathbf{Z}^{(1)}$ around at all.

      The `/ B` appears four times, and it is the only thing separating this gradient from

      step stays the same and the shape tests still pass. What changes is the scale, which

      never outside it. And the loss gets measured *before* the update, so `curve[0]` is
      the untrained network and `curve[-1]` the final one, which is why there are
      `steps + 1` values rather than `steps`.

{/* COURSE-P5-02 — Block 2, Lesson 9. */}

Backpropagation hands over the gradient of a whole network in a single backward pass, and the
previous lesson derived it without skipping a single step. What it produced, though, was a list of
numbers on a screen. A printed gradient moves no weight on its own: it's an instruction (which way is
down, and how much of it falls to each parameter), and somebody still has to carry that out, a
thousand times over, before a network goes from answering anything at all to answering well. That
loop hasn't been written in any cell of this course yet. Its four pieces have, spread across four
lessons of this block, and putting them together also settles a debt open since the very beginning:
the eight weights of the review neuron and the seven that separate the XOR (exclusive or) phrases
were written by me, by looking at the data alone.

The place to check all this has been chosen since then.
<Leccion slug="xor-y-capas-ocultas">The lesson on XOR and hidden layers</Leccion> proved that no
single line leaves <W>it's good</W> and <W>not bad</W> on one side and the other two phrases on the
other, then solved all four with a hidden layer whose seven numbers I picked by hand. If the loop
actually works, the network has to find weights that get all four right (not necessarily mine)
starting from numbers that mean nothing at all. And if it doesn't work, you'll notice immediately:
with four examples and two coordinates, there's nowhere left for a fault to hide.

The four pieces aren't loose ends. They fall into an order, and that order closes back on itself.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
Training repeats four things you already know how to do. The only thing left to decide is
where the weights come from the first time, and the comfortable answer (make them all equal) leaves
the network frozen forever.

## From the example to the batch: the backward pass, stacked by rows

The previous lesson derived the backward pass for one example, in column vectors. The forward pass
has been written for a whole batch since <Leccion slug="forward-pass">the lesson on the forward
pass</Leccion>: one example per row in $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times d_0}$, and from there
$\mathbf{H}^{(l)} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times d_l}$ layer by layer. The backward pass still needs that
same treatment, and it comes down to two changes: one of shape, one of size.

The shape change is stacking. Call $\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(l)} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times d_l}$ the
**batch error** at layer $l$: row $i$ is the $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}$ of example $i$, laid on its
side, exactly the way row $i$ of $\mathbf{H}^{(l)}$ is its activation laid on its side. Capital for
the batch, lower case for the example, this block's usual convention. The output layer enters exactly
as before, only now with $B$ rows at once:

$$
\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(L)} = \hat{\mathbf{Y}} - \mathbf{Y} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times d_L}.
$$

And the recurrence transposes whole. Take the previous lesson's version, for example $i$,
$\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}_i = \left(\left(\mathbf{W}^{(l+1)}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l+1)}_i\right) \odot \varphi^{\prime}\left(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}_i\right)$,
and transpose it. Transposing a product reverses the order of its factors, and the Hadamard product
works coordinate by coordinate, so it doesn't even notice:

$$
\left(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}_i\right)^{\top} = \left(\left(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l+1)}_i\right)^{\top}\mathbf{W}^{(l+1)}\right) \odot \left(\varphi^{\prime}\left(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}_i\right)\right)^{\top},
$$

which is row $i$ of what you're after, written using rows $i$ of $\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(l+1)}$ and of
$\mathbf{Z}^{(l)}$. Stack all $B$ of them, and you get

$$
\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(l)} = \left(\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(l+1)}\mathbf{W}^{(l+1)}\right) \odot \varphi^{\prime}\left(\mathbf{Z}^{(l)}\right),
$$

where what's worth noticing is what disappeared: the transpose. That's neither an oversight nor a
different convention. The forward pass *gained* a transpose when it moved to rows
($\mathbf{W}^{(l)}\mathbf{h}$ became $\mathbf{H}\left(\mathbf{W}^{(l)}\right)^{\top}$), and the
backward pass *loses* it for the exact same reason, since it started from a matrix that was already
transposed. Both times, it's the same product read with the examples sitting in rows.

The size change is the $1/B$, and it comes straight from how the loss is defined. $\mathcal{L}$ is
the mean of the $\ell$ across the $B$ examples, and differentiating a mean means taking the mean of
the derivatives, so the gradient of the batch is the mean of the $B$ gradients the previous lesson
would have handed you one at a time. That mean turns out to be another matrix product:

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}^{(l)}}\mathcal{L}
= \frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}_i\left(\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)}_i\right)^{\top}
= \frac{1}{B}\left(\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(l)}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{(l-1)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_l \times d_{l-1}},
\qquad
\nabla_{\mathbf{b}^{(l)}}\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{B}\left(\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(l)}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{1}_B,
$$

with $\mathbf{1}_B \in \mathbb{R}^{B}$ the vector of ones from <Leccion slug="forward-pass">the
lesson on the forward pass</Leccion>. The sum over examples the previous lesson wrote out by hand is
exactly the index the matrix product runs over and discards, which is why the result has the shape
of $\mathbf{W}^{(l)}$ and carries no memory of $B$ at all.

With that in hand, the descent rule from <Leccion slug="descenso-gradiente">the lesson on gradient
descent</Leccion> doesn't change by a single letter:
$\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta\,\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta_t)$, applied to each matrix and
each bias vector on its own. One concession, worth saying out loud: every step here looks at the
entire **training set** (the labelled examples the network has, in this case the four phrases),
exactly as that lesson did with the ten reviews. Splitting it into pieces and taking one step per
piece only becomes necessary once the set grows large, and this one has all of four rows.

Everything the four-phrase network needs is on the table now: the smallest multilayer perceptron
(MLP) worth trying it on, with two inputs, four hidden neurons using $\tanh$ and one output neuron
with a sigmoid, that is, $L = 2$ and $d_0, d_1, d_2 = 2, 4, 1$. Its forward pass takes one line per
layer,

$$
\mathbf{H}^{(1)} = \tanh\left(\mathbf{X}\left(\mathbf{W}^{(1)}\right)^{\top} + \mathbf{1}_B\left(\mathbf{b}^{(1)}\right)^{\top}\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 4},
\qquad
\hat{\mathbf{Y}} = \sigma\left(\mathbf{H}^{(1)}\left(\mathbf{W}^{(2)}\right)^{\top} + \mathbf{1}_B\left(\mathbf{b}^{(2)}\right)^{\top}\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 1},
$$

with $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 2}$ and $B = 4$, and its backward pass is the three
formulas above with $L = 2$. Twelve weights and five biases: seventeen numbers that all need to go
somewhere before the very first step.

### Four identical neurons are a single neuron

Deciding where those seventeen numbers start is the one call no earlier formula makes, and the
comfortable answer, all of them zero, fails outright. Follow the arithmetic through with that same
network and seventeen zeros. Then $\mathbf{Z}^{(1)} = \mathbf{0}$, and since $\tanh(0) = 0$, also
$\mathbf{H}^{(1)} = \mathbf{0}$; from there $\hat{\mathbf{Y}} = \sigma(\mathbf{0})$, which is $1/2$
for all four phrases. Feed that into the two gradients above:

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}^{(2)}}\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{B}\left(\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(2)}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{(1)} = \mathbf{0},
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(1)} = \left(\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(2)}\mathbf{W}^{(2)}\right) \odot \varphi^{\prime}\left(\mathbf{Z}^{(1)}\right) = \mathbf{0},
$$

the first because $\mathbf{H}^{(1)}$ is null and the second because $\mathbf{W}^{(2)}$ is. With
$\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(1)} = \mathbf{0}$, the two gradients of the first layer vanish too, so the
step leaves the network exactly where it was, and the next one finds the same starting point all
over again. The hidden layer will never move.

And zero isn't really the problem, it's a particular case of something much wider. Take two hidden
neurons $j$ and $k$ that start out with the same input weights, the same bias and the same output
weight. The forward pass gives them the same pre-activation and the same activation,
$z^{(1)}_j = z^{(1)}_k$ and $h^{(1)}_j = h^{(1)}_k$; the backward pass makes them receive the same
error, $\delta^{(1)}_j = \delta^{(1)}_k$; and with the same error and the same activation, all three
of their gradients coincide:

$$
\left(\nabla_{\mathbf{W}^{(1)}}\mathcal{L}\right)_{jm} = \left(\nabla_{\mathbf{W}^{(1)}}\mathcal{L}\right)_{km} \;\; \text{for all } m,
\qquad
\nabla_{b^{(1)}_j}\mathcal{L} = \nabla_{b^{(1)}_k}\mathcal{L},
\qquad
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}^{(2)}_{1j}}\mathcal{L} = \nabla_{\mathbf{W}^{(2)}_{1k}}\mathcal{L}.
$$

Subtracting equal quantities from equal quantities preserves the equality, so the two neurons come
out of the step exactly as identical as they went in. And out of the next one after that.

That single line is the very one from <Leccion slug="xor-y-capas-ocultas">the lesson on XOR and

width of one neuron, no matter how loudly $d_1$ claims four.

Seen from the seventeen numbers rather than from the plane, the equality turns into a trap shaped
like a subspace. A configuration with all four neurons identical has only five real numbers dressed
up as seventeen: one row of $\mathbf{W}^{(1)}$, one bias, one weight of $\mathbf{W}^{(2)}$, and
$b^{(2)}$. Together they form a five-dimensional subspace sitting inside the seventeen, and the
gradient always comes out tangent to it.

Nothing in the algorithm ever breaks that equality on its own, because everything it does is
deterministic: the break has to come from outside, and the cheapest source of "different" is a
random draw. Choosing those seventeen starting numbers is the **initialisation**, the one point in
training where chance actually enters. Its role isn't quite what it looks like: it contributes no
information whatsoever about the phrases, it only pulls some neurons apart from others so the
gradient can push them down different paths. How large those numbers are matters too (tiny weights
end up looking too much like the case above), and the third cell measures exactly that. Which scale
to pick in general depends on the depth of the network, and it comes back in Block 3, on recurrent
networks, once the problem stops being a minor inconvenience and becomes the whole subject.

## The loop in NumPy, and the loss falling

The first cell turns the formulas above into NumPy: the forward pass, the loss and the backward
pass, with no loop yet. Run it and look at the column of shapes, then the last line.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

# The four XOR phrases: x1 is +1 if "not" appears and x2 is +1 if the
# evaluative word is "good". The label is 1 if the phrase speaks well.
X = np.array([[-1., 1.], [-1., -1.], [1., 1.], [1., -1.]])
Y = np.array([[1.], [0.], [0.], [1.]])

The column of shapes holds the whole division of labour: the $4$ examples show up in $\mathbf{X}$,
in $\mathbf{H}^{(1)}$ and in $\hat{\mathbf{Y}}$, and in none of the three gradients, which come out
with the shape of their own parameters instead. The last line checks the $1/B$ and returns zero: the
four outer products from the previous lesson, averaged by hand, match the matrix product above entry
for entry.

The second cell puts that into a loop and repeats it two thousand times, with $\eta = 0.5$. Run it
and watch the loss fall.

They're better than that: they're the same ones, rediscovered independently. Look at neurons $2$ and
$4$. Their weights point in almost exactly the same direction ($(1.83,\ 1.77)$ and $(1.79,\ 1.85)$,
both close to $x_1 + x_2$), their biases are opposite ($-1.80$ and $+1.80$), and so are their output
weights ($-3.98$ and $+3.93$). That's the strip from <Leccion slug="xor-y-capas-ocultas">the lesson

table confirms it: that pair contributes $+7.5$ to the two positive phrases and only $+0.2$ to the
other two. And the pair $1$ and $3$ does the same on the other diagonal with the sign reversed,
subtracting $6.7$ exactly where the first pair stays quiet.

What's left is deciding when to stop, and it's the flimsiest of the three decisions by far. Here I
stop at a round number of steps because the curve has flattened out; I could equally well have
stopped once the loss stopped falling by a thousandth, or once the norm of the gradient dropped
below some threshold, and all three are rules of thumb. Correct-answer counts are no use here: by
step $25$ there are already four of them, and there they stay, while the loss keeps falling by a
factor of three hundred afterwards. And all three rules share a limit none of them can fix, because
all three only ever look at these same four phrases.

The third cell goes back to the start of the loop and changes exactly one thing: where the weights
come from. Six scales at the same seed, and below them, four genuinely identical neurons.

import numpy as np

X = np.array([[-1., 1.], [-1., -1.], [1., 1.], [1., -1.]])
Y = np.array([[1.], [0.], [0.], [1.]])
B, eta, steps = 4, 0.5, 1000


def train(scale, identical=False):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(0)               # the same seed in all of them
    W1, W2 = rng.normal(size=(4, 2)) * scale, rng.normal(size=(1, 4)) * scale
    if identical:                                # four copies of one and the same neuron
        W1, W2 = np.repeat(W1[:1], 4, axis=0), np.full((1, 4), W2[0, 0])
    b1, b2 = np.zeros(4), np.zeros(1)
    curve = []
    for t in range(steps + 1):
        H1 = np.tanh(X @ W1.T + b1)
        Y_hat = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-(H1 @ W2.T + b2)))
        p = np.clip(Y_hat, 1e-12, 1.0 - 1e-12)
        curve.append(-np.mean(Y * np.log(p) + (1.0 - Y) * np.log(1.0 - p)))
        if t == steps:
            break
        D2 = Y_hat - Y
        D1 = (D2 @ W2) * (1.0 - H1 ** 2)
        W1 -= eta * (D1.T @ X) / B
        b1 -= eta * D1.sum(0) / B
        W2 -= eta * (D2.T @ H1) / B
        b2 -= eta * D2.sum(0) / B
    return curve, int(((Y_hat >= 0.5) == (Y == 1.0)).sum()), W1


print("%7s %8s %8s %8s %9s  %s"
      % ("scale", "step 0", "step 50", "step 200", "step 1000", "correct"))
for scale in [0.0, 0.01, 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 10.0]:
    curve, correct, _ = train(scale)
    print("%7.2f %8.4f %8.4f %8.4f %9.4f  %d of 4"
          % (scale, curve[0], curve[50], curve[200], curve[1000], correct))

# And symmetry with no zeros: four copies of one neuron, with normal weights.
curve, correct, W1 = train(1.0, identical=True)
print()
print("four identical neurons: loss %.4f -> %.4f, %d of 4"
      % (curve[0], curve[-1], correct))
print("W(1) after 1000 steps:", np.round(W1, 4).tolist())
print("are the four rows still the same one?", bool(np.allclose(W1, W1[0])))

The first two rows of the table never budge from $0.6931$, which is $\log 2$: the network answers
$0.5$ to all four phrases at the start and is still answering the same thing a thousand steps later.
The second of the two is the one that actually teaches something, since its scale is $0.01$ rather
than zero: the four neurons really are different from each other. What happens instead is that each
layer receives a gradient multiplied by the other one's scale, and with two small factors stacked
together, everything advances hundreds of times more slowly: that network isn't stuck at all, it
still reaches the four phrases, only around step fifty thousand instead. At the other end, the scale $10$ starts at
$4.73$ (confidently wrong) and recovers anyway: with only two layers that comes cheap, and once
depth increases, it stops being cheap at all.

The last three lines show symmetry with no zeros anywhere in sight. All four neurons start with
proper normal weights, only equal to one another, and a thousand steps later they're still the same
row repeated four times over. The loss has fallen from $0.7240$ to $0.6931$, which is exactly what a
network that answers the same thing to everything achieves: four neurons working as one, and one
neuron cannot do the job.

The network gets the four phrases it has seen right, and there is no fifth to check it against.
That isn't a defect of the training, it's that here getting it right and genuinely learning are
indistinguishable, since with only four examples, memorising the answer and understanding the rule
produce exactly the same numbers. Nothing this loop prints says what the network would do with
<W>not that good</W>, and the loop has no way of ever finding out.

There's also a debt this whole business with the weights has left sitting in plain sight. I invented
the two input coordinates myself: whether <W>not</W> appears, and whether the evaluative word is
<W>good</W> or <W>bad</W>, are two questions somebody had to formulate after reading the four
phrases, and an arbitrary piece of text doesn't arrive already packaged into two numbers. It did
