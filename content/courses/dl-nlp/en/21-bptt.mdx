      import numpy as np


      def bptt(hs, X, Whh, dhT):
          d_h, d_model, T = Whh.shape[0], X.shape[1], len(hs) - 1
          gWhh, gWxh, gbh = np.zeros((d_h, d_h)), np.zeros((d_h, d_model)), np.zeros(d_h)
          dh = np.array(dhT, dtype=float)                  # ∂ℓ/∂h_T, copied so the original stays put
          for t in range(T, 0, -1):
              delta = dh * (1 - np.asarray(hs[t]) ** 2)    # δ_t = (∂ℓ/∂h_t) ⊙ (1 - h_t²)
              gWhh += np.outer(delta, hs[t - 1])           # δ_t h_{t-1}ᵀ : what step t contributes
              gWxh += np.outer(delta, X[t - 1])
              gbh += delta
              dh = Whh.T @ delta                           # ∂ℓ/∂h_{t-1} : transport with Whhᵀ
          return gWhh, gWxh, gbh
    explanation: |
      The structure is the previous block's backward pass, with one difference: the gradients
      aren't returned one step at a time, they're *accumulated* in `gWhh`, `gWxh` and `gbh`,
      because the same matrix receives a contribution at every position. The loop runs from `T`
      down to `1` because $\boldsymbol{\delta}_t$ needs the error already transported back from
      $t+1$; running it forwards would call for a $\boldsymbol{\delta}$ that doesn't exist yet.

      Three details do the work. `dh = np.array(dhT, dtype=float)` copies the incoming error
      before the loop starts carrying it backwards. Rebinding the name, as `dh = Whh.T @ delta`
      does, would never touch the caller's array, but an in-place update such as `dh *= …` would
      write straight into `dhT`; the copy keeps the function safe whichever of the two you write.
      The mask `(1 - hs[t] ** 2)` is $\tanh^{\prime}$ read off the state, with no need to store the

{/* COURSE-P5-03 — Block 3, Lesson 3. COURSE-P11-06 — English translation. */}

The previous lesson, on the vanilla recurrent neural network (RNN), ended with a network that reads
text of any length using two matrices and a loop, but that nobody has trained yet: every weight in
it still holds the value it was given when the network was built. Training it means asking for the
gradient of the loss with respect to those weights, and that question raises something the previous
block never had to deal with. An RNN doesn't spread its work across one matrix per layer:
$\mathbf{W}_{hh}$ is a single matrix that the loop uses at every position, so it's tangled up in
the whole sequence.

How do you differentiate with respect to a weight that takes part in all $T$ steps at once? The
previous block already has both halves of the answer. <Leccion slug="regla-de-la-cadena">The chain rule</Leccion> says what happens when a weight reaches
the loss along several paths; <Leccion slug="backpropagation">backpropagation</Leccion> gets every derivative in a network in a single pass
from the end to the start, without probing one weight at a time. What's missing is putting the two
together inside a loop, and the result has a name of its own: backpropagation through time (BPTT).
This lesson doesn't stop at stating it: it derives it in full and then checks, number by number,
that its gradients match what a numerical probe measures.

Unrolling the loop lays the network out as a row of steps, and you can walk the backward pass along
that row by hand. The loss sits at the end, hanging off the last state; to reach a weight, its
influence has to retrace the route the forward pass took, from the last state to the one before,
and from that one to the one before it, crossing the same $\mathbf{W}_{hh}$ at every hop. There's no
other route, because there was no other route forward.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
BPTT is the previous block's backward pass with the layers laid out along time. Because the same
$\mathbf{W}_{hh}$ acts at every position, its gradient doesn't come from any single step: it's the
sum of what each step asks of it.
</Callout>

## The error that travels back, one step at a time

Fix one sequence, a single document, and do the derivation for that alone. A document is one whole
example (one $\hat y$, one label), so its loss is $\ell$, the loss of a single example, as it has
been throughout the previous block since <Leccion slug="funcion-de-perdida">the lesson on loss functions</Leccion>. The $T$ tokens the loop runs
through aren't $T$ examples: they're positions inside this one document, the axis that plays the
role of the layers here, and none of them is the batch. The batch is the other axis, the familiar
one ($B$ documents instead of one), and its mean, $\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{B}\sum_i \ell_i$, averages
these per-document gradients one level further up, without touching anything that follows. The
answer is read off the last state, as in the previous lesson:

$$
\hat{y} = \sigma\left(\mathbf{W}_{hy}\mathbf{h}_T + \mathbf{b}_y\right),
$$

so the recurrence's weights reach the loss in exactly one place: through $\mathbf{h}_T$. The rest of
the backward pass is a matter of reconstructing, step by step, how each state affects that last one.

First, give a name to something the previous lesson left unnamed. The pre-activation of step $t$,
the argument of the $\tanh$, is

$$
\mathbf{p}_t = \mathbf{W}_{hh}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{W}_{xh}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_h \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h},
\qquad
\mathbf{h}_t = \tanh\left(\mathbf{p}_t\right),
$$

and call the gradient of the loss with respect to that pre-activation the **error of step** $t$,
$\boldsymbol{\delta}_t = \nabla_{\mathbf{p}_t}\,\ell \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}$. It's exactly the
$\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}$ from <Leccion slug="backpropagation">the lesson on backpropagation</Leccion> (a layer's error, taken at its pre-activation),
except that the index now counts steps in time rather than layers.

That error runs from the end back to the start, in two moves per step. The $\tanh$ acts coordinate
by coordinate, so getting from the gradient at $\mathbf{h}_t$ to the gradient at $\mathbf{p}_t$ means
multiplying by its slope. Using the identity $\tanh^{\prime} = 1 - \tanh^{2}$ from <Leccion slug="funciones-activacion">the lesson on activation functions</Leccion> together with $\mathbf{h}_t = \tanh(\mathbf{p}_t)$, that slope is $\mathbf{1} - \mathbf{h}_t \odot \mathbf{h}_t$,
which you can read straight off the state the forward pass already stored, without keeping
$\mathbf{p}_t$ around. That leaves the other factor, $\nabla_{\mathbf{h}_t}\ell$, which splits into
two cases depending on where the loss reaches $\mathbf{h}_t$ from: at the last step, only from the
output layer; at every other step, only from the next one:

$$
\boldsymbol{\delta}_T = \left(\left(\hat{y} - y\right)\mathbf{W}_{hy}^{\top}\right) \odot \left(\mathbf{1} - \mathbf{h}_T \odot \mathbf{h}_T\right),
$$

$$
\boldsymbol{\delta}_t = \left(\mathbf{W}_{hh}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{t+1}\right) \odot \left(\mathbf{1} - \mathbf{h}_t \odot \mathbf{h}_t\right), \qquad t = T-1, \dots, 1.
$$

The starting point, $\boldsymbol{\delta}_T$, is the backward pass through the previous lesson's
output layer: the sigmoid with cross-entropy delivers $\hat{y} - y$, and $\mathbf{W}_{hy}^{\top}$
distributes it across the $d_h$ coordinates of the state. The recurrence handles everything else,
and it's the two moves from <Leccion slug="backpropagation">the lesson on backpropagation</Leccion> with nothing changed: it **transports** the error with $\mathbf{W}_{hh}^{\top}$ and **masks** it with the
slope of the $\tanh$. The only difference is that the transport always uses the same matrix. Hold on
to that, because it's half of <Leccion slug="gradiente-desvanecido">a later lesson, the one on the vanishing gradient</Leccion>.

<Details summary="See the transport index by index">
Here it is with no matrices in the way, for an interior step $t < T$, where $\mathbf{h}_t$ reaches
the loss only through the next step. Coordinate $j$ of $\nabla_{\mathbf{h}_t}\ell$ is the derivative
of $\ell$ with respect to $h_{t,j}$, and $h_{t,j}$ feeds into every one of the $d_h$ coordinates of
the pre-activation $\mathbf{p}_{t+1}$: it's the sum over paths from <Leccion slug="regla-de-la-cadena">the lesson on the chain rule</Leccion>, with $d_h$ paths.

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial h_{t,j}} = \sum_{i=1}^{d_h} \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial p_{t+1,i}}\,\frac{\partial p_{t+1,i}}{\partial h_{t,j}} = \sum_{i=1}^{d_h} \delta_{t+1,i}\,(\mathbf{W}_{hh})_{ij},
$$

where the first factor is $\delta_{t+1,i}$ by the definition of the error, and the second comes from
the fact that $p_{t+1,i} = \sum_k (\mathbf{W}_{hh})_{ik}\,h_{t,k} + \dots$ depends on $h_{t,j}$ only
through the term $k = j$, whose derivative is $(\mathbf{W}_{hh})_{ij}$. That sum runs down column $j$
of $\mathbf{W}_{hh}$, which is row $j$ of its transpose, in other words coordinate $j$ of
$\mathbf{W}_{hh}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{t+1}$. So $\nabla_{\mathbf{h}_t}\ell = \mathbf{W}_{hh}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{t+1}$,
and multiplying by the slope of the $\tanh$ gives $\boldsymbol{\delta}_t$. At the last step, the same
argument starts from the output layer instead of the next step, and that's where the other equation
comes from.
</Details>

## The gradient of the shared weight is a sum

With one error per step in hand, the weight gradients follow without any further walking. This is
where the sum the previous lesson promised turns up. Look at where $\mathbf{W}_{hh}$ takes part: in
the pre-activation of *every* step, $\mathbf{p}_t = \mathbf{W}_{hh}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \dots$,
with $t$ running from $1$ to $T$. It's a single weight reached along $T$ paths, one per position, and <Leccion slug="regla-de-la-cadena">the chain rule</Leccion> from the previous block has already settled what to do about that: across paths, you add. Each path contributes
what <Leccion slug="backpropagation">the lesson on backpropagation</Leccion> gives for a pre-activation (the error times the transposed input), and the total
is the sum of all $T$ contributions:

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{hh}}\ell = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \boldsymbol{\delta}_t\,\mathbf{h}_{t-1}^{\top},
\qquad
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{xh}}\ell = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \boldsymbol{\delta}_t\,\mathbf{x}_t^{\top},
\qquad
\nabla_{\mathbf{b}_h}\ell = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \boldsymbol{\delta}_t.
$$

Put them next to the previous block's formulas and you'll find they're the same. There, a layer's
gradient was $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}\left(\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)}\right)^{\top}$, a single outer
product; here it's that same outer product summed over the steps, because there each matrix appeared
once, and here $\mathbf{W}_{hh}$ appears $T$ times. That sum is the only difference between
backpropagation and backpropagation through time, and it's the exact price of sharing: one matrix,
$T$ jobs, $T$ contributions to collect.

Two checks settle the account. The first is about shapes, the habit from <Leccion slug="backpropagation">the lesson on backpropagation</Leccion>: each
term of $\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{hh}}\ell$ is $d_h \times d_h$, and so is the sum, so the gradient has the
shape of the matrix it corrects however long the sequence is. $T$ decides how many terms get added,
not the size of the result. The second looks at the first term: in $\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{hh}}\ell$,
the term for $t = 1$ is $\boldsymbol{\delta}_1\mathbf{h}_0^{\top}$, and since $\mathbf{h}_0 = \mathbf{0}$,
it's the zero matrix. That fits with the previous lesson, where
$\mathbf{h}_1 = \tanh\left(\mathbf{W}_{xh}\mathbf{x}_1 + \mathbf{b}_h\right)$ had no
$\mathbf{W}_{hh}$ term: the first step never used that matrix, so it owes it no gradient. In
$\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{xh}}\ell$, on the other hand, the $t = 1$ term does count, because
$\mathbf{x}_1$ is a real token.

That leaves the output weights, $\mathbf{W}_{hy}$ and $\mathbf{b}_y$, and they bring nothing new.
They appear only once (the network reads out its answer once, at the end), so their gradient is an
outer product with no sum, $\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{hy}}\ell = \left(\hat{y} - y\right)\mathbf{h}_T^{\top}$:
the previous block's output layer, exactly as it was. The sum over time belongs to the three shared
weights, and to nothing else.

## BPTT in NumPy, against the numerical probe

This is the same check as in <Leccion slug="backpropagation">the lesson on backpropagation</Leccion>: differentiate by hand, then compare every
number against a probe that nudges each weight slightly and measures how the loss changes. The setup
is a toy RNN with a fixed seed, a state of $d_h = 3$ and a sequence of $T = 5$. Run it and look at
two things: each step's contribution to $\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{hh}}$ (the last one, for $t = 1$,
comes out as zero) and the final line.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
d_model, d_h, T = 4, 3, 5
Wxh = rng.normal(size=(d_h, d_model)) * 0.5
Whh = rng.normal(size=(d_h, d_h)) * 0.5
bh = np.zeros(d_h)
Why = rng.normal(size=(1, d_h)) * 0.5             # scalar output: 1 x d_h
by = np.zeros(1)
X = rng.normal(size=(T, d_model)) * 0.5           # one row per position
y = 1.0                                            # the document's label


def forward():
    hs = [np.zeros(d_h)]                           # h_0 = 0
    for x in X:
        hs.append(np.tanh(Whh @ hs[-1] + Wxh @ x + bh))
    p = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-(Why @ hs[-1] + by)[0]))
    return hs, p, -(y * np.log(p) + (1 - y) * np.log(1 - p))


hs, p, loss = forward()
print("ŷ = %.4f   ℓ = %.4f" % (p, loss))

# Backwards: one δ per position, from the end to the start.
gWhh, gWxh, gbh = np.zeros_like(Whh), np.zeros_like(Wxh), np.zeros_like(bh)
dh = Why[0] * (p - y)                              # ∂ℓ/∂h_T, from the output layer
contributions = []
for t in range(T, 0, -1):
    delta = dh * (1 - hs[t] ** 2)                  # δ_t = (∂ℓ/∂h_t) ⊙ (1 - h_t²)
    gWhh += np.outer(delta, hs[t - 1])             # δ_t h_{t-1}ᵀ : what step t contributes
    gWxh += np.outer(delta, X[t - 1])
    gbh += delta
    contributions.append(np.linalg.norm(np.outer(delta, hs[t - 1])))
    dh = Whh.T @ delta                             # ∂ℓ/∂h_{t-1} : transport with Whhᵀ
gWhy, gby = np.outer([p - y], hs[T]), np.array([p - y])

print("each step's contribution to ∇Whh, from t=%d to t=1:" % T)
print("  ", np.round(contributions, 4), " <- the last one, t=1, is 0 because h_0 = 0")

# The probe: move each weight by hand and see how much the loss changes.
worst = 0.0
for P, G in [(Whh, gWhh), (Wxh, gWxh), (bh, gbh), (Why, gWhy), (by, gby)]:
    for k in np.ndindex(P.shape):
        v = P[k]
        P[k] = v + 1e-6; plus = forward()[2]
        P[k] = v - 1e-6; minus = forward()[2]
        P[k] = v
        worst = max(worst, abs((plus - minus) / 2e-6 - G[k]))
print("largest difference against the numerical probe: %.1e" % worst)
`} />

Check the output against the derivation. The list of contributions has five numbers, one per step,
and the last is exactly zero: step $t = 1$ multiplies by $\mathbf{h}_0 = \mathbf{0}$ and asks
nothing of $\mathbf{W}_{hh}$, exactly as the algebra predicted. And the last line is the whole
lesson: the gradients of all five groups of weights agree with the probe to within $10^{-10}$, which
is the probe's own error, not the algorithm's. The sum over time isn't an approximation or a
workaround; it's the exact gradient, and the probe confirms it number by number.

---

The gradient is done, exact and verified, but it carries a warning inside it that we haven't read
yet. The backward recurrence multiplies by $\mathbf{W}_{hh}^{\top}$ at every step (the same matrix,
once per position), and it also masks with the slopes $1 - \mathbf{h}_t \odot \mathbf{h}_t$, which <Leccion slug="funciones-activacion">the lesson on activation functions</Leccion> already showed switching off when the pre-activation $\mathbf{p}_t$
strays far from zero. An error that leaves position $T$ and has to reach position $1$ passes
through that product dozens of times, and multiplying by one and the same matrix that many times
does one of two things: it shrinks towards nothing, or it blows up.

Either outcome breaks exactly what the RNN was brought in to fix. If the gradient linking the first
position to the loss has vanished by the time it gets there, the network can't learn that something
at the start of the document mattered for the end: the long-range dependency, which was the whole
point of having memory. How much it shrinks or grows, and why that comes down to a single property
of $\mathbf{W}_{hh}$, is the subject of <Leccion slug="gradiente-desvanecido">the next lesson, on the vanishing gradient</Leccion>: we derive it
carefully and then watch it plummet on a plot as the distance grows.
