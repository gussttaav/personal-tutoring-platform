      import numpy as np


      def bptt(hs, X, Whh, dhT):
          d_h, d_model, T = Whh.shape[0], X.shape[1], len(hs) - 1
          gWhh, gWxh, gbh = np.zeros((d_h, d_h)), np.zeros((d_h, d_model)), np.zeros(d_h)
          dh = np.array(dhT, dtype=float)                  # ∂ℓ/∂h_T, copied so the original stays put
          for t in range(T, 0, -1):
              delta = dh * (1 - np.asarray(hs[t]) ** 2)    # δ_t = (∂ℓ/∂h_t) ⊙ (1 - h_t²)
              gWhh += np.outer(delta, hs[t - 1])           # δ_t h_{t-1}ᵀ : what step t contributes
              gWxh += np.outer(delta, X[t - 1])
              gbh += delta
              dh = Whh.T @ delta                           # ∂ℓ/∂h_{t-1} : transport with Whhᵀ
          return gWhh, gWxh, gbh
    explanation: |
      The structure is the previous block's backward pass, with one difference: the gradients
      aren't handed back step by step, they're *accumulated* in `gWhh`, `gWxh` and `gbh`, because
      the same matrix receives a contribution at every position. The loop runs from `T` down to `1`
      because $\boldsymbol{\delta}_t$ needs the error already transported from $t+1$; running it
      forwards would ask for a $\boldsymbol{\delta}$ that doesn't exist yet.

      Three details do the work. `dh = np.array(dhT, dtype=float)` copies the incoming error before
      the loop starts carrying it backwards. Rebinding it, as `dh = Whh.T @ delta` does, would never
      reach the caller's array; an in-place update such as `dh *= …` would write straight into
      `dhT`, and the copy is what keeps the function safe whichever of the two you write. The mask
      `(1 - hs[t] ** 2)` is $\tanh^{\prime}$ read off the state, without storing the pre-activation.
      And `np.outer(delta, hs[t - 1])` at step `t = 1` multiplies by $\mathbf{h}_0 = \mathbf{0}$, so
      that term goes in as the zero matrix: the first step owes nothing to $\mathbf{W}_{hh}$, because
      it never used it.

{/* COURSE-P5-03 — Block 3, Lesson 3. COURSE-P11-06 — English translation. */}

The previous lesson, on the vanilla recurrent neural network (RNN), ended with a network that reads
a text of any length using two matrices and a loop, and that nobody has trained yet: every weight in
it still holds the value it was given at construction. Training it means asking for the gradient of
the loss with respect to those weights, and the question turns up something the previous block never
had to face. An RNN doesn't spread its work over one matrix per layer: $\mathbf{W}_{hh}$ is a single
matrix that the loop uses at every position, tangled up in the whole sequence.

How do you differentiate with respect to a weight that takes part in all $T$ steps at once? The
previous block has both pieces of the answer. <Leccion slug="regla-de-la-cadena">The chain rule</Leccion> says what happens when a weight reaches
the loss along several paths; <Leccion slug="backpropagation">backpropagation</Leccion> gets every derivative of a network in a single pass
from the end to the start, without probing one weight at a time. What's missing is fitting them into
a loop, and the result has a name of its own: backpropagation through time (BPTT). What this lesson
does with it isn't to state it but to derive it in full and check, number by number, that its
gradients are the ones a numerical probe confirms.

Unrolling the loop lays the network out as a row of steps, and along that row you can walk the
backward pass by hand. The loss sits at the end, hanging from the last state; to reach a weight, its
influence has to come back the way it went, from the last state to the one before and from that one
to the one before it, crossing the same $\mathbf{W}_{hh}$ at every hop. There is no other path back,
because there was no other path forward.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
BPTT is the previous block's backward pass with the layers laid out along time. Since the same
$\mathbf{W}_{hh}$ acts at every position, its gradient doesn't come from one step: it's the sum of
what each step asks of it.
</Callout>

## The error that travels back, one step at a time

Fix one sequence, one document, and do the derivation for it alone. A document is one whole example
(a single $\hat y$, a single label), so its loss is $\ell$, the loss of one example, as it has been
throughout the previous block since <Leccion slug="funcion-de-perdida">the lesson on loss functions</Leccion>. The $T$ tokens the loop runs through
aren't $T$ examples: they're positions inside this same document, the axis that plays the part of the
layers here, and none of them is the batch. The batch is the other axis, the usual one ($B$
documents, not one), and its mean, $\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{B}\sum_i \ell_i$, averages these
per-document gradients: a level further up, which touches nothing of what follows. The answer is
read off the last state, as in the previous lesson:

$$
\hat{y} = \sigma\left(\mathbf{W}_{hy}\mathbf{h}_T + \mathbf{b}_y\right),
$$

so the weights of the recurrence reach the loss in one place only: through $\mathbf{h}_T$.
Everything else in the backward pass is rebuilding, step by step, how each state affects that last
one.

Give a name to what the previous lesson left unnamed. The pre-activation of step $t$, the argument of
the $\tanh$, is

$$
\mathbf{p}_t = \mathbf{W}_{hh}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{W}_{xh}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_h \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h},
\qquad
\mathbf{h}_t = \tanh\left(\mathbf{p}_t\right),
$$

and call the gradient of the loss with respect to that pre-activation the **error of step** $t$,
$\boldsymbol{\delta}_t = \nabla_{\mathbf{p}_t}\,\ell \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}$. It's exactly the
$\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}$ of <Leccion slug="backpropagation">the lesson on backpropagation</Leccion> (the error of a layer, at its pre-activation),
except that the index no longer counts layers but steps in time.

That error runs from the end to the start, in two moves per step. The $\tanh$ acts coordinate by
coordinate, so going from the gradient at $\mathbf{h}_t$ to the gradient at $\mathbf{p}_t$ means
multiplying by its slope, and that slope, by the identity $\tanh^{\prime} = 1 - \tanh^{2}$ from <Leccion slug="funciones-activacion">the lesson on activation functions</Leccion> and $\mathbf{h}_t = \tanh(\mathbf{p}_t)$, is $\mathbf{1} - \mathbf{h}_t \odot \mathbf{h}_t$: you read it
straight off the state the forward pass already stored, without keeping $\mathbf{p}_t$. That leaves
the other factor, $\nabla_{\mathbf{h}_t}\ell$, and it has two cases depending on where the loss
reaches $\mathbf{h}_t$ from. At the last step, only from the output layer; at the others, only from
the next step:

$$
\boldsymbol{\delta}_T = \left(\left(\hat{y} - y\right)\mathbf{W}_{hy}^{\top}\right) \odot \left(\mathbf{1} - \mathbf{h}_T \odot \mathbf{h}_T\right),
$$

$$
\boldsymbol{\delta}_t = \left(\mathbf{W}_{hh}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{t+1}\right) \odot \left(\mathbf{1} - \mathbf{h}_t \odot \mathbf{h}_t\right), \qquad t = T-1, \dots, 1.
$$

The starting point, $\boldsymbol{\delta}_T$, is the backward pass through the previous lesson's
output layer: the sigmoid with cross-entropy delivers $\hat{y} - y$, and $\mathbf{W}_{hy}^{\top}$
shares it out among the $d_h$ coordinates of the state. The recurrence is everything else, and it's
the two moves of <Leccion slug="backpropagation">the lesson on backpropagation</Leccion> without any variation: it **transports** the error with $\mathbf{W}_{hh}^{\top}$ and **masks** it with the
slope of the $\tanh$. The only difference is that the transport always uses the same matrix. Keep
hold of that, because it's half of <Leccion slug="gradiente-desvanecido">a lesson further on, the one on the vanishing gradient</Leccion>.

<Details summary="See the transport index by index">
With no matrices in the way, and for an interior step $t < T$, where $\mathbf{h}_t$ reaches the loss
only through the next step. Coordinate $j$ of $\nabla_{\mathbf{h}_t}\ell$ is the derivative of $\ell$
with respect to $h_{t,j}$, and $h_{t,j}$ enters the pre-activation $\mathbf{p}_{t+1}$ through each of
its $d_h$ coordinates: it's the sum over paths from <Leccion slug="regla-de-la-cadena">the lesson on the chain rule</Leccion>, with $d_h$ paths.

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial h_{t,j}} = \sum_{i=1}^{d_h} \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial p_{t+1,i}}\,\frac{\partial p_{t+1,i}}{\partial h_{t,j}} = \sum_{i=1}^{d_h} \delta_{t+1,i}\,(\mathbf{W}_{hh})_{ij},
$$

where the first factor is $\delta_{t+1,i}$ by the definition of the error, and the second comes from
$p_{t+1,i} = \sum_k (\mathbf{W}_{hh})_{ik}\,h_{t,k} + \dots$ depending on $h_{t,j}$ only through the
term $k = j$, with derivative $(\mathbf{W}_{hh})_{ij}$. That sum runs down column $j$ of
$\mathbf{W}_{hh}$, which is row $j$ of its transpose, that is, coordinate $j$ of
$\mathbf{W}_{hh}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{t+1}$. So $\nabla_{\mathbf{h}_t}\ell = \mathbf{W}_{hh}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{t+1}$,
and multiplying by the slope of the $\tanh$ gives $\boldsymbol{\delta}_t$. At the last step the same
argument starts from the output layer instead of the next step, and that's where the other line
comes from.
</Details>

## The gradient of the shared weight is a sum

With one error per step in hand, the gradients of the weights come out without walking anything
else. That's where the sum the previous lesson announced turns up. Look at where $\mathbf{W}_{hh}$
takes part: in the pre-activation of *every* step, $\mathbf{p}_t = \mathbf{W}_{hh}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \dots$,
with $t$ from $1$ to $T$. It's a single weight reached along $T$ paths, one per position, and <Leccion slug="regla-de-la-cadena">the chain rule</Leccion> from the previous block has already settled what to do about that: across paths, you add. Each path contributes
what <Leccion slug="backpropagation">the lesson on backpropagation</Leccion> leaves for a pre-activation (the error times the transposed input), and the total
is the sum of the $T$ contributions:

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{hh}}\ell = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \boldsymbol{\delta}_t\,\mathbf{h}_{t-1}^{\top},
\qquad
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{xh}}\ell = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \boldsymbol{\delta}_t\,\mathbf{x}_t^{\top},
\qquad
\nabla_{\mathbf{b}_h}\ell = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \boldsymbol{\delta}_t.
$$

Read them beside the previous block's and you'll find they're the same. There, the gradient of a
layer was $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}\left(\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)}\right)^{\top}$, a single outer product;
here it's that outer product summed over the steps, because there each matrix appeared once and here
$\mathbf{W}_{hh}$ appears $T$ times. That sum is the only difference between backpropagation and
backpropagation through time, and it's the exact price of sharing: one matrix, $T$ jobs, $T$
contributions to collect.

Two checks close the account. The first is about shapes, the reflex from <Leccion slug="backpropagation">the lesson on backpropagation</Leccion>: each
term of $\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{hh}}\ell$ is $d_h \times d_h$, and so is the sum, so the gradient has the
shape of the matrix it corrects however long the sequence is: $T$ decides how many terms get added,
not the size of the result. The second looks at the first term: in $\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{hh}}\ell$ the
term for $t = 1$ is $\boldsymbol{\delta}_1\mathbf{h}_0^{\top}$, and since $\mathbf{h}_0 = \mathbf{0}$
it's the zero matrix. That squares with the previous lesson, where
$\mathbf{h}_1 = \tanh\left(\mathbf{W}_{xh}\mathbf{x}_1 + \mathbf{b}_h\right)$ carried no
$\mathbf{W}_{hh}$ term: the first step didn't use that matrix, so it owes it no gradient. In
$\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{xh}}\ell$, on the other hand, the $t = 1$ term does count, because $\mathbf{x}_1$
is a real token.

That leaves the output weights, $\mathbf{W}_{hy}$ and $\mathbf{b}_y$, and they bring nothing new.
They appear only once (the network reads its answer once, at the end), so their gradient is an outer
product with no sum, $\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{hy}}\ell = \left(\hat{y} - y\right)\mathbf{h}_T^{\top}$: the
previous block's output layer, as it was. The sum over time is the business of the three shared
weights, and nobody else's.

## BPTT in NumPy, against the numerical probe

The same check as in <Leccion slug="backpropagation">the lesson on backpropagation</Leccion>, applied here: differentiate by hand and set every
number against a probe that nudges the weight a little and measures how the loss changes. A toy RNN
with a fixed seed, a state of $d_h = 3$ and a sequence of $T = 5$. Run it and look at two things:
each step's contribution to $\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{hh}}$ (the last one, for $t = 1$, comes out zero)
and the final line.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
d_model, d_h, T = 4, 3, 5
Wxh = rng.normal(size=(d_h, d_model)) * 0.5
Whh = rng.normal(size=(d_h, d_h)) * 0.5
bh = np.zeros(d_h)
Why = rng.normal(size=(1, d_h)) * 0.5             # scalar output: 1 x d_h
by = np.zeros(1)
X = rng.normal(size=(T, d_model)) * 0.5           # one row per position
y = 1.0                                            # the document's label


def forward():
    hs = [np.zeros(d_h)]                           # h_0 = 0
    for x in X:
        hs.append(np.tanh(Whh @ hs[-1] + Wxh @ x + bh))
    p = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-(Why @ hs[-1] + by)[0]))
    return hs, p, -(y * np.log(p) + (1 - y) * np.log(1 - p))


hs, p, loss = forward()
print("ŷ = %.4f   ℓ = %.4f" % (p, loss))

# Backwards: one δ per position, from the end to the start.
gWhh, gWxh, gbh = np.zeros_like(Whh), np.zeros_like(Wxh), np.zeros_like(bh)
dh = Why[0] * (p - y)                              # ∂ℓ/∂h_T, from the output layer
contributions = []
for t in range(T, 0, -1):
    delta = dh * (1 - hs[t] ** 2)                  # δ_t = (∂ℓ/∂h_t) ⊙ (1 - h_t²)
    gWhh += np.outer(delta, hs[t - 1])             # δ_t h_{t-1}ᵀ : what step t contributes
    gWxh += np.outer(delta, X[t - 1])
    gbh += delta
    contributions.append(np.linalg.norm(np.outer(delta, hs[t - 1])))
    dh = Whh.T @ delta                             # ∂ℓ/∂h_{t-1} : transport with Whhᵀ
gWhy, gby = np.outer([p - y], hs[T]), np.array([p - y])

print("each step's contribution to ∇Whh, from t=%d to t=1:" % T)
print("  ", np.round(contributions, 4), " <- the last one, t=1, is 0 because h_0 = 0")

# The probe: move each weight by hand and see how much the loss changes.
worst = 0.0
for P, G in [(Whh, gWhh), (Wxh, gWxh), (bh, gbh), (Why, gWhy), (by, gby)]:
    for k in np.ndindex(P.shape):
        v = P[k]
        P[k] = v + 1e-6; plus = forward()[2]
        P[k] = v - 1e-6; minus = forward()[2]
        P[k] = v
        worst = max(worst, abs((plus - minus) / 2e-6 - G[k]))
print("largest difference against the numerical probe: %.1e" % worst)
`} />

Read the output against the derivation. The list of contributions has five numbers, one per step,
and the last is exactly zero: step $t = 1$ multiplies by $\mathbf{h}_0 = \mathbf{0}$ and asks nothing
of $\mathbf{W}_{hh}$, which is what the algebra said. And the last line is the lesson: the gradients
of all five groups of weights agree with the probe to $10^{-10}$, which is the probe's error and not
the algorithm's. The sum over time isn't an approximation or a workaround; it's the exact gradient,
and the probe confirms it number by number.

---

The gradient is done, exact and checked, and it carries a warning inside it that we haven't read yet.
The backward recurrence multiplies by $\mathbf{W}_{hh}^{\top}$ at every step (the same matrix, once
per position), and it also masks with the slopes $1 - \mathbf{h}_t \odot \mathbf{h}_t$, the ones <Leccion slug="funciones-activacion">the lesson on activation functions</Leccion> already showed you switching off when the pre-activation $\mathbf{p}_t$
strays from zero. An error that leaves position $T$ and has to reach position $1$ crosses that
product dozens of times, and multiplying that many times by one and the same matrix does one of two
things: it shrinks towards nothing, or it blows up.

Either one breaks precisely what the RNN was brought in to fix. If the gradient that links the first
position to the loss has vanished by the time it arrives, the network can't learn that something at
the start of the document mattered for the end: the long-range dependency, which was the reason for
having memory. How much it shrinks or grows, and why that depends on a single property of
$\mathbf{W}_{hh}$, is <Leccion slug="gradiente-desvanecido">the next lesson, on the vanishing gradient</Leccion>: we derive it carefully and watch it
plummet on a plot as the distance grows.
