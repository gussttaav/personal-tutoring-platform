      import numpy as np


      def softmax(Z):
          E = np.exp(Z - Z.max(axis=1, keepdims=True))
          return E / E.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)


      def cross_entropy(Y_hat, Y):
          P = np.clip(Y_hat, 1e-12, 1.0)
          return float(-np.sum(Y * np.log(P), axis=1).mean())

Measuring isn't answering, and answering is all the network knows how to do. The previous lesson,
on the forward pass in matrix form, closed off one half of the route from $\mathbf{X}$ to
$\hat{\mathbf{Y}}$: a chain of products that crosses every layer without ever once asking whether
what it hands back looks anything like what it should. The other half starts here, by fixing a rule
that compares $\hat{\mathbf{Y}}$ with $\mathbf{Y}$ and sums up, in a single number, how badly the
batch is doing. That rule is called a loss function, and it has to be chosen carefully.

Plenty of rules would technically work, and the choice among them carries real weight. Take
<W>fun and i recommend it</W>, which speaks well of the movie: its label is $y = 1$, and the network
answers with a number between $0$ and $1$ that grows the more it leans towards that class. Suppose
it hands back $0.002$. It's wrong, since it should have landed right up against $1$, and it's wrong
with total confidence, since it couldn't have sat any closer to $0$. This lesson compares two
candidates, mean squared error and cross-entropy, and both agree the loss is high here; that's not
where they part ways. What separates them is how much that loss changes when a weight moves, and on
an example like this one, the first candidate, the one most people would reach for instinctively,
barely reacts at all.

Before writing down a single formula, look at the shape of that difference. What's drawn below isn't
the two losses themselves but the **correction** each one hands back: its derivative with respect to
the pre-activation $z$ of the output neuron, which is exactly where the weights pull from (how much
the loss shifts for every unit that $z$ moves). In green, cross-entropy; in grey, mean squared error
(MSE). With the label at $1$, the network gets it right towards the right of the drawing and gets it
wrong towards the left.

A loss isn't chosen for what it measures. It's chosen for what it hands back once you differentiate

## From predictions to a single number

The network hands over $\hat{\mathbf{Y}} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times d_L}$, and the examples bring
their labels in an array of the same shape, $\mathbf{Y} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times d_L}$, row for row.
A **loss** sums that pair up into one number, and it's built on two levels: a function $\ell$ that
judges *one* example by comparing its prediction with its label, and the mean of the $B$ results
that come out of it,

$$
\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{i=1}^{B} \ell\left(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_i, \mathbf{y}_i\right),
$$

where $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_i, \mathbf{y}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d_L}$ are the prediction and the label of
example $i$: rows $i$ of $\hat{\mathbf{Y}}$ and of $\mathbf{Y}$, written as columns. It's the mean
rather than the sum precisely so $\mathcal{L}$ doesn't grow with $B$. The two symbols share a name
throughout the lesson (MSE, cross-entropy): $\ell$ and $\mathcal{L}$ are the same criterion, one per
example and the other averaged over the whole batch.

Three demands fall on $\ell$, and they don't carry equal weight: it has to return a number, it has
to fall as the prediction approaches the label, and it has to have a derivative with respect to
every parameter, because the method that corrects the weights runs entirely on those derivatives
(<Leccion slug="descenso-gradiente">the next lesson, on gradient descent</Leccion>). Almost anything
satisfies the first two; the third is the one that actually rules candidates out, and it's the same
criterion <Leccion slug="funciones-activacion">the lesson on activation functions and
non-linearity</Leccion> used to choose $\varphi$: there, the step got ruled out for having a null
derivative, not for measuring badly.

The first candidate measures the squared distance. **Mean squared error** is

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}} = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{i=1}^{B} \left\lVert \hat{\mathbf{y}}_i - \mathbf{y}_i \right\rVert^{2},
\qquad
\ell_{\text{MSE}}(\hat{y}, y) = (\hat{y} - y)^{2},
$$

with the subscript naming which candidate it is, and the second form written for $d_L = 1$, the
review network, where $\hat{y}$ and $y$ are scalars: they've dropped the bold of $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_i$
and $\mathbf{y}_i$, since with a single output the vector shrinks down to a single number. The
square does two things at once: it strips away the sign, so overshooting and falling short cost the
same, and it punishes large errors more than proportionally, since missing by $0.8$ costs sixteen
times what missing by $0.2$ does, not four.

That's exactly the right criterion when the network is predicting a quantity: if the job is to guess
how many stars its author gave the movie, the difference $\hat{y} - y$ is stars, and its square
measures precisely what you want. Differentiating gives
$\partial \ell_{\text{MSE}} / \partial \hat{y} = 2(\hat{y} - y)$, the first good property of this
loss: the correction stays proportional to the error. About that factor of $2$, there's a convention
I don't follow (many people put a $\tfrac{1}{2}$ in front so it cancels on differentiating), and it
changes nothing measurable: multiplying every correction by a constant gets absorbed into the size
of the step (how far the weights move on each update), which is what the next lesson adjusts.

### Why it switches off exactly when it's most needed

Classifying isn't predicting a quantity, and there the arithmetic changes. The label is $0$ or $1$,
the output comes out of a sigmoid, $\hat{y} = \sigma(z)$, and the derivative you actually need isn't
the one with respect to $\hat{y}$ but the one with respect to $z$, which is what the weights
control. Chain the two, using $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)\left(1 - \sigma(z)\right)$ from
<Leccion slug="funciones-activacion">the lesson on activation functions</Leccion>:

$$
\frac{\partial \ell_{\text{MSE}}}{\partial z}
= \frac{\partial \ell_{\text{MSE}}}{\partial \hat{y}} \cdot \frac{d\hat{y}}{dz}
= 2\left(\hat{y} - y\right)\hat{y}\left(1 - \hat{y}\right).
$$

Look at the product of the last two factors: it's the derivative of the sigmoid, and
<Leccion slug="funciones-activacion">the lesson on activation functions</Leccion> already told you
where that switches off: when $\hat{y}$ approaches $0$ or $1$, that is, exactly when the network is
sure of itself. The saturation that was a defect of the activation there resurfaces here, multiplying
straight into the correction. With $y = 1$ and $z = -6$ (the network says no, confidently, and is
wrong), the first factor is almost $-2$, but $\hat{y}\left(1 - \hat{y}\right)$ is $0.0025$, and the
product stalls at $0.0049$. The worst error in the whole batch gets the smallest correction in the
whole batch.

This isn't some rare case picked by hand: it's the entire left half of the grey curve above. And no
amount of adjusting the step size fixes a loss that confuses its own two tails, since it scales both
of them the same way.

## Cross-entropy, reading the output as a probability

The fix isn't to patch the formula, but to change what the network is supposed to be saying in the
first place. Up to now $\hat{y}$ was a number between $0$ and $1$; read it instead as the
probability the network assigns to the positive class, $\hat{y} = P(y = 1 \mid \mathbf{x})$. With
that reading, the probability it assigns to whichever label the example actually came with is
written once, covering both cases,

$$
P(y \mid \mathbf{x}) = \hat{y}^{\,y}\left(1 - \hat{y}\right)^{1-y},
$$

because with $y = 1$ the second factor becomes $1$ and what's left is $\hat{y}$, while with $y = 0$
it's the first factor that disappears. Assuming the $B$ examples are independent, the probability of
observing the whole set is the product of all $B$ of them, and that quantity, viewed as a function
of the parameters, is called the **likelihood**. It has to be made as large as possible. Multiplying
$B$ numbers smaller than one drives the product towards zero very fast, so take logarithms instead
(which preserve the order, so the maximum stays put), flip the sign so there's something to
minimise, and divide by $B$. Out comes **cross-entropy**:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}} = -\frac{1}{B} \sum_{i=1}^{B} \left[\, y_i \log \hat{y}_i + \left(1 - y_i\right)\log\left(1 - \hat{y}_i\right) \right].
$$

Read it example by example. One with $y_i = 1$ contributes $-\log \hat{y}_i$: it's $0$ if the
network gives it probability $1$, and it grows without a ceiling as that probability falls towards
zero; one with $y_i = 0$ looks at $1 - \hat{y}_i$ instead. That missing ceiling is the underlying
difference from MSE, which never climbed above $1$.

Now for the derivative, which is what actually decides things. Start from the loss of a single
example,

$$
\ell_{\text{CE}} = -y\log\hat{y} - (1-y)\log(1-\hat{y}),
$$

differentiate it with respect to $\hat{y}$, put the two terms over a common denominator, and chain
with the sigmoid:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial \ell_{\text{CE}}}{\partial \hat{y}}
&= -\frac{y}{\hat{y}} + \frac{1 - y}{1 - \hat{y}}
 = \frac{-y\left(1 - \hat{y}\right) + \hat{y}\left(1 - y\right)}{\hat{y}\left(1 - \hat{y}\right)}
 = \frac{\hat{y} - y}{\hat{y}\left(1 - \hat{y}\right)}, \\[4pt]
\frac{\partial \ell_{\text{CE}}}{\partial z}
&= \frac{\partial \ell_{\text{CE}}}{\partial \hat{y}} \cdot \frac{d\hat{y}}{dz}
 = \frac{\hat{y} - y}{\hat{y}\left(1 - \hat{y}\right)} \cdot \hat{y}\left(1 - \hat{y}\right)
 = \hat{y} - y.
\end{aligned}
$$

On the first line, the numerator simplifies because the two $y\hat{y}$ terms cancel out. On the
second, the denominator turns out to be exactly the $\hat{y}(1 - \hat{y})$ the sigmoid contributes
when you chain, and the two cancel each other away.

The correction turns out to be nothing but the plain difference between what the network said and
what it should have said. On the example from before, that's $-0.9975$ instead of MSE's $0.0049$.
The saturation hasn't gone anywhere, the sigmoid still has it; what happens is that the loss's
derivative carries the exact inverse factor that undoes it, which is why the final formula never
even mentions $\varphi$.

That's why it's called a **loss** and not an error: mean squared error genuinely measures something
in the units of the label, while cross-entropy measures nothing of the kind (it's a logarithm of a
probability), and it's still the better of the two. What decides isn't what they measure, but what
they hand back once you differentiate them.

### More than two classes: softmax

With three answers instead of two (positive, negative and neutral), the output layer has $d_L = 3$
neurons, and the sigmoid stops being any use: it squashes each coordinate on its own, and three
numbers in $(0,1)$ don't automatically form a distribution. The function that does is softmax, the
same one from <Leccion slug="word2vec">the lesson on Word2Vec</Leccion>, which shared probability
out among the entries of the vocabulary:

$$
\hat{y}_k = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{m=1}^{d_L} e^{z_m}}, \qquad k = 1, \dots, d_L,
$$

positive because it's an exponential, and summing to $1$ thanks to the denominator. The label is
written one-hot, $\mathbf{y} \in \{0,1\}^{d_L}$ with a single $1$ at the position of the correct
class $c$, exactly the way <Leccion slug="one-hot">the lesson on one-hot encoding</Leccion> encoded
a vocabulary entry. The loss is the same one as before, written out for $d_L$ classes:

$$
\ell = -\sum_{k=1}^{d_L} y_k \log \hat{y}_k = -\log \hat{y}_c,
$$

where the second equality comes from the one-hot encoding cancelling every term but one. Only the
probability the network gave the true class actually counts, though what it shares out among the
others still matters, since the denominator ties all of them together.

<Details summary="The derivative, with the two kinds of index that have to be told apart">

Differentiate $\ell = -\log \hat{y}_c$ with respect to each $z_j$. The softmax mixes every
coordinate into each one (moving $z_j$ moves the denominator, and with it all $d_L$ probabilities),
so you need two separate computations, depending on whether $j$ is the correct class or not. Call
$S = \sum_{m} e^{z_m}$, with $\partial S / \partial z_j = e^{z_j}$.

When $j = c$, the numerator depends on $z_j$ too, and it's the derivative of a quotient:

$$
\frac{\partial \hat{y}_c}{\partial z_c}
= \frac{e^{z_c} S - e^{z_c} e^{z_c}}{S^{2}}
= \frac{e^{z_c}}{S}\left(1 - \frac{e^{z_c}}{S}\right)
= \hat{y}_c\left(1 - \hat{y}_c\right).
$$

When $j \neq c$, the numerator $e^{z_c}$ doesn't depend on $z_j$ at all, and only the denominator
moves:

$$
\frac{\partial \hat{y}_c}{\partial z_j}
= e^{z_c} \cdot \left(-\frac{1}{S^{2}}\right) e^{z_j}
= -\frac{e^{z_c}}{S}\cdot\frac{e^{z_j}}{S}
= -\hat{y}_c\,\hat{y}_j.
$$

With $\partial \ell / \partial \hat{y}_c = -1/\hat{y}_c$, the chain rule gives both cases:

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z_c} = -\frac{1}{\hat{y}_c}\,\hat{y}_c\left(1 - \hat{y}_c\right) = \hat{y}_c - 1,
\qquad
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z_j} = -\frac{1}{\hat{y}_c}\left(-\hat{y}_c\,\hat{y}_j\right) = \hat{y}_j \quad (j \neq c),
$$

and the two turn out to be the same expression, since $y_c = 1$ and $y_j = 0$. Coordinate by
coordinate:

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\,\ell = \hat{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbf{y}.
$$

With $d_L = 2$ this reproduces the binary case exactly: the short formula from the previous section
was never an accident of having a single output.

</Details>

Both derivations land in the same place, and that's exactly why they're worth doing once: from here
through <Leccion slug="backpropagation">the lesson on backpropagation</Leccion>, the output layer
never gets differentiated again, it goes in as $\hat{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbf{y}$ and that's the
end of it. Softmax matters here because the rest of the course predicts over entire vocabularies,
where the classes number in the tens of thousands.

The first table makes the case for the whole lesson. The count of correct answers ties the first two
networks at nine out of ten: it can't tell apart saying $0.881$ from saying $0.538$, because both
land on the same side. The two losses separate them cleanly ($0.049$ against $0.220$ for the
confident network, $0.220$ against $0.633$ for the lukewarm one), and both put the one that answers
$0.5$ to everything in last place, at $0.250$ and $0.693$, which is $\log 2$. They rank the networks
the same way. What they don't do the same way is correct them.

That's the second table. At $z = -6$, with the label at $1$, cross-entropy hands back $0.9975$ and
MSE hands back $0.0049$: a ratio of $202.7$. At $z = 0$, with the network holding no opinion either
way, the ratio drops to $2$. The advantage grows towards the extremes, and one of those extremes is
exactly where the network is wrong. The last row repeats that same $202.7$ with the network getting
it right, and there both corrections are tiny, which is exactly as it should be.

The third experiment shows the detail that breaks a real implementation. $-\log 0$ is infinite, and
one example the network assigns probability zero to is enough to send the mean of the entire
batch to infinity and stop it from saying anything useful at all. Clipping at $10^{-12}$ avoids that,
at a cost worth naming out loud: nobody ever actually measured that $27.63$.

There's now a number that says how badly the network is doing, and across this entire lesson, not a
single weight has moved. All three networks arrived with their numbers already fixed, the loss has
ranked them, which is all it was ever asked to do, and none of them has improved one bit for having
been measured.

Training means making that number small, and the only thing you can actually touch to get there is
the weights: changing them changes $\hat{\mathbf{Y}}$, and through it, $\mathcal{L}$. Of that
movement, the derivative gives you the direction (if $\hat{y} - y$ is positive, raising $z$ raises
the loss, so it needs to come down instead), but it doesn't give you the distance: it describes the
slope right where you're standing and stops holding the moment you move. So the weights don't get
corrected in one single leap, but in a series of steps, and the size of each step is itself a
decision: too short and you never arrive, too long and you overshoot. How that size gets chosen,
what goes wrong when it's chosen badly, and why repeating the process ends up finding the minimum
without ever actually seeing it, is <Leccion slug="descenso-gradiente">the next lesson, on gradient
descent</Leccion>.
