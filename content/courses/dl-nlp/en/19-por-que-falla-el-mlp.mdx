An input layer is exactly as wide as it was built to be; a text is however long it happens to be.
The previous block, on the multilayer perceptron (MLP), kept bumping into that tension without ever
quite naming it. Its review classifier got about $86.7\%$ of unseen reviews right, and what held it
back wasn't the network but the input: the order of the tokens never made it through the door.
Fixing that means changing the input, and changing the input means going back to $\mathbf{W}^{(1)}$,
which has had $d_0$ columns from the moment it was built.

The fix anyone would think of first is to line the tokens up in a row. Each one brings its own
vector (the embedding from <Leccion slug="embeddings-densos">the lesson on dense
representations</Leccion>), and gluing those vectors end to end writes the order into where each
one sits: position becomes a coordinate, and there's no sum to wipe it out. The fix lasts until the
second text. <W>the cinematography is superb</W> brings four vectors and <W>weak script</W> brings
two, so they produce inputs of two different widths, and a layer that multiplies by
$\mathbf{W}^{(1)}$ accepts exactly one.

There's a second consequence too, and it only shows up once you draw it.

The rest of this lesson is that figure, first written out in symbols and then measured with NumPy.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
Concatenation builds order into the input, but at a price: each position gets its own slice of the
first layer. Whatever the network learns about a vocabulary entry at position 3 is unavailable at
position 5, because those are different weights, adjusted by different examples.
</Callout>

## A sequence that doesn't fit a fixed-size input

The tokeniser turns a document into a sequence of tokens $w_1, \dots, w_T$. That length $T$ belongs
to this particular document; the next one will have a different one. Each position contributes the
vector of its vocabulary entry, $\mathbf{x}_t = \mathbf{e}_{w_t} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}$,
its own row of $\mathbf{E}$. Stacking them all into a single column means writing

$$
\mathbf{x} = \left[\mathbf{x}_1 ; \mathbf{x}_2 ; \dots ; \mathbf{x}_T\right] \in \mathbb{R}^{T \cdot d_{\text{model}}},
$$

where the semicolons stack downwards: the result is one vector of $T \cdot d_{\text{model}}$
numbers, not a matrix with $T$ rows. The order is in there, intact, and reading it off is a matter
of looking at the right stretch. The letter now appears in two forms on purpose, so it's worth
pinning them down: $\mathbf{x}$ with no subscript is the input for the whole example, as it was
throughout the previous block, and $\mathbf{x}_t$ is the piece that position $t$ contributes.

On the other side sits a layer that won't negotiate. $\mathbf{W}^{(1)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_1 \times d_0}$
has $d_0$ columns, a number fixed when the network is built, which knows nothing about the document
that's about to come in. With $\mathbf{x}$ of length $T \cdot d_{\text{model}}$ and $T$ changing
from one text to the next, the product $\mathbf{W}^{(1)}\mathbf{x}$ is defined for documents of one
length and undefined for every other. This isn't a matter of rounding or of training: the
multiplication doesn't exist.

The only way out that leaves the architecture alone is to fix a number of positions, $T_{\max}$, and
force every document to that length:

$$
\mathbf{x}_t = \begin{cases}
\mathbf{e}_{w_t} & \text{if } t \leq T,\\
\mathbf{0} & \text{if } T < t \leq T_{\max},
\end{cases}
$$

keeping only the first $T_{\max}$ positions when $T > T_{\max}$. Filling the leftover positions with
the zero vector is called **padding**; cutting at $T_{\max}$ is **truncation**. With both in place,
$d_0 = T_{\max} \cdot d_{\text{model}}$ for every document, and the network can be built.

Each half of that arrangement has its own cost, and both deserve spelling out. Truncation throws
away the whole tail of any long document, and it does so *by position*. That's nothing like the
out-of-vocabulary (OOV) problem from <Leccion slug="vocabulario-oov">the lesson on the
vocabulary</Leccion>, where the casualties were the rare types, which is why so little was lost.
Here the most frequent entry in the corpus can be the one that goes, if it happens to sit at
position $T_{\max} + 1$. Padding pays at the other end: with $T_{\max}$ set to reach the longest
document, a document of average length enters the network with more than half its vector at zero.

## Each position learns on its own

Now slice $\mathbf{W}^{(1)}$ along the same seams as the input. Its
$d_0 = T_{\max} \cdot d_{\text{model}}$ columns fall into $T_{\max}$ consecutive blocks of
$d_{\text{model}}$ columns each,
$\mathbf{W}^{(1)} = \left[\mathbf{W}^{(1)}_1 \mid \dots \mid \mathbf{W}^{(1)}_{T_{\max}}\right]$ with
$\mathbf{W}^{(1)}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_1 \times d_{\text{model}}}$ (here the subscript is a position in
the sequence, not a coordinate), and the product splits the same way:

$$
\mathbf{z}^{(1)} = \mathbf{W}^{(1)}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}^{(1)} = \sum_{t=1}^{T_{\max}} \mathbf{W}^{(1)}_t\,\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}^{(1)}.
$$

Nothing has actually changed yet: it's the same product as always, grouped into stretches instead
of single columns. The interesting part shows up when you differentiate.

<Leccion slug="backpropagation">The lesson on backpropagation</Leccion> boiled a layer's gradient
down to one line, $\nabla_{\mathbf{W}^{(l)}}\ell = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}\left(\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)}\right)^{\top}$,
and for the first layer $\mathbf{h}^{(0)} = \mathbf{x}$, so

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}^{(1)}}\ell = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)}\,\mathbf{x}^{\top} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_1 \times d_0}.
$$

That outer product splits by columns at exactly the places where $\mathbf{W}^{(1)}$ was split, and
the piece that goes to each block is

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}^{(1)}_t}\ell = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)}\,\mathbf{x}_t^{\top} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_1 \times d_{\text{model}}}.
$$

<Details summary="The split by columns, step by step">
The outer product is defined coordinate by coordinate: entry $(i, j)$ of
$\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)}\mathbf{x}^{\top}$ is $\delta^{(1)}_i \cdot x_j$, with $i$ running over the
$d_1$ neurons and $j$ over the $d_0$ columns.

Column $j$ belongs to block $t$ when $(t-1) \cdot d_{\text{model}} < j \leq t \cdot d_{\text{model}}$,
and within that block it's column $j - (t-1) \cdot d_{\text{model}}$. Since concatenation defines
$x_j$ as coordinate $j - (t-1) \cdot d_{\text{model}}$ of $\mathbf{x}_t$, the submatrix made of
those $d_{\text{model}}$ columns holds $\delta^{(1)}_i \cdot x_{t,k}$ at entry $(i, k)$, which,
entry for entry, is $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)}\mathbf{x}_t^{\top}$.

The split works because the two things being split, the columns of $\mathbf{W}^{(1)}$ and the
coordinates of $\mathbf{x}$, are split at the same places. And nothing else enters the calculation:
every block receives the whole of $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)}$, so the only thing that distinguishes
one block's gradient from another's is which vector of the sequence it gets multiplied by.
</Details>

Read that slowly, because it's the key result of the lesson. The gradient of block $t$ depends on
$\mathbf{x}_t$ and on no other part of the input. If that position was padded, then
$\mathbf{x}_t = \mathbf{0}$ and the block receives the zero matrix: that example teaches it nothing
at all, not a little and not a lot. And if the input is one-hot, $\mathbf{x}_t = \mathbf{o}_{w_t}$
has a single one, so $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)}\mathbf{o}_{w_t}^{\top}$ is zero everywhere except in
one column: inside block $t$, the only column that moves is the one for the vocabulary entry that
sat in that position.

Put those two facts together and you get the claim this section is named after. The column that
represents an entry inside block $t$ is adjusted *only* by the examples in which that entry appeared
at position $t$. If an entry never reached position 5 in the training set, the matching columns of
block 5 stay exactly as initialisation left them, however many steps gradient descent takes; and
whatever the network has worked out about that same entry at position 3 is stored in other weights,
which position 5 can't reach.

### What it costs in weights

$\mathbf{W}^{(1)}$ has shape $d_1 \times d_0$, so it holds $d_1 \cdot d_0$ weights, and with a
concatenated input that comes to

$$
d_1 \cdot T_{\max} \cdot d_{\text{model}} \quad \text{weights}.
$$

Plug in some ordinary sizes (embeddings of $d_{\text{model}} = 100$, a hidden layer of
$d_1 = 128$ neurons and $T_{\max} = 30$ positions) and the first layer alone has $384\,000$ weights.
For comparison, the previous lesson's review classifier, a single hidden layer over a bag of words,
had $5\,257$ parameters in total. Reading paragraphs instead of sentences, with $T_{\max} = 300$,
pushes the figure up to $3\,840\,000$.

The growth itself isn't the problem; it's a symptom. The real issue underneath is that the
$3\,456\,000$ weights added in going from $30$ positions to $300$ are *new* weights: they start from
their initialisation and inherit nothing of what the first thirty blocks learned. Meanwhile the
corpus still has the same examples it always had, so more positions means spreading those examples
across more blocks: less experience per parameter, at exactly the moment there are more parameters
to train.

## Measuring it with NumPy

A small experiment puts numbers on all this. I made up the sequences for the course: six positions,
eight vocabulary entries, and the label is $1$ whenever <W>not</W> appears among them. The catch is
where it appears: in training, only in the first four positions; in the test, only in the last two.
That's the only difference between the two sets.

The cell trains the same network twice on the same data: once on the concatenation of the six
positions, and once on their bag of words, the same one-hot vectors summed into eight coordinates.
The training loop is the one from <Leccion slug="implementar-mlp">the lesson on implementing an
MLP</Leccion>, without a single line changed. Read the output in three parts: what concatenation
does, what the bag of words does with the same data, and what has happened to each block of
$\mathbf{W}^{(1)}$.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

V = ["the", "film", "is", "very", "good", "long", "fun", "not"]
NOT, T = 7, 6                                 # "not" is the last entry; six positions
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)


def corpus(n, positions):
    S = rng.integers(0, NOT, size=(n, T))     # filler, drawn from the first seven
    y = np.zeros((n, 1))
    y[::2] = 1.0                              # exactly half carry "not"...
    for i in np.nonzero(y[:, 0])[0]:
        S[i, rng.choice(positions)] = NOT     # ...and only where they're told
    U = np.eye(len(V))[S]                     # (n, 6, 8): one one-hot per position
    return U.reshape(n, T * len(V)), U.sum(1), y   # concatenate them, or sum them


C_train, B_train, Y_train = corpus(600, [0, 1, 2, 3])   # "not" in positions 1-4
C_test, B_test, Y_test = corpus(300, [4, 5])            # in the test, only in 5-6


def train(X_train, X_test, steps=1500, eta=0.5, d1=16):
    r = np.random.default_rng(1)
    W1, b1 = r.normal(size=(d1, X_train.shape[1])) * 0.1, np.zeros(d1)
    W2, b2 = r.normal(size=(1, d1)) * 0.1, np.zeros(1)
    W1_init = W1.copy()

    def output(X):
        H = np.tanh(X @ W1.T + b1)
        return H, 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-(H @ W2.T + b2)))

    def accuracy(X, Y):
        return float(((output(X)[1] >= 0.5) == (Y == 1.0)).mean())

    for _ in range(steps):                    # the previous block's loop, untouched
        H, P = output(X_train)
        D2 = P - Y_train
        D1 = (D2 @ W2) * (1.0 - H ** 2)
        W1 -= eta * (D1.T @ X_train) / len(X_train)
        b1 -= eta * D1.sum(0) / len(X_train)
        W2 -= eta * (D2.T @ H) / len(X_train)
        b2 -= eta * D2.sum(0) / len(X_train)
    return (accuracy(X_train, Y_train), accuracy(X_test, Y_test),
            W1 - W1_init, output(X_test)[1])


acc_train, acc_test, moved, P_test = train(C_train, C_test)
print("concatenation   training %.3f   test %.3f" % (acc_train, acc_test))
print("bag of words    training %.3f   test %.3f" % train(B_train, B_test)[:2])
print('mean ŷ of the 150 test examples carrying "not": %.4f' % P_test[Y_test == 1.0].mean())

print('\\nhow far the "not" column has moved in each block:')
for t in range(T):
    print("   position %d:  %.6f" % (t + 1, np.linalg.norm(moved[:, t * len(V) + NOT])))

The first line is the failure: $1.000$ on what the network has seen, $0.500$ on what it hasn't. And
that $0.500$ isn't a coin toss. It gets all hundred and fifty test sequences that *don't* contain
<W>not</W> right, and all hundred and fifty that do contain it wrong, because as far as it can tell
they're identical to the first lot. The third line shows how far this goes: the mean $\hat{y}$ over
the hundred and fifty positives is $0.0014$. The network isn't wavering between the two answers;
it's certain, and wrong.

The second line is the awkward one. The bag of words (the very representation this block set out to
replace, the one that can't tell one order from another) scores $1.000$ on the shifted test. Its
advantage is precisely what concatenation lacks: it has *one* coordinate for <W>not</W>, which
collects the occurrences from all six positions at once, so a weight adjusted on what happened at
position 2 works as it stands when <W>not</W> turns up at position 5. Concatenation gained order
and lost exactly that.

The last part of the output is the formula, verified. The <W>not</W> column has moved about $2.4$
units in blocks 1 to 4, and $0.000000$ in blocks 5 and 6 (genuinely zero, not something rounded
down to it), because its share of the gradient $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)}\mathbf{x}_t^{\top}$ was
zero on all six hundred examples at every one of the fifteen hundred steps. Change the
initialisation and the figure for the first four blocks changes; the last two still read zero, and
test accuracy still reads $0.500$.

The two things this lesson has measured are really one. The fixed length forces truncation and
padding because the first layer has exactly one block per position and not one more; and what's
learned at one position never reaches the others because each block is adjusted only by its own.
Both come from putting the whole sequence in front of the network at once, as if it were a single
point in $\mathbb{R}^{d_0}$ with a great many coordinates, and neither can be fixed from within that
idea: not with more neurons, not with more steps, and certainly not with a larger $T_{\max}$, which
makes both worse.

What's needed is a network in which position 5 is multiplied by *the same* weights as position 3,
so that adjusting them on one occurrence adjusts them for every position. That forces two things at
once: reading the sequence one position at a time rather than all in one go, and keeping what has
already been read somewhere else (because if the matrix is the same at every position, the order
can't be stored in it). That's <Leccion slug="la-rnn-vanilla">the next lesson, on the simplest
recurrent neural network (RNN) there is</Leccion>: a single matrix applied $T$ times, a state passed
from one step to the next, and an input that no longer has to be the same length for every
document.
