An input layer is as wide as it was built to be; a text is as long as it happens to be. The previous
block, on the multilayer perceptron (MLP), ended up running into that tension without ever quite
putting it into words. Its review classifier was right about $86.7\%$ of the reviews it hadn't read,
and what held it back was the input rather than the network, because the order of the tokens never
made it through the door. Fixing that means changing the input, and changing the input means going back
to $\mathbf{W}^{(1)}$, which has had $d_0$ columns since the instant it was built.

The fix that occurs to anyone is to put the tokens in a row. Each one brings its vector (the
embedding from <Leccion slug="embeddings-densos">the lesson on dense representations</Leccion>), and
gluing those vectors one after another leaves the order written into the place each one occupies:
position becomes a coordinate, and no sum wipes it out. The fix lasts until the second text.
<W>the cinematography is superb</W> brings four vectors and <W>weak script</W> brings two, so they
produce inputs of two different widths, and a layer that multiplies by $\mathbf{W}^{(1)}$ accepts
exactly one.

There's a second consequence, and that one doesn't show until you draw it.

The rest of the lesson is that figure written out in symbols, and then measured with NumPy.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
Concatenating puts order inside the input and charges for it: each position takes its own slice of
the first layer. What the network learns about a vocabulary entry in position 3 isn't available in
position 5, because those are different weights, adjusted with different examples.
</Callout>

## A sequence that doesn't fit a fixed-size input

The tokeniser turns a document into a sequence of tokens $w_1, \dots, w_T$. That length $T$ belongs
to that document: the next one will bring another. Each position contributes the vector of its
vocabulary entry, $\mathbf{x}_t = \mathbf{e}_{w_t} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}$, the row of
$\mathbf{E}$ that belongs to it. Stacking them into a single column means writing

$$
\mathbf{x} = \left[\mathbf{x}_1 ; \mathbf{x}_2 ; \dots ; \mathbf{x}_T\right] \in \mathbb{R}^{T \cdot d_{\text{model}}},
$$

where the semicolons stack downwards: what comes out is a vector of $T \cdot d_{\text{model}}$
numbers, not a matrix with $T$ rows. The order is inside, intact, and reading it is a matter of
looking at the right stretch. The two ways of writing the letter live side by side on purpose, and
they're worth pinning down here: $\mathbf{x}$ without a subscript is the input for the whole
example, as throughout the previous block, and $\mathbf{x}_t$ is the piece that position $t$
contributes.

Facing it is a layer that won't negotiate. $\mathbf{W}^{(1)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_1 \times d_0}$ has
$d_0$ columns; that number is chosen when the network is built, and it knows nothing about the
document that's going to come in. With $\mathbf{x}$ measuring $T \cdot d_{\text{model}}$ and $T$
changing from one text to the next, the product $\mathbf{W}^{(1)}\mathbf{x}$ is defined for
documents of one length and undefined for all the others. It isn't a problem of rounding or of
training: the multiplication doesn't exist.

The only way out that doesn't change the architecture is to fix a number of positions $T_{\max}$
and make every document measure that:

$$
\mathbf{x}_t = \begin{cases}
\mathbf{e}_{w_t} & \text{if } t \leq T,\\
\mathbf{0} & \text{if } T < t \leq T_{\max},
\end{cases}
$$

keeping the first $T_{\max}$ positions when $T > T_{\max}$. Filling what's left over with the zero
vector is called **padding**; cutting at $T_{\max}$ is **truncation**. With the two together,
$d_0 = T_{\max} \cdot d_{\text{model}}$ for every document, and the network can be built.

The two halves of that arrangement cost different things, and both need saying out loud. Truncation
loses the whole end of any long document, and loses it *by position*. It's nothing like the
out-of-vocabulary (OOV) problem from <Leccion slug="vocabulario-oov">the lesson on the
vocabulary</Leccion>, where what fell away were the rare types, which is why little was lost. Here
the most frequent entry in the corpus can be the one to go, if it happens to sit at position
$T_{\max} + 1$. And padding pays at the other end: with $T_{\max}$ set wherever the longest document
reaches, a document of average length enters the network with more than half of its vector at zero.

## Each position learns on its own

Cut $\mathbf{W}^{(1)}$ where the input is already cut. Its $d_0 = T_{\max} \cdot d_{\text{model}}$
columns fall into $T_{\max}$ consecutive blocks of $d_{\text{model}}$ columns each,
$\mathbf{W}^{(1)} = \left[\mathbf{W}^{(1)}_1 \mid \dots \mid \mathbf{W}^{(1)}_{T_{\max}}\right]$ with
$\mathbf{W}^{(1)}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_1 \times d_{\text{model}}}$ (that subscript is a position in
the sequence, not a coordinate), and the product splits along with it:

$$
\mathbf{z}^{(1)} = \mathbf{W}^{(1)}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}^{(1)} = \sum_{t=1}^{T_{\max}} \mathbf{W}^{(1)}_t\,\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}^{(1)}.
$$

Nothing has happened there yet: it's the same product as ever, grouped by stretches instead of by
single columns. What does happen shows up when you differentiate.

<Leccion slug="backpropagation">The lesson on backpropagation</Leccion> leaves the gradient of a
layer in one line, $\nabla_{\mathbf{W}^{(l)}}\ell = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}\left(\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)}\right)^{\top}$,
and for the first layer $\mathbf{h}^{(0)} = \mathbf{x}$, so

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}^{(1)}}\ell = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)}\,\mathbf{x}^{\top} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_1 \times d_0}.
$$

That outer product splits by columns exactly where $\mathbf{W}^{(1)}$ was split, and the piece that
falls to each block is

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}^{(1)}_t}\ell = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)}\,\mathbf{x}_t^{\top} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_1 \times d_{\text{model}}}.
$$

<Details summary="The split by columns, step by step">
The outer product is defined coordinate by coordinate: entry $(i, j)$ of
$\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)}\mathbf{x}^{\top}$ is $\delta^{(1)}_i \cdot x_j$, with $i$ running over the
$d_1$ neurons and $j$ over the $d_0$ columns.

Column $j$ belongs to block $t$ when $(t-1) \cdot d_{\text{model}} < j \leq t \cdot d_{\text{model}}$,
and within that block it is column $j - (t-1) \cdot d_{\text{model}}$. Since concatenation defines
$x_j$ as coordinate $j - (t-1) \cdot d_{\text{model}}$ of $\mathbf{x}_t$, the submatrix formed by
those $d_{\text{model}}$ columns holds the number $\delta^{(1)}_i \cdot x_{t,k}$ at entry $(i, k)$,
which is, entry by entry, $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)}\mathbf{x}_t^{\top}$.

The split works because the two things being split are split at the same places: the columns of
$\mathbf{W}^{(1)}$ and the coordinates of $\mathbf{x}$. And nothing else enters the calculation:
$\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)}$ goes whole to every block, so the only thing that tells one block's
gradient from another's is which vector of the sequence multiplies it.
</Details>

Read that slowly, because it's the result of the lesson. The gradient of block $t$ depends on
$\mathbf{x}_t$ and on no other part of the input. If that position was padded, $\mathbf{x}_t =
\mathbf{0}$, the block receives the zero matrix: that example teaches it nothing, neither a little
nor a lot. And if the input arrives one-hot, $\mathbf{x}_t = \mathbf{o}_{w_t}$ has a single one, so
$\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)}\mathbf{o}_{w_t}^{\top}$ is zero except in one column: inside block $t$,
the only column that moves is the one belonging to the vocabulary entry that occupied that position.

Put the two sentences together and out comes the claim this section is named after. The column that
represents an entry inside block $t$ is adjusted *only* with the examples in which that entry
appeared at position $t$. An entry that never reached position 5 in the training set leaves the
corresponding columns of block 5 exactly as they came out of initialisation, however many steps the
descent takes; and whatever the network has worked out about that same entry at position 3 is
stored in other weights, which position 5 has no access to.

### What it costs in weights

$\mathbf{W}^{(1)}$ has shape $d_1 \times d_0$, so it holds $d_1 \cdot d_0$ weights, and with the
input concatenated that comes to

$$
d_1 \cdot T_{\max} \cdot d_{\text{model}} \quad \text{weights}.
$$

Put in numbers of an ordinary size: embeddings of $d_{\text{model}} = 100$, a hidden layer of
$d_1 = 128$ neurons and $T_{\max} = 30$ positions give $384\,000$ weights in the first layer. The
previous lesson's review classifier, one hidden layer over a bag of words, had $5\,257$ parameters
in total. And reading paragraphs instead of sentences, $T_{\max} = 300$, takes the figure up to
$3\,840\,000$.

That growth isn't the problem; it's the symptom. What lies underneath is that the $3\,456\,000$
weights added in going from $30$ positions to $300$ are *new* weights, which start at their
initialisation and receive nothing of what was learned in the first thirty blocks. The corpus,
meanwhile, still has the examples it had: more positions means the same examples spread across more
blocks, which is to say less experience per parameter right when there are more parameters.

## Measuring it with NumPy

A small experiment puts it in numbers. I made the sequences up for the course: six positions, eight
vocabulary entries, and the label is $1$ when <W>not</W> appears among them. The difference lies in
where it appears (in training, only in the first four positions; in the test, only in the last two),
and that is the only difference between the two sets.

The cell trains the same network twice on the same data: once on the concatenation of the six
positions and once on their bag of words, which is the same eight coordinates summed. The loop is
the one from <Leccion slug="implementar-mlp">the lesson on implementing an MLP</Leccion>, without a
line changed. Read its output in three stretches: what concatenation does, what the bag of words
does with that same data, and what has happened to each block of $\mathbf{W}^{(1)}$.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

V = ["the", "film", "is", "very", "good", "long", "fun", "not"]
NOT, T = 7, 6                                 # "not" is the last entry; six positions
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)


def corpus(n, positions):
    S = rng.integers(0, NOT, size=(n, T))     # filler, drawn from the first seven
    y = np.zeros((n, 1))
    y[::2] = 1.0                              # exactly half carry "not"...
    for i in np.nonzero(y[:, 0])[0]:
        S[i, rng.choice(positions)] = NOT     # ...and only where they're told
    U = np.eye(len(V))[S]                     # (n, 6, 8): one one-hot per position
    return U.reshape(n, T * len(V)), U.sum(1), y   # concatenate them, or sum them


C_train, B_train, Y_train = corpus(600, [0, 1, 2, 3])   # "not" in positions 1-4
C_test, B_test, Y_test = corpus(300, [4, 5])            # in the test, only in 5-6


def train(X_train, X_test, steps=1500, eta=0.5, d1=16):
    r = np.random.default_rng(1)
    W1, b1 = r.normal(size=(d1, X_train.shape[1])) * 0.1, np.zeros(d1)
    W2, b2 = r.normal(size=(1, d1)) * 0.1, np.zeros(1)
    W1_init = W1.copy()

    def output(X):
        H = np.tanh(X @ W1.T + b1)
        return H, 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-(H @ W2.T + b2)))

    def accuracy(X, Y):
        return float(((output(X)[1] >= 0.5) == (Y == 1.0)).mean())

    for _ in range(steps):                    # the previous block's loop, untouched
        H, P = output(X_train)
        D2 = P - Y_train
        D1 = (D2 @ W2) * (1.0 - H ** 2)
        W1 -= eta * (D1.T @ X_train) / len(X_train)
        b1 -= eta * D1.sum(0) / len(X_train)
        W2 -= eta * (D2.T @ H) / len(X_train)
        b2 -= eta * D2.sum(0) / len(X_train)
    return (accuracy(X_train, Y_train), accuracy(X_test, Y_test),
            W1 - W1_init, output(X_test)[1])


acc_train, acc_test, moved, P_test = train(C_train, C_test)
print("concatenation   training %.3f   test %.3f" % (acc_train, acc_test))
print("bag of words    training %.3f   test %.3f" % train(B_train, B_test)[:2])
print('mean ŷ of the 150 test examples carrying "not": %.4f' % P_test[Y_test == 1.0].mean())

print('\\nhow far the "not" column has moved in each block:')
for t in range(T):
    print("   position %d:  %.6f" % (t + 1, np.linalg.norm(moved[:, t * len(V) + NOT])))

The first line is the failure: $1.000$ on what it has seen and $0.500$ on what it hasn't. That
$0.500$ isn't a coin toss. It gets all hundred and fifty of the test sequences that *don't* carry
<W>not</W> right, and all hundred and fifty of the ones that do carry it wrong, because to its eyes
they're identical to the first lot. The third line says how far that goes: the mean $\hat{y}$ of the
hundred and fifty positives is $0.0014$. It isn't wavering between the two answers; it's certain,
and wrong.

The second line is the uncomfortable one. The bag of words (the representation this block came to
replace, the one that can't tell one order from another) scores $1.000$ on the shifted test. Its
advantage is exactly what the other one lacks: it has *one* coordinate for <W>not</W>, which
collects the occurrences from all six positions at once, so a weight adjusted with what happened at
position 2 works as it stands when <W>not</W> turns up at position 5. Concatenation gained order and
lost that.

The last output is the formula, verified. The <W>not</W> column has moved about $2.4$ units in
blocks 1 to 4, and $0.000000$ in blocks 5 and 6 (exactly zero, not a zero that was rounded), because
its gradient $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)}\mathbf{x}_t^{\top}$ was the zero matrix on all six hundred
examples and at all fifteen hundred steps. Change the initialisation and the figure for the first
four blocks changes; the last two still give zero, and test accuracy still gives $0.500$.

The two things this lesson has measured are the same thing. The fixed length forces truncation and
padding because the first layer has one block per position and not one more; and what's learned at
one position doesn't reach the others because each block is adjusted with its own. Both come from
having put the whole sequence in front of the network at once, as though it were a point in
$\mathbb{R}^{d_0}$ with a great many coordinates, and neither can be fixed from inside that idea:
not with more neurons, not with more steps, and not with a larger $T_{\max}$, which makes both
worse.

What's needed is a network in which position 5 multiplies by *the same* weights as position 3, so
that adjusting them with one occurrence leaves them adjusted for all of them. That forces two things
at once: reading the sequence one position at a time instead of all in one go, and keeping what has
already been read somewhere (because if the matrix is the same at every position, the order can't be
stored in it). That's <Leccion slug="la-rnn-vanilla">the next lesson, on the simplest recurrent
neural network (RNN) there is</Leccion>: a single matrix applied $T$ times, a state that passes from
one step to the next, and an input that no longer has to measure the same in every document.
