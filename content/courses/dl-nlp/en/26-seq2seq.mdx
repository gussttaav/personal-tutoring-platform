The previous lesson's character-level model can carry on a text: give it an opening and it keeps
going, character by character, for as long as you let it. But carrying on is all it does. Plenty of
tasks have a different shape: shortening a paragraph into a headline, correcting a line full of
typos, turning a date written out in words into digits. In each of them one sequence goes in and
another comes out, and the second isn't a continuation of the first, nor does it need to be the same
length. That shape is what this lesson builds.

It's called a **sequence-to-sequence model** (*seq2seq* for short), and the idea fits in a sentence:
two networks instead of one. The first, the encoder, reads the input sequence from start to finish
and compresses it into a vector; the second, the decoder, starts from that vector and writes the
output sequence one token at a time, the way the previous lesson's model wrote. Nothing passes
between them except that vector: everything the encoder took from the input has to fit in there.

Take the simplest task with this shape: reversal. The input is <W>live</W> and the output is
<W>evil</W>, the same string read backwards. To write the first letter of the output (the <W>e</W>),
the decoder needs to know which letter the input ended on; to write the last (the <W>l</W>), it needs
to remember the first. So to reverse a string, you have to have read all of it before you start
writing, and that's exactly what the split into two networks reflects. The encoder reads
<W>live</W> to the end and leaves its summary; only then does the decoder start producing
<W>evil</W>.

That summary is the encoder's last state, and it has a name: the **context vector**, $\mathbf{c}$.
It's a vector of fixed size (exactly as many coordinates as the state, not one more), and it's the
only part of the input the decoder ever gets to see. The decoder never looks at <W>live</W> again: it
starts from $\mathbf{c}$ and writes from there, feeding itself what it produces, the way the previous
lesson's model generated text.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
The encoder reads the input sequence and summarises it in a fixed vector, $\mathbf{c}$. The decoder
starts from $\mathbf{c}$ and writes the output sequence token by token, feeding back what it
produces. The two networks train together, even though only the decoder has a loss.
</Callout>

## The encoder summarises, the decoder writes

Both networks are <Leccion slug="la-rnn-vanilla">the vanilla recurrent neural network (RNN)</Leccion>, with nothing new in the
recurrence; the only novelty is how they're chained together. The **encoder** reads the input
tokens $\mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_{T_x}$ (each $\mathbf{x}_j$ being the one-hot vector of the
$j$-th symbol, as in the previous lesson) and updates its state:

$$
\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j = \tanh\left(\mathbf{W}^{\text{enc}}_{xh}\mathbf{x}_j + \mathbf{W}^{\text{enc}}_{hh}\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{j-1} + \mathbf{b}^{\text{enc}}_h\right),
$$

with $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_0 = \mathbf{0}$. The bar over $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j$ marks it as the encoder's
state, so it can't be confused with the decoder's; the superscript $\text{enc}$ does the same for its
weights, and it isn't a layer index (the encoder has no layers to number) but a label saying which
network the weights belong to. When the encoder finishes reading, its last state is the context
vector:

$$
\mathbf{c} = \bar{\mathbf{h}}_{T_x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}.
$$

Notice the size of $\mathbf{c}$: $d_h$ coordinates, the same as the state, and that number doesn't
depend on $T_x$. A three-token input and a thirty-token input produce a $\mathbf{c}$ of the same
size.

The **decoder** is another RNN, with its own weights, that picks up where the encoder left off: its
initial state is $\mathbf{s}_0 = \mathbf{c}$. From there it produces the output step by step. At step
$i$ it reads a token (at the first step <W>\<GO></W>, the symbol the decoder starts from, and after
that the previous token) and updates its state:

$$
\mathbf{s}_i = \tanh\left(\mathbf{W}^{\text{dec}}_{xh}\mathbf{o}_{u_{i-1}} + \mathbf{W}^{\text{dec}}_{hh}\mathbf{s}_{i-1} + \mathbf{b}^{\text{dec}}_h\right),
$$

where $\mathbf{o}_{u_{i-1}}$ is the one-hot vector of the token $u_{i-1}$ fed in at position $i$.
Each state produces a distribution over the next symbol, as in the previous lesson's language model:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{y}}_i = \text{softmax}\left(\mathbf{W}_{hy}\mathbf{s}_i + \mathbf{b}_y\right),
$$

and the loss adds one cross-entropy per output position, averaged per token:

$$
\ell = \frac{1}{T_y}\sum_{i=1}^{T_y} -\log \hat{y}_{i,\,y_i},
$$

where $y_i$ is the symbol that really belongs at position $i$, and $T_y$ is the length of the
output, which needn't equal $T_x$. What matters is where that loss lives: *only* in the decoder. The
encoder predicts nothing at any of its positions, so it contributes not a single term to $\ell$. In
the previous lesson's model every position had its own output and its own loss term; here, half the
network has neither.

One detail about the token $u_{i-1}$ the decoder reads at each step. During training it's given the
correct symbol (the one from the real output, even if the decoder got its own prediction wrong);
this is called *teacher forcing*, and it makes training more stable. During generation there's no
correct answer to give it, so its own output is fed back in. It's the same split between training
and generating as in the previous lesson, with <W>\<GO></W> at the start instead of a space.

## How the encoder learns with no output of its own

If the encoder has no loss, where does its gradient come from? From a single place, and that's what
lets this model train end to end despite being two networks. The backward pass starts at the only
loss there is, the decoder's, and step for step it's the one from <Leccion slug="proyecto-char-lm">the lesson on the character-level language model</Leccion>, without a line changed: each position starts an error from its output and
transports it to the previous step. What's new happens at the end of that pass. The decoder's first
state is $\mathbf{s}_0 = \mathbf{c}$, so once the error has finished crossing the decoder backwards,
it leaves a gradient on the context vector:

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{c}}\ell = \left(\mathbf{W}^{\text{dec}}_{hh}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}_1,
$$

where $\boldsymbol{\delta}_1$ is the error at the decoder's first step, the same quantity as in <Leccion slug="proyecto-char-lm">the lesson on the character-level language model</Leccion>. That
$\nabla_{\mathbf{c}}\ell$ is the only gradient the encoder receives, and it's all the encoder needs:
since $\mathbf{c} = \bar{\mathbf{h}}_{T_x}$, it's exactly the gradient on the encoder's last state,
and from there the encoder runs its own backpropagation through time (BPTT), as if
$\nabla_{\mathbf{c}}\ell$ had come from an output at its last step. Spread backwards over its $T_x$
steps, that single gradient adjusts $\mathbf{W}^{\text{enc}}_{hh}$, $\mathbf{W}^{\text{enc}}_{xh}$
and $\mathbf{b}^{\text{enc}}_h$.

The limit is already visible here. The whole decoder hangs off $\mathbf{c}$, and the whole encoder
is corrected through $\mathbf{c}$: if some part of the input didn't make it into those $d_h$
coordinates, the decoder can't use it, and the gradient can't ask for it to be kept. The longer the
input, the more has to be squeezed into the same vector.

<Details summary="See the full backward pass, from the decoder to the encoder">
The decoder's backward pass is the one from <Leccion slug="proyecto-char-lm">the lesson on the character-level language model</Leccion>, with $\mathbf{s}$ in place of $\mathbf{h}$: the error at the
pre-activation of each position is
$$
\boldsymbol{\delta}_i = \left(\mathbf{1} - \mathbf{s}_i \odot \mathbf{s}_i\right) \odot \left(\mathbf{W}_{hy}^{\top}\left(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_i - \mathbf{y}_i\right) + \left(\mathbf{W}^{\text{dec}}_{hh}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{i+1}\right),
$$
with $\boldsymbol{\delta}_{T_y+1} = \mathbf{0}$. After crossing all $T_y$ steps, the transport
reaches $\mathbf{s}_0 = \mathbf{c}$ and stops there: $\nabla_{\mathbf{c}}\ell = (\mathbf{W}^{\text{dec}}_{hh})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}_1$.
That vector kicks off the encoder's backward pass, which is the one from <Leccion slug="bptt">the lesson on BPTT</Leccion> but *with no output term* (the
encoder makes no prediction at any step, so the term with $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ disappears and only the
transport is left):
$$
\bar{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_j = \left(\mathbf{1} - \bar{\mathbf{h}}_j \odot \bar{\mathbf{h}}_j\right) \odot \left(\mathbf{W}^{\text{enc}}_{hh}\right)^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j+1},
$$
starting at the last step with $\bar{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{T_x} = \left(\mathbf{1} - \mathbf{c} \odot \mathbf{c}\right) \odot \nabla_{\mathbf{c}}\ell$. That single entry point, $\mathbf{c}$, is the
encoder's only contact with the loss.
</Details>

## Reversing sequences in NumPy

Now to train a seq2seq on the toy task: reversing strings of symbols. It's a toy on purpose (real
translation doesn't fit in the browser), but it has the whole shape of the problem: two networks, a
context vector between them, and an output that isn't a continuation of the input. The alphabet is
six letters, plus a <W>\<GO></W> symbol that only ever enters the decoder, to get it started. Each
example is a random string of 3 to 7 letters, paired with its reverse. The cell builds the two
networks, chains the two backward passes (the decoder's and, hanging off $\mathbf{c}$, the
encoder's) and trains. Watch the loss per character it prints every few hundred steps.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

# Toy task: read a string of letters and write it backwards.
V = list("abcdef")                 # the 6 letters: the encoder's input and the decoder's output
GO = 6                             # start symbol; it only enters the decoder, never predicted
n_v, n_sym = 7, 6                  # 7 inputs (letters + GO); 6 output classes
d_h, eta, kappa, iters = 64, 0.10, 5.0, 1200

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)     # fixed seed: you'll see these numbers
sc = 0.1
Wxh_e = rng.normal(size=(d_h, n_v)) * sc; Whh_e = rng.normal(size=(d_h, d_h)) * sc; bh_e = np.zeros(d_h)
Wxh_d = rng.normal(size=(d_h, n_v)) * sc; Whh_d = rng.normal(size=(d_h, d_h)) * sc; bh_d = np.zeros(d_h)
Why = rng.normal(size=(n_sym, d_h)) * sc; by = np.zeros(n_sym)
weights = [Wxh_e, Whh_e, bh_e, Wxh_d, Whh_d, bh_d, Why, by]


def train_step(x, y):                                  # x: the source; y: the source reversed
    L = len(x)
    He = np.zeros((d_h, L)); h = np.zeros(d_h)         # --- encoder: reads and summarises ---
    for j in range(L):
        h = np.tanh(Wxh_e[:, x[j]] + Whh_e @ h + bh_e); He[:, j] = h
    c = h                                              # context vector: the last state
    dec_in = np.concatenate(([GO], y[:-1]))            # decoder with teacher forcing: GO, y1, ..., y_{L-1}
    S = np.zeros((d_h, L)); P = np.zeros((n_sym, L)); s = c.copy(); loss = 0.0
    for i in range(L):                                 # --- decoder: writes from c ---
        s = np.tanh(Wxh_d[:, dec_in[i]] + Whh_d @ s + bh_d); S[:, i] = s
        o = Why @ s + by; o -= o.max(); p = np.exp(o); p /= p.sum(); P[:, i] = p
        loss -= np.log(p[y[i]] + 1e-12)
    gWhy = np.zeros_like(Why); gby = np.zeros_like(by)  # --- decoder backward pass ---
    gWxh_d = np.zeros_like(Wxh_d); gWhh_d = np.zeros_like(Whh_d); gbh_d = np.zeros_like(bh_d)
    ds = np.zeros(d_h)
    for i in reversed(range(L)):
        dO = P[:, i].copy(); dO[y[i]] -= 1.0
        gWhy += np.outer(dO, S[:, i]); gby += dO
        dp = (1 - S[:, i] ** 2) * (Why.T @ dO + ds)
        s_prev = S[:, i - 1] if i > 0 else c
        gWhh_d += np.outer(dp, s_prev); gWxh_d[:, dec_in[i]] += dp; gbh_d += dp
        ds = Whh_d.T @ dp
    gWxh_e = np.zeros_like(Wxh_e); gWhh_e = np.zeros_like(Whh_e); gbh_e = np.zeros_like(bh_e)
    dh = ds                                            # the only link: the gradient at c
    for j in reversed(range(L)):                       # --- encoder backward pass ---
        dp = (1 - He[:, j] ** 2) * dh
        h_prev = He[:, j - 1] if j > 0 else np.zeros(d_h)
        gWhh_e += np.outer(dp, h_prev); gWxh_e[:, x[j]] += dp; gbh_e += dp
        dh = Whh_e.T @ dp
    return loss / L, [g / L for g in (gWxh_e, gWhh_e, gbh_e, gWxh_d, gWhh_d, gbh_d, gWhy, gby)]


smooth = None
for it in range(iters):
    L = int(rng.integers(3, 8))                        # lengths 3..7
    x = rng.integers(0, n_sym, size=L); y = x[::-1].copy()
    loss, grads = train_step(x, y)
    for g in grads:
        nrm = np.linalg.norm(g)
        if nrm > kappa: g *= kappa / nrm               # gradient clipping
    for Pm, g in zip(weights, grads):
        Pm -= eta * g
    smooth = loss if smooth is None else 0.99 * smooth + 0.01 * loss
    if it % 300 == 0 or it == iters - 1:
        print("iter %4d   loss/char %.3f" % (it, smooth))
`} />

The loss starts near $\ln 6 \approx 1.79$ (the cost of spreading the probability evenly over the six
letters, an echo of the previous lesson's $\ln 26$ with a six-sided die instead of a twenty-six-sided
one) and falls to around $0.75$. It doesn't reach zero, and it shouldn't: the loop is cut short to
fit within the few seconds the browser allows a cell, and even fully trained, this model has a
ceiling, which the next cell brings out. It takes the trained weights, asks them to reverse strings
they never saw during training, and measures how many characters come out right at each length.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

# Needs the previous cell: the trained weights, V, n_sym.
def write(x):                                          # generates without teacher forcing: feeds itself back
    h = np.zeros(d_h)
    for j in range(len(x)):
        h = np.tanh(Wxh_e[:, x[j]] + Whh_e @ h + bh_e)
    s = h; ix = GO; out = []
    for _ in range(len(x)):
        s = np.tanh(Wxh_d[:, ix] + Whh_d @ s + bh_d)
        ix = int((Why @ s + by).argmax()); out.append(ix)   # the most probable (greedy)
    return out


rng_ex = np.random.default_rng(7)
print("A few examples, from short to long:")
for L in [3, 4, 8, 12]:
    x = rng_ex.integers(0, n_sym, size=L)
    got = write(x)
    print("  %-9s -> want %-9s  and writes %-9s  %s" % (
        "".join(V[i] for i in x), "".join(V[i] for i in x[::-1]),
        "".join(V[i] for i in got), "OK" if list(got) == list(x[::-1]) else ""))

rng_acc = np.random.default_rng(123)
print("\\nAccuracy by length (150 new strings each):")
for L in range(3, 9):
    right = total = 0
    for _ in range(150):
        x = rng_acc.integers(0, n_sym, size=L); y = x[::-1]
        right += int((np.array(write(x)) == y).sum()); total += L
    print("  L=%d   %.0f%% of characters" % (L, 100 * right / total))
`} />

Short strings come out well (three- and four-letter ones are often exactly right); long ones go
wrong, and the way they go wrong tells you where the problem lies. The beginning of what the decoder
writes (which is the end of what the encoder read, the most recent thing in $\mathbf{c}$) is usually
right; the end (which requires remembering the beginning of the input, the thing furthest back) is
where it falls apart. The table puts numbers on it: close to perfect accuracy at length 3, and
around half the characters on the longest strings. It isn't a lack of training. The long string and
the short one reach the decoder as the same $\mathbf{c}$, the same $d_h$ numbers; the long one has
more to fit into the same space, and something gets left out.

And with that, the block is complete. <Leccion slug="la-rnn-vanilla">The vanilla RNN</Leccion> read any length; <Leccion slug="bptt">backpropagation through time</Leccion> trained it; the gates of <Leccion slug="lstm">the long short-term memory (LSTM)</Leccion> and <Leccion slug="gru">the gated recurrent unit (GRU)</Leccion> gave it a memory
able to survive distance. The sequence-to-sequence model puts them all to work together on the
course's first task that turns one sequence into a different one, rather than continuing it or
hanging a verdict on it.

But the seam between the two networks is a single vector. Everything the encoder read, three tokens
or thirty, has to fit into the $d_h$ numbers of $\mathbf{c}$, and that's why long strings fray: a
whole sentence doesn't fit where a word did. That's exactly the problem the next block, the bridge
to attention, opens with. Instead of squeezing the input into a single summary and never looking at
it again, the idea will be to let the decoder look back over the whole sequence the encoder read and
choose, at every step it writes, which part it needs. How that's done (and why it reshapes the rest
of the course) is what comes next.
