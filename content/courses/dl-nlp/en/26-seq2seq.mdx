The previous lesson's character-level model can carry on a text: give it an opening and it goes on,
character by character, for as long as you let it. Carrying on is all it does. Plenty of tasks have a
different shape: shortening a paragraph to a headline, correcting a line full of typos, turning a date
written out in words into digits. In each of them one sequence goes in and another comes out, and the
second is not the continuation of the first and need not even be the same length. That shape is what
this lesson builds.

It is called a **sequence-to-sequence model** (*seq2seq*), and the idea fits in a sentence: two
networks instead of one. The first, the encoder, reads the input sequence from start to finish and
compresses it into a vector; the second, the decoder, starts from that vector and writes the output
sequence one token at a time, the way the previous lesson's model wrote. Nothing crosses between them
but that vector: everything the encoder took from the input has to fit there.

Take the simplest task with this shape: reversing. The input is <W>live</W> and the output
<W>evil</W>, the same string read backwards. To write the first letter of the output (the <W>e</W>)
the decoder needs to know which letter the input ended on; to write the last (the <W>l</W>) it needs
to remember the first. So to reverse, you have to have read the whole input before you start
writing, and that is exactly what separates the two networks. The encoder reads <W>live</W> to the
end and leaves its summary; only then does the decoder start producing <W>evil</W>.

That summary is the encoder's last state, and it has a name: the **context vector**, $\mathbf{c}$.
It is a vector of fixed size (as many coordinates as the state has, not one more) and it is the only
thing of the input the decoder ever gets to see. The decoder doesn't look at <W>live</W> again: it
starts from $\mathbf{c}$ and from there writes by feeding itself what it produces, the way the
previous lesson's model generated.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
The encoder reads the input sequence and summarises it in a fixed vector, $\mathbf{c}$. The decoder
starts from $\mathbf{c}$ and writes the output sequence token by token, feeding back what it
produces. The two networks train together, even though only the decoder has a loss.
</Callout>

## The encoder summarises, the decoder writes

Both networks are <Leccion slug="la-rnn-vanilla">the vanilla recurrent neural network (RNN)</Leccion>, with nothing new in the
recurrence; the only new thing is how they are chained. The **encoder** reads the input tokens
$\mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_{T_x}$ (each $\mathbf{x}_j$ the one-hot vector of the $j$-th symbol,
as in the previous lesson) and updates its state:

$$
\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j = \tanh\left(\mathbf{W}^{\text{enc}}_{xh}\mathbf{x}_j + \mathbf{W}^{\text{enc}}_{hh}\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{j-1} + \mathbf{b}^{\text{enc}}_h\right),
$$

with $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_0 = \mathbf{0}$. The bar over $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_j$ marks it as the encoder's
state, so it can't be confused with the decoder's; the superscript $\text{enc}$ does the same for its
weights, and it is not a layer index (the encoder has no layers to number) but a label saying which
network they belong to. When the encoder finishes reading, its last state is the context vector:

$$
\mathbf{c} = \bar{\mathbf{h}}_{T_x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}.
$$

Look at the size of $\mathbf{c}$: $d_h$ coordinates, the state's, and that number doesn't depend on
$T_x$. An input of three tokens and one of thirty produce a $\mathbf{c}$ of the same size.

The **decoder** is another RNN, with its own weights, that starts where the encoder finished: its
initial state is $\mathbf{s}_0 = \mathbf{c}$. From there it produces the output step by step. At step
$i$ it reads a token (at the first step <W>\<GO></W>, the symbol the decoder starts from, and after
that the previous token) and updates its state:

$$
\mathbf{s}_i = \tanh\left(\mathbf{W}^{\text{dec}}_{xh}\mathbf{o}_{u_{i-1}} + \mathbf{W}^{\text{dec}}_{hh}\mathbf{s}_{i-1} + \mathbf{b}^{\text{dec}}_h\right),
$$

where $\mathbf{o}_{u_{i-1}}$ is the one-hot vector of the token $u_{i-1}$ that enters at position $i$.
From each state hangs a distribution over the next symbol, as in the previous lesson's language
model:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{y}}_i = \text{softmax}\left(\mathbf{W}_{hy}\mathbf{s}_i + \mathbf{b}_y\right),
$$

and the loss adds one cross-entropy per output position, averaged per token:

$$
\ell = \frac{1}{T_y}\sum_{i=1}^{T_y} -\log \hat{y}_{i,\,y_i},
$$

with $y_i$ the symbol that really belonged at position $i$ and $T_y$ the length of the output, which
need not equal $T_x$. What matters is where that loss lives: *only* in the decoder. The encoder
predicts nothing at any of its positions, so it contributes not a single term to $\ell$. In the
previous lesson's model every position had its output and its loss term; here, half the network has
none.

A note on the token $u_{i-1}$ the decoder reads at each step. In training it is given the correct
symbol (the one in the real output, even if the decoder got its own wrong); this is called *teacher
forcing*, and it makes training more stable. In generation there is no correct answer to give it, so
its own output is fed back. It is the same distinction between training and generating as in the
previous lesson, with <W>\<GO></W> at the start instead of a space.

## How the encoder learns with no output of its own

If the encoder has no loss, where does its gradient come from? From a single place, and that is why
this model trains end to end despite being two networks. The backward pass starts at the only loss
there is, the decoder's, and step by step it is the one from <Leccion slug="proyecto-char-lm">the lesson on the character-level language model</Leccion>, without changing a line: each position starts its error from its output and transports
it to the previous step. What is new happens at the end of that pass. The decoder's first state is
$\mathbf{s}_0 = \mathbf{c}$, so when the error has finished crossing the decoder backwards, it leaves
a gradient on the context vector:

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{c}}\ell = \left(\mathbf{W}^{\text{dec}}_{hh}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}_1,
$$

where $\boldsymbol{\delta}_1$ is the error of the decoder's first step, the same one as in <Leccion slug="proyecto-char-lm">the lesson on the character-level language model</Leccion>. That
$\nabla_{\mathbf{c}}\ell$ is the only gradient the encoder receives, and it is all it needs: since
$\mathbf{c} = \bar{\mathbf{h}}_{T_x}$, it is exactly the gradient on the encoder's last state, and
from there the encoder runs its own backpropagation through time (BPTT), as if $\nabla_{\mathbf{c}}\ell$
had come from an output on its last step. Shared out backwards over its $T_x$ steps, that single
gradient adjusts $\mathbf{W}^{\text{enc}}_{hh}$, $\mathbf{W}^{\text{enc}}_{xh}$ and
$\mathbf{b}^{\text{enc}}_h$.

The limit already shows here. The whole decoder hangs from $\mathbf{c}$, and the whole encoder is
corrected through $\mathbf{c}$: if something of the input didn't make it into those $d_h$
coordinates, the decoder can't use it and the gradient can't ask for it to be kept. The longer the
input, the more has to be squeezed into the same vector.

<Details summary="See the full backward pass, from the decoder to the encoder">
The decoder's backward pass is the one from <Leccion slug="proyecto-char-lm">the lesson on the character-level language model</Leccion>, with $\mathbf{s}$ in place of $\mathbf{h}$: the error of the
pre-activation at each position is
$$
\boldsymbol{\delta}_i = \left(\mathbf{1} - \mathbf{s}_i \odot \mathbf{s}_i\right) \odot \left(\mathbf{W}_{hy}^{\top}\left(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_i - \mathbf{y}_i\right) + \left(\mathbf{W}^{\text{dec}}_{hh}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{i+1}\right),
$$
with $\boldsymbol{\delta}_{T_y+1} = \mathbf{0}$. Once the $T_y$ steps are crossed, the transport
reaches $\mathbf{s}_0 = \mathbf{c}$ and stops there: $\nabla_{\mathbf{c}}\ell = (\mathbf{W}^{\text{dec}}_{hh})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\delta}_1$.
That vector starts the encoder's backward pass, which is the one from <Leccion slug="bptt">the lesson on BPTT</Leccion> but *with no output term* (the
encoder predicts at no step, so the term with $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ disappears and only the transport is
left):
$$
\bar{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_j = \left(\mathbf{1} - \bar{\mathbf{h}}_j \odot \bar{\mathbf{h}}_j\right) \odot \left(\mathbf{W}^{\text{enc}}_{hh}\right)^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j+1},
$$
starting at the last step with $\bar{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{T_x} = \left(\mathbf{1} - \mathbf{c} \odot \mathbf{c}\right) \odot \nabla_{\mathbf{c}}\ell$. That single entry point, $\mathbf{c}$, is all the
contact the encoder has with the loss.
</Details>

## Reversing sequences in NumPy

Now to train a seq2seq on the toy task: reversing strings of symbols. A toy on purpose (a real
translation doesn't fit in the browser), but with the whole shape of the problem: two networks, a
context vector in between, and an output that isn't the continuation of the input. The alphabet is
six letters, plus a <W>\<GO></W> symbol that only enters the decoder, to start it. Each example is a
random string of between 3 and 7 letters and its reverse. The cell builds the two networks, chains
the two backward passes (the decoder's and, hanging from $\mathbf{c}$, the encoder's) and trains.
Watch the loss per character it prints every few hundred steps.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

# Toy task: read a string of letters and write it backwards.
V = list("abcdef")                 # the 6 letters: the encoder's input and the decoder's output
GO = 6                             # start symbol; it only enters the decoder, never predicted
n_v, n_sym = 7, 6                  # 7 inputs (letters + GO); 6 output classes
d_h, eta, kappa, iters = 64, 0.10, 5.0, 1200

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)     # fixed seed: you'll see these numbers
sc = 0.1
Wxh_e = rng.normal(size=(d_h, n_v)) * sc; Whh_e = rng.normal(size=(d_h, d_h)) * sc; bh_e = np.zeros(d_h)
Wxh_d = rng.normal(size=(d_h, n_v)) * sc; Whh_d = rng.normal(size=(d_h, d_h)) * sc; bh_d = np.zeros(d_h)
Why = rng.normal(size=(n_sym, d_h)) * sc; by = np.zeros(n_sym)
weights = [Wxh_e, Whh_e, bh_e, Wxh_d, Whh_d, bh_d, Why, by]


def train_step(x, y):                                  # x: the source; y: the source reversed
    L = len(x)
    He = np.zeros((d_h, L)); h = np.zeros(d_h)         # --- encoder: reads and summarises ---
    for j in range(L):
        h = np.tanh(Wxh_e[:, x[j]] + Whh_e @ h + bh_e); He[:, j] = h
    c = h                                              # context vector: the last state
    dec_in = np.concatenate(([GO], y[:-1]))            # decoder with teacher forcing: GO, y1, ..., y_{L-1}
    S = np.zeros((d_h, L)); P = np.zeros((n_sym, L)); s = c.copy(); loss = 0.0
    for i in range(L):                                 # --- decoder: writes from c ---
        s = np.tanh(Wxh_d[:, dec_in[i]] + Whh_d @ s + bh_d); S[:, i] = s
        o = Why @ s + by; o -= o.max(); p = np.exp(o); p /= p.sum(); P[:, i] = p
        loss -= np.log(p[y[i]] + 1e-12)
    gWhy = np.zeros_like(Why); gby = np.zeros_like(by)  # --- decoder backward pass ---
    gWxh_d = np.zeros_like(Wxh_d); gWhh_d = np.zeros_like(Whh_d); gbh_d = np.zeros_like(bh_d)
    ds = np.zeros(d_h)
    for i in reversed(range(L)):
        dO = P[:, i].copy(); dO[y[i]] -= 1.0
        gWhy += np.outer(dO, S[:, i]); gby += dO
        dp = (1 - S[:, i] ** 2) * (Why.T @ dO + ds)
        s_prev = S[:, i - 1] if i > 0 else c
        gWhh_d += np.outer(dp, s_prev); gWxh_d[:, dec_in[i]] += dp; gbh_d += dp
        ds = Whh_d.T @ dp
    gWxh_e = np.zeros_like(Wxh_e); gWhh_e = np.zeros_like(Whh_e); gbh_e = np.zeros_like(bh_e)
    dh = ds                                            # the only link: the gradient at c
    for j in reversed(range(L)):                       # --- encoder backward pass ---
        dp = (1 - He[:, j] ** 2) * dh
        h_prev = He[:, j - 1] if j > 0 else np.zeros(d_h)
        gWhh_e += np.outer(dp, h_prev); gWxh_e[:, x[j]] += dp; gbh_e += dp
        dh = Whh_e.T @ dp
    return loss / L, [g / L for g in (gWxh_e, gWhh_e, gbh_e, gWxh_d, gWhh_d, gbh_d, gWhy, gby)]


smooth = None
for it in range(iters):
    L = int(rng.integers(3, 8))                        # lengths 3..7
    x = rng.integers(0, n_sym, size=L); y = x[::-1].copy()
    loss, grads = train_step(x, y)
    for g in grads:
        nrm = np.linalg.norm(g)
        if nrm > kappa: g *= kappa / nrm               # gradient clipping
    for Pm, g in zip(weights, grads):
        Pm -= eta * g
    smooth = loss if smooth is None else 0.99 * smooth + 0.01 * loss
    if it % 300 == 0 or it == iters - 1:
        print("iter %4d   loss/char %.3f" % (it, smooth))
`} />

The loss starts near $\ln 6 \approx 1.79$ (what it costs to spread the probability evenly over the
six letters, the echo of the previous lesson's $\ln 26$ with six faces instead of twenty-six) and
falls to around $0.75$. It doesn't reach zero, and it shouldn't: the loop is cut short to fit in the
few seconds the browser gives a cell, and even fully trained this model has a ceiling, which the next
cell brings out. It takes the trained weights, asks them to reverse strings they never saw in
training, and measures how many characters come out right at each length.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

# Needs the previous cell: the trained weights, V, n_sym.
def write(x):                                          # generates without teacher forcing: feeds itself back
    h = np.zeros(d_h)
    for j in range(len(x)):
        h = np.tanh(Wxh_e[:, x[j]] + Whh_e @ h + bh_e)
    s = h; ix = GO; out = []
    for _ in range(len(x)):
        s = np.tanh(Wxh_d[:, ix] + Whh_d @ s + bh_d)
        ix = int((Why @ s + by).argmax()); out.append(ix)   # the most probable (greedy)
    return out


rng_ex = np.random.default_rng(7)
print("A few examples, from short to long:")
for L in [3, 4, 8, 12]:
    x = rng_ex.integers(0, n_sym, size=L)
    got = write(x)
    print("  %-9s -> want %-9s  and writes %-9s  %s" % (
        "".join(V[i] for i in x), "".join(V[i] for i in x[::-1]),
        "".join(V[i] for i in got), "OK" if list(got) == list(x[::-1]) else ""))

rng_acc = np.random.default_rng(123)
print("\\nAccuracy by length (150 new strings each):")
for L in range(3, 9):
    right = total = 0
    for _ in range(150):
        x = rng_acc.integers(0, n_sym, size=L); y = x[::-1]
        right += int((np.array(write(x)) == y).sum()); total += L
    print("  L=%d   %.0f%% of characters" % (L, 100 * right / total))
`} />

Short strings come out well (those of three or four letters, often exactly); long ones go wrong, and
they go wrong in a way that says where the problem comes from. The beginning of what the decoder
writes (which is the end of what the encoder read, the most recent thing in $\mathbf{c}$) is usually
right; the end (which asks it to remember the beginning of the input, the furthest thing back) is
where it falls apart. The table puts numbers on it: close to perfect accuracy at length 3, and around
half the characters on the longest. It isn't a lack of training. The long string and the short one
reach the decoder as the same $\mathbf{c}$, the same $d_h$ numbers; the long one has more to fit in
the same place, and something is left out.

With this the block closes. <Leccion slug="la-rnn-vanilla">The vanilla RNN</Leccion> read any length; <Leccion slug="bptt">backpropagation through time</Leccion> trained it; the gates of <Leccion slug="lstm">the long short-term memory (LSTM)</Leccion> and <Leccion slug="gru">the gated recurrent unit (GRU)</Leccion> gave it a memory
able to survive distance. The sequence-to-sequence model puts them all to work together on the
course's first task that turns one sequence into a different one, instead of only continuing it or
hanging a verdict on it.

But the seam between the two networks is a single vector. Everything the encoder read (three tokens
or thirty) has to fit in the $d_h$ numbers of $\mathbf{c}$, and that is why long strings fray: a
whole sentence doesn't fit where a word did. That is exactly the problem the next block, the bridge
to attention, opens with. Instead of squeezing the input into a single summary and never looking at
it again, the idea will be to let the decoder look back over the whole sequence it read and choose,
at every step it writes, which part of it it needs. How that is done (and why it reorders the whole
course) is what comes next.
