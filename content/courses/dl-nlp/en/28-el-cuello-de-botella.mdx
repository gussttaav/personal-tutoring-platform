The decoder from the previous lesson, on the encoder-decoder architecture, never reads the input
sentence: it reads a vector. $\mathbf{c}$ has $d_h$ coordinates, that number is fixed when the
network is built (before it has seen a single sentence), and it's the same for five tokens as for
five hundred. That lesson recorded this as what it is: a bet on what fits in $d_h$ numbers, not
something it had shown.

A bet can be lost in two very different ways, and telling them apart is the whole of today's work. A
model can fail because it's undertrained, and then training it more fixes it. Or it can fail because
what you ask of it doesn't fit where you ask it to fit, and then no amount of training will help: the
wall doesn't move. This lesson takes the two separately. First it counts two quantities and compares
them. One is how many distinct values $\mathbf{c}$ can take: I'll call each of them a *message*,
because it's the only thing the encoder gets through to the decoder. The other is how many distinct
*sentences* the encoder can receive, taking a sentence to mean any combination of tokens from the
input vocabulary with a given length, not only the ones that appear in the data. For the decoder to
be able to reconstruct every sentence, each one needs a message of its own. Then it measures what
happens to a real model as the input grows longer, with two state widths and several training
budgets.

The two quantities in the count grow in incompatible ways, and the explorable lays one over the
other. The green line doesn't move with the length: it says how many distinct messages a vector of
$d_h$ coordinates can encode at most, when $q$ levels can be told apart in each coordinate (where
$q$ comes from is the next section's business). That number knows nothing about the sentence it's
summarising. The amber one counts the sentences of each length, and climbs a full rung for every
token you add. Widen the state or raise the levels as far as you like, then look for the point where
the two cross.

## How many messages fit, and how many sentences there are

The encoder's state comes out of a $\tanh$, so each coordinate of $\mathbf{c}$ lives in the interval
$(-1, 1)$. On paper that's a continuum and anything fits in it; in a model that is trained with
gradients and read by another network, it isn't. Let $q$ be the number of levels of one coordinate
that can really be told apart: two values closer together than that reach the decoder as the same
number. I pick the value of $q$ myself: eight levels, sixteen, thirty-two, depending on how
optimistic you want to be. What follows doesn't depend on which you choose.

With that convention, the encoder has a finite number of distinct messages to send, and the
sentences it has to tell apart are all those of length $T_x$ over the input vocabulary:

$$
\lvert\{\text{messages}\}\rvert \le q^{d_h},
\qquad
\lvert\{\text{sentences of length } T_x\}\rvert = \lvert V_x \rvert^{T_x}.
$$

Look at where each letter sits. In the first count, $T_x$ *doesn't appear*: the context vector is
as big for a short sentence as for a long one, which is exactly the previous lesson's design
decision. In the second, $T_x$ is in the exponent.

Now the move that turns that asymmetry into an impossibility. Once $\mathbf{c}$ is fixed, everything
the decoder writes is determined: it starts at $\mathbf{s}_0 = \mathbf{c}$, at each step writes the
most probable token (the one with the largest coordinate in its output vector) and feeds that token
back in. It's the previous lesson's greedy decoding. The complete output is therefore a *function*
of $\mathbf{c}$, and a function can't give two different answers to the same argument. If there are
fewer messages than sentences, two different sentences share a message and the decoder returns the
same output for both, so it reconstructs at least one of them wrongly. Counting the ones it can get
right:

$$
\frac{\lvert\{\text{sentences reconstructed exactly}\}\rvert}{\lvert V_x \rvert^{T_x}}
\le \min\left(1,\, \frac{q^{d_h}}{\lvert V_x \rvert^{T_x}}\right).
$$

The ceiling is $1$ (it says nothing) as long as the sentences fit, and from there on it's divided by
$\lvert V_x \rvert$ with every new token. We find the point where it changes by taking logarithms in
$\lvert V_x \rvert^{T_x} \le q^{d_h}$:

$$
T^{\star} = \left\lfloor \frac{d_h \log q}{\log \lvert V_x \rvert} \right\rfloor.
$$

Plug in numbers that real models use. A state of $d_h = 256$ coordinates, eight levels per
coordinate and a subword vocabulary of $\lvert V_x \rvert = 32\,768$ entries give $T^{\star} = 51$
tokens; ten tokens further on, the ceiling above has fallen to $10^{-44}$. And there's one word that
appears in none of the three formulas: training. Neither the number of iterations, nor the learning
rate, nor the data enters the argument, because what we counted is how many different answers a
mechanism can give at most, not how well it chooses among them.

<Callout type="warning" title="What widening the state buys">
$T^{\star}$ is linear in $d_h$: doubling the state width doubles the maximum length. The recurrence,
on the other hand, holds $\mathbf{W}_{hh} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h \times d_h}$, so doubling $d_h$
multiplies those weights by four. You buy length linearly and pay for it quadratically.
</Callout>

## A ceiling far above where it breaks

That count proves the wall exists. It doesn't say where the wall is, and the difference is worth
being explicit about. The next section trains encoder-decoders on a toy task: read a string of
letters, written with only four of them (<W>a</W>, <W>b</W>, <W>c</W> and <W>d</W>), and write it
backwards. The best of them has $d_h = 32$, and with $q = 8$ levels and $\lvert V_x \rvert = 4$ the
count gives it a ceiling of $T^{\star} = 48$ tokens. Even so, it will reverse only one five-letter
string in three correctly.

The gap between those two figures has an explanation, and it's material from the previous block seen
from a new place. The gradient that teaches the encoder to keep token $j$ of the input leaves from
the loss term at position $i$ of the output, goes back through the decoder's steps as far as
$\mathbf{s}_0 = \mathbf{c}$, and from there carries on through the encoder's down to position $j$.
There's no other route ($\mathbf{c}$ is the only point of contact between the two networks), and its
length is

$$
i + (T_x - j) \quad \text{multiplications},
$$

largest when $j = 1$, that is, for the first token of the input. <Leccion slug="gradiente-desvanecido">The lesson
on the vanishing gradient</Leccion> says what happens to a signal that crosses that route: each step
multiplies it by $\mathbf{W}_{hh}^{\top}$ and by the $\tanh$ mask, and with $\gamma\,\sigma_{\max}$
(the steepest slope of the $\tanh$ times the largest singular value of $\mathbf{W}_{hh}$) below one,
the magnitude falls exponentially with the distance. The first token of the sentence receives the
weakest signal of all.

So the encoder doesn't share $\mathbf{c}$ out among the positions the way an optimal code would: it
fills it mostly with what it read last, because that's the only thing it manages to learn to keep.
The $48$ tokens of the count assume a perfect encoding; the handful of letters in the experiment is
what you get by training a recurrence with gradient descent. Both are true and they say different
things: counting proves the wall exists; training shows where it is.

## Two state widths and three training budgets

The task is the one from <Leccion slug="seq2seq">the lesson on sequence-to-sequence models</Leccion>:
read a string of letters and write it backwards. It's deliberately poor (no grammar, no meaning,
nothing to generalise), and that poverty is what keeps the experiment clean: reversing a string takes
nothing beyond remembering it, so every failure is a failure of memory.

The first cell builds a model of whatever width you ask for and defines its forward and backward
passes, line for line the previous lesson's. The only thing to look at is that $\mathbf{c}$ is still
the only link between the two networks: a single variable, marked in the code.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

# The sequence-to-sequence task from the previous block: read a string of letters and write it backwards.
V = list("abcd")                                       # four letters: |V_x| = 4
n_sym, GO = 4, 4                                       # GO only enters the decoder; it's never predicted
L_MIN, L_MAX = 2, 7
eta, kappa = 0.10, 5.0


def build(d_h):                                        # same seed: every model starts the same way
    r = np.random.default_rng(1); sc = 0.1
    return [r.normal(size=(d_h, 5)) * sc, r.normal(size=(d_h, d_h)) * sc, np.zeros(d_h),
            r.normal(size=(d_h, 5)) * sc, r.normal(size=(d_h, d_h)) * sc, np.zeros(d_h),
            r.normal(size=(n_sym, d_h)) * sc, np.zeros(n_sym)]


def train_step(weights, x, y):                         # forward and backward pass, as in the previous lesson
    Wxh_e, Whh_e, bh_e, Wxh_d, Whh_d, bh_d, Why, by = weights
    d_h = len(bh_e); L = len(x)
    He = np.zeros((d_h, L)); h = np.zeros(d_h)
    for j in range(L):
        h = np.tanh(Wxh_e[:, x[j]] + Whh_e @ h + bh_e); He[:, j] = h
    c = h                                              # the bottleneck: everything passes through here
    dec_in = np.concatenate(([GO], y[:-1]))            # teacher forcing
    S = np.zeros((d_h, L)); P = np.zeros((n_sym, L)); s = c.copy()
    for i in range(L):
        s = np.tanh(Wxh_d[:, dec_in[i]] + Whh_d @ s + bh_d); S[:, i] = s
        o = Why @ s + by; o -= o.max(); p = np.exp(o); P[:, i] = p / p.sum()
    gWxh_e = np.zeros_like(Wxh_e); gWhh_e = np.zeros_like(Whh_e); gbh_e = np.zeros_like(bh_e)
    gWxh_d = np.zeros_like(Wxh_d); gWhh_d = np.zeros_like(Whh_d); gbh_d = np.zeros_like(bh_d)
    gWhy = np.zeros_like(Why); gby = np.zeros_like(by); ds = np.zeros(d_h)
    for i in reversed(range(L)):                       # --- decoder backward pass ---
        dO = P[:, i].copy(); dO[y[i]] -= 1.0
        gWhy += np.outer(dO, S[:, i]); gby += dO
        dp = (1 - S[:, i] ** 2) * (Why.T @ dO + ds)
        gWhh_d += np.outer(dp, S[:, i - 1] if i > 0 else c); gWxh_d[:, dec_in[i]] += dp; gbh_d += dp
        ds = Whh_d.T @ dp
    dh = ds                                            # the only link between the two networks
    for j in reversed(range(L)):                       # --- encoder backward pass ---
        dp = (1 - He[:, j] ** 2) * dh
        gWhh_e += np.outer(dp, He[:, j - 1] if j > 0 else np.zeros(d_h))
        gWxh_e[:, x[j]] += dp; gbh_e += dp
        dh = Whh_e.T @ dp
    return [g / L for g in (gWxh_e, gWhh_e, gbh_e, gWxh_d, gWhh_d, gbh_d, gWhy, gby)]
`} />

The second trains and measures: for each length, the percentage of strings that come out *exactly*
right (a hundred and twenty new strings behind each number, generated without *teacher forcing*).
Four rows come out. The first three are the same narrow state, $d_h = 8$, at three points in its
training; the fourth has a state four times as wide and trains half as long as the third. The test
strings are the same in all four rows and the networks start from the same seed, so the only
difference between rows is the one printed at the start of each.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

# Needs the previous cell: build, train_step, n_sym, GO, L_MIN, L_MAX, eta, kappa.
def write(weights, x):                                 # greedy generation, no teacher forcing
    Wxh_e, Whh_e, bh_e, Wxh_d, Whh_d, bh_d, Why, by = weights
    h = np.zeros(len(bh_e))
    for j in range(len(x)):
        h = np.tanh(Wxh_e[:, x[j]] + Whh_e @ h + bh_e)
    s = h; ix = GO; out = []
    for _ in range(len(x)):
        s = np.tanh(Wxh_d[:, ix] + Whh_d @ s + bh_d)
        ix = int((Why @ s + by).argmax()); out.append(ix)
    return out


def accuracy(weights):                                 # % of strings EXACTLY right, by length
    test_rng = np.random.default_rng(123)              # the same test strings in every row
    row = []
    for L in range(L_MIN, L_MAX + 1):
        right = 0
        for _ in range(120):
            x = test_rng.integers(0, n_sym, size=L)
            right += int(write(weights, x) == list(x[::-1]))
        row.append(100 * right / 120)
    return row


print("  model                " + "".join(" L=%d   " % L for L in range(L_MIN, L_MAX + 1)))
for d_h, checkpoints in [(8, (250, 1000, 2000)), (32, (1000,))]:
    weights = build(d_h); rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
    for it in range(max(checkpoints)):
        L = int(rng.integers(L_MIN, L_MAX + 1))
        x = rng.integers(0, n_sym, size=L); y = x[::-1].copy()
        grads = train_step(weights, x, y)
        for g in grads:
            nrm = np.linalg.norm(g)
            if nrm > kappa: g *= kappa / nrm           # gradient clipping, from the previous block
        for Pm, g in zip(weights, grads):
            Pm -= eta * g
        if it + 1 in checkpoints:
            print("  d_h=%2d, %4d iter    " % (d_h, it + 1)
                  + "".join("%3.0f%%   " % v for v in accuracy(weights)))
`} />

Read the table in pairs of rows. From the first to the second, training does exactly what you'd
expect of it: in 750 more iterations, the two- and three-letter strings go from never coming out to
coming out most of the time. From the second to the third, doubling the budget, that progress
continues, and only in the short columns: the two-letter strings reach a perfect score and the three-
and four-letter ones climb another stretch, while the five-, six- and seven-letter ones stay at floor
level, where they already were. That's the finding, and it's worth saying slowly: training buys
accuracy on what fits, and buys nothing on what doesn't.

And now the fourth row, which trains half as long as the third with a state four times as wide:
everything that had room to rise does, and the three long columns go from anecdotal to real (one
five-letter string in three, against one in twelve). That's the answer to the question from the
start, measured rather than guessed. The width of $\mathbf{c}$ and the training budget don't buy the
same thing: the second refines the use of the vector as far as the vector reaches, and only the first
moves the wall.

The most revealing thing, though, is *how* it gets things wrong, which is the previous section seen
from the output's side. <Leccion slug="seq2seq">The lesson on sequence-to-sequence models</Leccion>
already let you see it on this same task: in a long string, the start of what the decoder writes
(which is the end of what the encoder read, the most recent thing in $\mathbf{c}$) is usually right,
and what gets scrambled is the end, which would ask it to remember the first token of the input. The
worst-kept token is the one at the end of the longest route.

The two halves of the lesson point at the same place, and it isn't the size of $d_h$. The count
compares the messages that fit with the sentences there are because we accepted that there would be
*a single* message; the route gets long because all the information passes through *a single*
point. Widening $\mathbf{c}$ moves the ceiling a few tokens and leaves both of those statements
untouched. What lies underneath isn't a small vector: it's the idea of summarising.

And put like that, one thing draws attention. The encoder computed $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_1, \dots,
\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{T_x}$, one state per input token, and the architecture throws away all but the
last. The others existed, were computed and were discarded. Getting them back (letting the decoder
look back at the whole input instead of settling for the summary) is <Leccion slug="la-idea-de-atencion">the
next lesson, on the idea of attention</Leccion>. Giving that backward look its formulas takes up the
rest of the block.
