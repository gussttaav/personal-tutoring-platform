The previous lesson, on the artificial neuron, left one piece unchosen. The weights and the bias are
the neuron (they fix the hyperplane, and with it everything it can ever tell apart), but between the
pre-activation $z$ and what the neuron returns there is one more function, $\varphi$, not yet chosen:
the step fills it by default, marked provisional. Choosing it is what this lesson is about, and it
isn't a cosmetic finish. Two things depend on $\varphi$: how much of the number the neuron computes
survives, and what comes of putting one neuron behind another.

Look at what the step does with a weight that moves. Take any neuron and raise one of its weights, one
alone, from $1.0$ to $1.1$, to $1.2$, to $1.3$. The pre-activation follows the movement: it grows a
little each time, and every value in between exists. The output doesn't notice. It stays at $0$ while
$z$ is negative, and the instant $z$ crosses zero it jumps to $1$ and stays there. Everything there
was to know about how far the weight went up is lost on the way, and always at the same place.

Before writing a single candidate down it helps to look at them together. The explorable draws each
one on $[-5, 5]$, overlays its derivative as a dashed line, and shades in red the stretches where that
derivative nearly vanishes.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
The activation has two independent requirements: that it not be a straight line, because if it is then
stacking neurons builds nothing new, and that its derivative not be zero everywhere, because the
correction of the weights will later come out of it. The step meets the first and fails the second as
completely as it is possible to fail it.
</Callout>

## Why the activation cannot be a straight line

Let's start with the requirement that depends on no particular case. Put two neurons in a chain: the
first looks at the input $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ and returns a number, and the second looks at
that number and returns another. There is one neuron at each level, so the first level's weights are a
vector and the second's a scalar; the parenthesised superscript says the level, the same mark the
layers will carry once there are several neurons in each:

$$
a^{(1)} = \varphi\left(\left(\mathbf{w}^{(1)}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b^{(1)}\right),
\qquad
a^{(2)} = \varphi\left(w^{(2)} a^{(1)} + b^{(2)}\right),
$$

with $\mathbf{w}^{(1)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ and $b^{(1)}, w^{(2)}, b^{(2)} \in \mathbb{R}$. Now put the
identity in both activations, $\varphi(z) = z$, and substitute the first expression into the second:

$$
\begin{aligned}
a^{(2)}
&= w^{(2)}\left(\left(\mathbf{w}^{(1)}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b^{(1)}\right) + b^{(2)} \\[2pt]
&= \left(w^{(2)}\mathbf{w}^{(1)}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{x} + \left(w^{(2)}b^{(1)} + b^{(2)}\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

The right-hand side is a neuron. A single one, with weight vector $w^{(2)}\mathbf{w}^{(1)}$ and bias
$w^{(2)}b^{(1)} + b^{(2)}$, and no input tells it apart from the chain that produced it: for every
$\mathbf{x}$ they give the same number. The previous lesson already said what that means: everything a
neuron tells apart is on its boundary, and that boundary is a hyperplane. The chain of two separates
with a hyperplane as well. Chaining has not curved the boundary.

And there was nothing special about the identity. Assign the general line to $\varphi$,
$\varphi(z) = \alpha z + \beta$ with $\alpha \neq 0$, and you will see that $\alpha$ is absorbed into
the next level's weights and $\beta$ into its bias: what comes out is a pre-activation again. Any
straight line does the same.

It helps to say it with the exact name. The expression $\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b$ is not linear
in the algebra's sense (a linear map sends zero to zero, and this one sends it to $b$): it is
**affine**. Composing affine functions gives an affine function again, and that chains without limit:
a hundred neurons in a row with a straight line in $\varphi$ are one neuron. The property that has to
be broken (and it has to be broken in the activation, nowhere else) is the one that gives this lesson
its title: **non-linearity**, though what gets broken, precisely, is affineness.

<Details summary="The same computation with several neurons at each level">

None of the above depends on there having been a single neuron per level. With several (and, as
before, with the identity in both activations), each level's weights gather into a matrix and its
biases into a vector, and the chain becomes

$$
\mathbf{W}^{(2)}\left(\mathbf{W}^{(1)}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}^{(1)}\right) + \mathbf{b}^{(2)}
= \left(\mathbf{W}^{(2)}\mathbf{W}^{(1)}\right)\mathbf{x}
+ \left(\mathbf{W}^{(2)}\mathbf{b}^{(1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(2)}\right),
$$

which is one level again. The full matrix form, with its sizes, is <Leccion slug="forward-pass">a
lesson further on, the one on the forward pass</Leccion>; here it serves one purpose: the collapse was
not an artefact of having put one neuron at each level.

</Details>

The second requirement shows up better in what happens to the step. Its derivative is zero at every
point where it exists,

$$
\frac{d}{dz}\,\text{escalón}(z) = 0 \quad \text{for all } z \neq 0,
$$

and at $z = 0$ it does not even exist, because there the function jumps. There is a debt I'll say out
loud: how a neuron comes by its own weights from examples is <Leccion slug="descenso-gradiente">a
lesson further on, the one on gradient descent</Leccion>. All that needs saying in advance is what
that method is made of: derivatives of $\varphi$. An activation whose derivative is zero everywhere
says nothing about which direction to move the weights in, and the procedure sits still. The step
passes the first requirement (it is not a straight line, not remotely) and fails the second.

## Three candidates and their derivative

The three that follow meet both requirements, and I write them with their derivative already worked
out: the rest of the block will live off those derivatives more than off the functions.

The **sigmoid** already has a reserved symbol in the course, $\sigma$:

$$
\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}},
$$

continuous, increasing, with values in $(0, 1)$: it sends $z = 0$ to $\sigma(0) = 1/2$ and crushes the
extremes against $0$ and against $1$ without ever touching them. Let's differentiate it. Written as
$\sigma(z) = \left(1 + e^{-z}\right)^{-1}$, the chain rule and a convenient factorisation give

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d\sigma}{dz}
&= -\left(1 + e^{-z}\right)^{-2} \cdot \left(-e^{-z}\right)
 = \frac{e^{-z}}{\left(1 + e^{-z}\right)^{2}} \\[2pt]
&= \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} \cdot \frac{e^{-z}}{1 + e^{-z}}
 = \sigma(z)\left(1 - \sigma(z)\right),
\end{aligned}
$$

where

$$
\dfrac{e^{-z}}{1 + e^{-z}} = \dfrac{1 + e^{-z} - 1}{1 + e^{-z}} = 1 - \sigma(z).
$$

The derivative of the sigmoid is computed from its own value, with no fresh exponential, and that will
make it cheap when it has to be evaluated thousands of times.

The **hyperbolic tangent** has the same silhouette over a different range of values:

$$
\tanh(z) = \frac{e^{z} - e^{-z}}{e^{z} + e^{-z}}.
$$

Its values are in $(-1, 1)$ and $\tanh(0) = 0$: it is centred on zero, which is the difference that
matters next to the sigmoid, whose output is always positive. Its derivative has an equally convenient
form,

$$
\frac{d\tanh(z)}{dz} = 1 - \tanh^{2}(z),
$$

and at the origin it is $1$, four times what the sigmoid reaches at its highest point.

<Details summary="Where 1 − tanh² comes from, and the likeness between the two curves">

For the derivative, write $u = e^{z} - e^{-z}$ and $v = e^{z} + e^{-z}$: each is the derivative of the
other, $\frac{du}{dz} = v$ and $\frac{dv}{dz} = u$. The quotient rule then gives

$$
\frac{d\tanh(z)}{dz} = \frac{v \cdot v - u \cdot u}{v^{2}}
= 1 - \left(\frac{u}{v}\right)^{2} = 1 - \tanh^{2}(z).
$$

The likeness between the two curves is no accident either: the hyperbolic tangent is a sigmoid
stretched by a factor of two and shifted, $\tanh(z) = 2\sigma(2z) - 1$. Expand the right-hand side and
multiply top and bottom by $e^{z}$,

$$
2\sigma(2z) - 1 = \frac{2}{1 + e^{-2z}} - 1 = \frac{1 - e^{-2z}}{1 + e^{-2z}}
= \frac{e^{z} - e^{-z}}{e^{z} + e^{-z}} = \tanh(z),
$$

and along the way the factor of four between the two derivatives is explained: differentiating
$2\sigma(2z) - 1$ multiplies by $2$ for the outer factor and by another $2$ for the chain rule.

</Details>

The third is the youngest and the most used: **ReLU**, for rectified linear unit. It is
$\text{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z)$ (it passes the positive part through unchanged and sends the negative
part to zero), so it crushes nothing from above: the magnitude of $z$ reaches the output whole
whenever it is positive. Its derivative is

$$
\frac{d}{dz}\,\text{ReLU}(z) = \begin{cases}
  1 & \text{if } z > 0 \\
  0 & \text{if } z < 0
\end{cases},
$$

and at $z = 0$ it does not exist: from the left the slope is $0$ and from the right it is $1$, and a
function with a corner has no single tangent there. The course takes $0$ there: it is a convention,
not a theorem, and the choice changes nothing measurable.

### Where each one switches off

The three derivatives share one problem and split it up differently. Call **saturation** the region
where the derivative of $\varphi$ is so close to zero that the input can move a lot and the output
barely moves: they are the red bands of the explorable.

For the sigmoid the arithmetic is direct. At $z = 5$ it is $\sigma(5) \approx 0.9933$, so its
derivative there is $0.9933 \cdot 0.0067 \approx 0.0066$: almost forty times less than at the origin.
The hyperbolic tangent switches off earlier and faster ($1 - \tanh^{2}(5) \approx 1.8 \times 10^{-4}$,
more than thirty times below the sigmoid at the same point), and that is why its red bands start
closer to the origin even though its derivative starts out four times higher: starting higher and
falling faster are compatible.

ReLU breaks that split, and its half-graph red band does not contradict it. On the right its
derivative is $1$ exactly, as far out as you care to go: there it does not saturate, and that is its
whole appeal. On the left it does not saturate either (saturating is approaching zero): it is switched
off, with the derivative at $0$ over a whole half-axis. Out of that comes a defect with a name of its
own, the **dying ReLU**: if the pre-activation stays negative for every example it sees, the
derivative is zero on all of them, nothing moves it, and it never comes back.

That leaves the fourth button. **GELU**, for Gaussian error linear unit, is the one used by the
Transformers of Block 5; its definition calls for the cumulative distribution function of a normal,
and it stays outside this lesson. It resembles ReLU except near the origin, where instead of folding
into a corner it curves.

## The activations and their derivatives in NumPy

The cell writes the four functions and the three derivatives, and puts them through four checks. Run
it and look, in this order: whether the derivatives hold up under a numerical check, how much of each
one is left far from the origin, what happens to the chain when the identity is swapped for the
sigmoid, and what the sigmoid does with the ten reviews from the previous lesson.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

np.random.seed(0)

# --- The four candidates, and the derivative of the three that have one.
def step(z):
    return (z >= 0).astype(float)


def sigmoid(z):
    return 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-z))


def d_sigmoid(z):
    s = sigmoid(z)
    return s * (1.0 - s)


def d_tanh(z):
    t = np.tanh(z)
    return 1.0 - t * t


def relu(z):
    return np.maximum(z, 0.0)


def d_relu(z):
    return (z > 0).astype(float)


# 1. Are they differentiated correctly? Central difference against the closed form.
h = 1e-5
zs = np.array([-3.0, -0.5, 0.7, 2.5])

for name, f, df in [("sigmoid", sigmoid, d_sigmoid),
                    ("tanh", np.tanh, d_tanh),
                    ("ReLU", relu, d_relu)]:
    central = (f(zs + h) - f(zs - h)) / (2 * h)
    print("  ", name.ljust(8), "max |formula - central difference| =",
          "%.1e" % np.abs(df(zs) - central).max())

print(" dReLU/dz:", "  ".join("%9.2e" % v for v in d_relu(far)))
print()

The first check earns the right to use everything else. The central difference
$\left(\varphi(z+h) - \varphi(z-h)\right) / 2h$ with $h = 10^{-5}$ approximates the derivative knowing
nothing of the formula, and the three agree to about $10^{-11}$. If you differentiate an activation
wrong, this tells you in two lines.

The second table puts numbers on saturation. The derivative of the sigmoid goes from
$2.50 \times 10^{-1}$ at the origin to $4.54 \times 10^{-5}$ at $z = 10$, four orders of magnitude;
that of the hyperbolic tangent covers eight over the same stretch. The ReLU row does not decay: $1$ in
the last three columns, as far out as you care to go. The $0$ in the first is the convention above put
into code.

The third check settles the question without training anything. Every affine function $f$ satisfies
the identity

$$
f(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{y}) - f(\mathbf{x}) - f(\mathbf{y}) + f(\mathbf{0}) = 0,
$$

whatever $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ are (expand it and you will see the four biases cancel). With
the identity in $\varphi$, the chain of two neurons gives $-6.66 \times 10^{-16}$: zero, as far as the
computer's arithmetic reaches. With the sigmoid it gives $-0.564$. It is the same pair of neurons and
the same inputs; the only thing that has changed is the activation.

The last table returns to the previous lesson: the ten pre-activations of that review neuron, with its
weights untouched, passed through the step and through the sigmoid. The step hands back the usual
column; the sigmoid separates what the step ran together. The review that sat right on the boundary
goes from a firm $1$ to $0.500$, and the four negatives, which all received the same $0$, now split
into two values: $0.269$ for the one that sat at $-1$, $0.119$ for the three that sat at $-2$. The
magnitude of $z$ is back, and without a single weight being touched.

There is something left to say about what $\varphi$ has not changed, before it looks as though the
sigmoid has solved more than it solves. The decision boundary is still exactly where it was. Asking
where $\sigma(z)$ crosses $0.5$ is asking where $z$ crosses $0$, because $\sigma$ is increasing and
$\sigma(0) = 1/2$: the hyperplane of the previous lesson survives every substitution of this one
intact. What has changed is what the neuron reports about its decision, not what the decision is.

So stacking neurons can now build something a single one cannot (without non-linearity it could not
even do that), but no one has yet shown a problem that forces you to stack them. There is a prior and
more uncomfortable question missing: is there something a neuron, with any activation at all, can
never do? There is, it fits in four points drawn on a sheet of paper, and it is
<Leccion slug="xor-y-capas-ocultas">the next lesson, on XOR (exclusive or) and hidden layers</Leccion>.
