{/* COURSE-P5-02 — Block 2, Lesson 2. */}

The previous lesson, on the artificial neuron, left one piece still unchosen. The weights and the
bias are the neuron itself (they fix the hyperplane, and with it everything it can ever tell apart),
but between the pre-activation $z$ and what the neuron actually returns sits one more function,
$\varphi$, that hasn't been chosen yet: the step filled that role by default, and only
provisionally. Choosing it properly is what this lesson is about, and it's no cosmetic detail. Two
things ride on $\varphi$: how much of the number the neuron computes survives, and what happens when
you put one neuron behind another.

Start by watching what the step does when a weight moves. Take any neuron and nudge a single one of
its weights from $1.0$ to $1.1$, to $1.2$, to $1.3$. The pre-activation tracks the movement exactly:
it grows a little each time, passing through every value in between. But the output doesn't notice
any of it. It sits at $0$ the whole time $z$ is negative, and the instant $z$ crosses zero, it jumps
straight to $1$ and stays there. Whatever there was to learn about how far the weight had moved gets
lost along the way, and always at the very same point.

Before writing down a single candidate, it helps to look at them side by side. The explorable draws
each one over $[-5, 5]$, overlays its derivative as a dashed line, and shades in red the stretches
where that derivative all but vanishes.

the red covers the whole graph.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
An activation function has two independent jobs to do. It can't be a straight line, because a
straight line means stacking neurons builds nothing new, and its derivative can't be zero
everywhere, because that derivative is exactly what later corrects the weights. The step passes the
first test and fails the second about as badly as a function can.
</Callout>

## Why the activation cannot be a straight line

Start with the requirement that doesn't depend on any particular case. Chain two neurons together:
the first looks at the input $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ and returns a number, and the second
looks at that number and returns another. There's one neuron at each level, so the first level's
weights form a vector and the second's is a single scalar; the superscript in parentheses marks the
level, the same notation the layers will carry once each one holds several neurons:

$$
a^{(1)} = \varphi\left(\left(\mathbf{w}^{(1)}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b^{(1)}\right),
\qquad
a^{(2)} = \varphi\left(w^{(2)} a^{(1)} + b^{(2)}\right),
$$

with $\mathbf{w}^{(1)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ and $b^{(1)}, w^{(2)}, b^{(2)} \in \mathbb{R}$. Now set
$\varphi$ to the identity in both activations, $\varphi(z) = z$, and substitute the first expression
into the second:

$$
\begin{aligned}
a^{(2)}
&= w^{(2)}\left(\left(\mathbf{w}^{(1)}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b^{(1)}\right) + b^{(2)} \\[2pt]
&= \left(w^{(2)}\mathbf{w}^{(1)}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{x} + \left(w^{(2)}b^{(1)} + b^{(2)}\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

The right-hand side is a neuron: a single one, with weight vector $w^{(2)}\mathbf{w}^{(1)}$ and bias
$w^{(2)}b^{(1)} + b^{(2)}$, and no input can tell it apart from the chain that produced it, since the
two give the same number for every $\mathbf{x}$. The previous lesson already told you what that
means: everything a neuron tells apart lives on its boundary, and that boundary is a hyperplane. So
the chain of two separates with a hyperplane too. Chaining hasn't curved the boundary at all.

And there was nothing special about picking the identity. Try any straight line for $\varphi$,
$\varphi(z) = \alpha z + \beta$ with $\alpha \neq 0$, and you'll find that $\alpha$ gets absorbed
into the next level's weights and $\beta$ into its bias: what comes out the other side is, again, a
pre-activation. Any straight line behaves the same way.

It helps to name this precisely. The expression $\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b$ isn't linear in the
strict algebraic sense (a linear map sends zero to zero, and this one sends it to $b$ instead): it's
**affine**. Composing affine functions always gives back an affine function, and that holds no
matter how far you chain it: a hundred neurons in a row, each with a straight line for $\varphi$, add
up to exactly one neuron. The property that has to break, and it can only break inside the
activation, is the one this lesson takes its title from: **non-linearity**, though what actually gets
broken is affineness.

<Details summary="The same computation with several neurons at each level">

None of this depended on having a single neuron per level. With several neurons per level (and, as
before, the identity in both activations), each level's weights gather into a matrix and its biases
into a vector, and the chain becomes

$$
\mathbf{W}^{(2)}\left(\mathbf{W}^{(1)}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}^{(1)}\right) + \mathbf{b}^{(2)}
= \left(\mathbf{W}^{(2)}\mathbf{W}^{(1)}\right)\mathbf{x}
+ \left(\mathbf{W}^{(2)}\mathbf{b}^{(1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(2)}\right),
$$

which is a single level all over again. The full matrix form, with its sizes worked out, belongs to
<Leccion slug="forward-pass">a lesson further on, the one on the forward pass</Leccion>; here it
serves one purpose only: showing that the collapse wasn't an artefact of putting a single neuron at
each level.

</Details>

The second requirement is easier to see in what happens to the step. Its derivative is zero
everywhere it even exists,

$$
\frac{d}{dz}\,\text{step}(z) = 0 \quad \text{for all } z \neq 0,
$$

and at $z = 0$ it doesn't even exist, because that's exactly where the function jumps. There's a
debt worth naming out loud here: how a neuron actually finds its own weights from examples belongs
to <Leccion slug="descenso-gradiente">a lesson further on, the one on gradient descent</Leccion>. All
you need for now is what that method runs on: derivatives of $\varphi$. An activation whose
derivative is zero everywhere has nothing to say about which direction to move the weights in, so the
whole procedure grinds to a halt. The step passes the first requirement (it's about as far from a
straight line as you can get) and fails the second completely.

## Three candidates and their derivative

The three candidates that follow all meet both requirements, and I've written each one alongside its
derivative already worked out: the rest of this block will lean on those derivatives far more than
on the functions themselves.

The **sigmoid** already has its own reserved symbol in this course, $\sigma$:

$$
\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}},
$$

continuous, increasing, with values in $(0, 1)$: it sends $z = 0$ to $\sigma(0) = 1/2$ and squeezes
the extremes towards $0$ and $1$ without ever quite reaching either. Let's work out its derivative.
Written as $\sigma(z) = \left(1 + e^{-z}\right)^{-1}$, the chain rule plus a convenient
factorisation give

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d\sigma}{dz}
&= -\left(1 + e^{-z}\right)^{-2} \cdot \left(-e^{-z}\right)
 = \frac{e^{-z}}{\left(1 + e^{-z}\right)^{2}} \\[2pt]
&= \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} \cdot \frac{e^{-z}}{1 + e^{-z}}
 = \sigma(z)\left(1 - \sigma(z)\right),
\end{aligned}
$$

where

$$
\dfrac{e^{-z}}{1 + e^{-z}} = \dfrac{1 + e^{-z} - 1}{1 + e^{-z}} = 1 - \sigma(z).
$$

The sigmoid's derivative comes out in terms of its own value, with no fresh exponential needed,
which is exactly what makes it cheap to evaluate thousands of times over.

The **hyperbolic tangent** traces the same shape over a different range of values:

$$
\tanh(z) = \frac{e^{z} - e^{-z}}{e^{z} + e^{-z}}.
$$

Its values sit in $(-1, 1)$ and $\tanh(0) = 0$: it's centred on zero, which is the difference that
actually matters compared with the sigmoid, whose output is always positive. Its derivative takes an
equally convenient form,

$$
\frac{d\tanh(z)}{dz} = 1 - \tanh^{2}(z),
$$

and at the origin it equals $1$, four times higher than the sigmoid's own peak.

<Details summary="Where 1 − tanh² comes from, and the likeness between the two curves">

To get the derivative, write $u = e^{z} - e^{-z}$ and $v = e^{z} + e^{-z}$: each one is the
derivative of the other, $\frac{du}{dz} = v$ and $\frac{dv}{dz} = u$. The quotient rule then gives

$$
\frac{d\tanh(z)}{dz} = \frac{v \cdot v - u \cdot u}{v^{2}}
= 1 - \left(\frac{u}{v}\right)^{2} = 1 - \tanh^{2}(z).
$$

The resemblance between the two curves isn't a coincidence, either: the hyperbolic tangent is really
a sigmoid, stretched by a factor of two and shifted, $\tanh(z) = 2\sigma(2z) - 1$. Expand the
right-hand side and multiply top and bottom by $e^{z}$,

$$
2\sigma(2z) - 1 = \frac{2}{1 + e^{-2z}} - 1 = \frac{1 - e^{-2z}}{1 + e^{-2z}}
= \frac{e^{z} - e^{-z}}{e^{z} + e^{-z}} = \tanh(z),
$$

and that also explains the factor of four between the two derivatives: differentiating
$2\sigma(2z) - 1$ picks up a $2$ from the outer factor and another $2$ from the chain rule.

</Details>

The third candidate is the youngest and the most widely used: **ReLU**, short for rectified linear
unit. It's defined as $\text{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z)$: it passes the positive part straight through and
sends the negative part to zero, so it crushes nothing from above. Whenever $z$ is positive, its
full magnitude reaches the output intact. Its derivative is

$$
\frac{d}{dz}\,\text{ReLU}(z) = \begin{cases}
  1 & \text{if } z > 0 \\
  0 & \text{if } z < 0
\end{cases},
$$

and at $z = 0$ it doesn't exist at all: the slope is $0$ coming from the left and $1$ coming from the
right, and a function with a corner has no single tangent line at that point. This course takes it
to be $0$ there, by convention rather than by any theorem, and the choice changes nothing that can
actually be measured.

### Where each one switches off

All three derivatives share one problem, and each one splits it up differently. Call **saturation**
the region where the derivative of $\varphi$ is so close to zero that the input can move a long way
while the output barely budges: that's what the red bands of the explorable mark out.

For the sigmoid, the arithmetic is straightforward. At $z = 5$, $\sigma(5) \approx 0.9933$, so its
derivative there works out to $0.9933 \cdot 0.0067 \approx 0.0066$: almost forty times smaller than
at the origin. The hyperbolic tangent switches off earlier and faster still
($1 - \tanh^{2}(5) \approx 1.8 \times 10^{-4}$, more than thirty times below the sigmoid at the same
point), which is why its red bands start closer to the origin even though its derivative starts out
four times higher. Starting higher and falling faster turn out to be entirely compatible.

ReLU breaks that pattern, and its half-graph red band doesn't contradict that at all. On the right,
its derivative stays exactly $1$ no matter how far out you go: it never saturates there, and that's
its whole appeal. On the left, it doesn't saturate either, since saturating means approaching zero:
it's switched off outright, with the derivative sitting at $0$ over an entire half-axis. Out of that
comes a defect with a name of its own, the **dying ReLU**: if a neuron's pre-activation stays
negative for every example it ever sees, its derivative is zero on all of them, nothing ever moves
it, and it never comes back.

That leaves the fourth button. **GELU**, short for Gaussian error linear unit, is the one used by
the Transformers of Block 5; its definition calls on the cumulative distribution function of a
normal distribution, and that's beyond the scope of this lesson. It looks much like ReLU except near
the origin, where instead of folding into a sharp corner it curves smoothly.

## The activations and their derivatives in NumPy

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

np.random.seed(0)

# --- The four candidates, and the derivative of the three that have one.
def step(z):
    return (z >= 0).astype(float)


def sigmoid(z):
    return 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-z))


def d_sigmoid(z):
    s = sigmoid(z)
    return s * (1.0 - s)


def d_tanh(z):
    t = np.tanh(z)
    return 1.0 - t * t


def relu(z):
    return np.maximum(z, 0.0)


def d_relu(z):
    return (z > 0).astype(float)


# 1. Are they differentiated correctly? Central difference against the closed form.
h = 1e-5
zs = np.array([-3.0, -0.5, 0.7, 2.5])
print("z =", zs, "  shape", zs.shape)
for name, f, df in [("sigmoid", sigmoid, d_sigmoid),
                    ("tanh", np.tanh, d_tanh),
                    ("ReLU", relu, d_relu)]:
    central = (f(zs + h) - f(zs - h)) / (2 * h)
    print("  ", name.ljust(8), "max |formula - central difference| =",
          "%.1e" % np.abs(df(zs) - central).max())
print()

# 2. How much of each derivative is left as we move away from the origin.

print("    dσ/dz:", "  ".join("%9.2e" % v for v in d_sigmoid(far)))
print(" dtanh/dz:", "  ".join("%9.2e" % v for v in d_tanh(far)))
print(" dReLU/dz:", "  ".join("%9.2e" % v for v in d_relu(far)))
print()

b2 = 0.7

    return z

    return w2 * phi(w1 @ x + b1) + b2

y = np.random.randn(d)

w_eq = w2 * w1

      bool(np.isclose(chain(x, identity), w_eq @ x + b_eq)))

print("     step:", "  ".join("%6.0f" % v for v in step(z_reviews)))

The first check earns the right to trust everything that follows. The central difference
$\left(\varphi(z+h) - \varphi(z-h)\right) / 2h$ with $h = 10^{-5}$ approximates the derivative
without knowing the formula at all, and all three agree with it to about $10^{-11}$. If you ever get
an activation's derivative wrong, this catches it in two lines.

The second table puts actual numbers on saturation. The sigmoid's derivative drops from
$2.50 \times 10^{-1}$ at the origin to $4.54 \times 10^{-5}$ at $z = 10$, four orders of magnitude;
the hyperbolic tangent's covers eight over that same stretch. The ReLU row doesn't decay at all: $1$
across the last three columns, however far out you go. The $0$ in the first column is the convention
above, written into code.

The third check settles the question without training a single thing. Every affine function $f$
satisfies the identity

$$
f(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{y}) - f(\mathbf{x}) - f(\mathbf{y}) + f(\mathbf{0}) = 0,
$$

no matter what $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ are (expand it out and you'll see the four biases
cancel). With the identity as $\varphi$, the chain of two neurons gives $-6.66 \times 10^{-16}$:
zero, as far as the computer's arithmetic can tell. With the sigmoid, it gives $-0.564$ instead. It's
the exact same pair of neurons and the exact same inputs; the only thing that changed is the
activation.

The last table circles back to the previous lesson: the same ten pre-activations from that review
neuron, weights untouched, run through both the step and the sigmoid. The step hands back the same
column as before; the sigmoid pulls apart what the step had lumped together. The review sitting
right on the boundary drops from a confident $1$ to $0.500$, and the four negatives, which had all
received the same flat $0$, now split into two distinct values: $0.269$ for the one sitting at $-1$,
and $0.119$ for the three sitting at $-2$. The magnitude of $z$ is back, without touching a single
weight.

There's still something worth saying about what $\varphi$ hasn't changed, before it starts to look
like the sigmoid has solved more than it actually has. The decision boundary is exactly where it
always was. Asking where $\sigma(z)$ crosses $0.5$ is the same as asking where $z$ crosses $0$,
because $\sigma$ is increasing and $\sigma(0) = 1/2$: the hyperplane from the previous lesson
survives every substitution this lesson has made, completely intact. What's changed is what the
neuron reports about its decision, not the decision itself.

So stacking neurons can now build something a single one never could (without non-linearity, it
couldn't even manage that much), but nobody has shown you a problem that actually forces you to
stack them. There's a more uncomfortable question still missing: is there something a neuron, with
any activation function at all, can never do, full stop? There is, and it fits in four points drawn
on a sheet of paper: it's <Leccion slug="xor-y-capas-ocultas">the next lesson, on XOR (exclusive or)
