Self-attention is fully assembled except for one symbol. The three projections $\mathbf{Q}$,
$\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ have their argument, the weight matrix $\mathbf{A}$ is square
($T \times T$) because the sentence looks at itself, and the softmax by rows shares one unit out
among the positions of each row. But the $\sqrt{d_k}$ in the denominator was written into the
formula without saying why it is there, and it still hasn't been explained. It's the only loan
still outstanding, and this lesson pays it off.

The question has more of an edge than it seems. Why a root, and not $d_k$ itself? And why of
$d_k$, and not of $d_{\text{model}}$ or of the length of the sentence? Both are answered at once,
because both depend on finding out how far a sum moves away from zero as terms are added to it.
That takes no elaborate probability theory, only averaging, and the calculation starts by
averaging over a finite list that can be walked through in full.

Take the crudest possible version of a query and a key, that is, of a row of $\mathbf{Q}$ and a
row of $\mathbf{K}$: all their coordinates are $+1$ or $-1$. The score is then a count of
agreements, because each term contributes $+1$ if the two coordinates share a sign and $-1$ if
they don't. With four coordinates, three agreements and one disagreement give $3 - 1 = 2$. With
four hundred, the same proportion (three hundred against one hundred) gives $200$.

And that's what there is to see, and it isn't that the terms have grown: they're still $\pm 1$.
It's that there are a hundred times as many. A small deviation from the exact half, repeated over
more terms, turns into a large number, and an exponential is about to swallow that number.

With random signs the proportion doesn't stay fixed at three to one, and the score grows more
slowly: its typical size goes with $\sqrt{d_k}$, as the next section derives. The figure takes one
row of six scores at that size in three widths and tries three divisors before the softmax: $1$
(that is, not dividing), $\sqrt{d_k}$ and $d_k$ itself. Each group of bars is the row's share-out,
one weight per position, and the number above it is what the winning position takes. Look for the
row that doesn't change from one column to the next.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
The root doesn't tune the share-out by eye: it takes away from the score exactly what the number of
coordinates had added to it, and leaves the same scale in a narrow model and in a wide one.
</Callout>

## A score grows with the root of the width

Each cell of $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}$ is the dot product of a row of $\mathbf{Q}$ (the query
of one position) with a row of $\mathbf{K}$ (the key of another). Both live in
$\mathbb{R}^{d_k}$, and I write them $\mathbf{q}$ and $\mathbf{k}$ with no position index because
the calculation is the same for any pair. The score is

$$
e = \mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{k} = \sum_{m=1}^{d_k} q_m k_m,
$$

a sum of $d_k$ terms. What size comes out depends on what size goes in, so let's fix the exact
case first: the coordinates are $+1$ or $-1$, as in the count of agreements above. Call
$s_m = q_m k_m$ the $m$-th term, which is then $+1$ or $-1$, and $e = \sum_m s_m$.

Squaring separates the diagonal from the rest:

$$
e^{2} = \left(\sum_{m} s_m\right)^{2} = \sum_{m} s_m^{2} + \sum_{m \neq n} s_m s_n = d_k + \sum_{m \neq n} s_m s_n,
$$

because $s_m^{2} = 1$ whatever the sign. The first term no longer depends on anything. The second
is the one that has to be averaged, and averaging here means something perfectly finite: running
through the $2^{d_k}$ sign combinations $\mathbf{q}$ can take, with $\mathbf{k}$ fixed, and taking
the mean. We'll write it $\mathbb{E}\left[\cdot\right]$, the **mean** of a quantity over the
randomness it is assumed to have. The cross terms all go.

<Details summary="See why each cross term averages to zero">

Fix two different indices $m \neq n$ and look at the term $s_m s_n = q_m k_m q_n k_n$. Pair each
sign combination with the one you get by flipping the sign of $q_m$ and leaving the other
$d_k - 1$ coordinates where they are. That's a perfect pairing of the $2^{d_k}$ combinations into
$2^{d_k - 1}$ pairs: each combination has exactly one partner, and it isn't itself.

Within a pair, $q_n$, $k_m$ and $k_n$ haven't moved and $q_m$ has changed sign, so the two values
of $s_m s_n$ are opposites and sum to zero. Adding zero $2^{d_k - 1}$ times and dividing by
$2^{d_k}$ leaves

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[s_m s_n\right] = 0, \qquad m \neq n.
$$

The same pairing gives $\mathbb{E}\left[e\right] = 0$: in $e = \sum_m s_m$ each term changes sign
along with its own coordinate, so each one averages zero on its own.

</Details>

So of the square, the first term survives and nothing else:

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[e^{2}\right] = d_k.
$$

With $\mathbb{E}\left[e\right] = 0$, that's all it takes. The score is spread around zero, and
what measures how far it typically lands from it (the **standard deviation**) is the root of that
mean:

$$
\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[e^{2}\right]} = \sqrt{d_k},
$$

which answers half the question. Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ leaves
$\mathbb{E}\left[\left(e/\sqrt{d_k}\right)^{2}\right] = 1$, and $d_k$ doesn't appear in that $1$:
the same scale with queries of eight coordinates and with queries of a thousand.

With the divisor in place, the whole operation (product, division, softmax and mixture) gets a
name of its own: the **scaled dot-product**, which is what the paper calls it. The dot product is
the usual one, the one in the line above; what is scaled is this division and nothing more.

The signs were there to make the calculation exact, but notice which properties it really needed.
Two: that $\mathbb{E}\left[s_m\right] = 0$ and that $\mathbb{E}\left[s_m^{2}\right] = 1$. Any
centred initialisation whose coordinates are independent and of typical size $1$ meets them, and
with them the same cancellation leaves the same $\mathbb{E}\left[e^{2}\right] = d_k$. The
Gaussians from `rng.normal` that the course has been using since <Leccion slug="implementar-mlp">the
lesson on implementing an MLP</Leccion> are one of those initialisations, and the first cell
measures it with them.

It's worth saying out loud what that assumption is about, because it isn't about the model you'll
come across. It's about a *freshly initialised* network: nothing guarantees that after thousands of
steps the coordinates of $\mathbf{q}$ are still independent or still of size $1$. That's why the
divisor is fixed when the layer is designed and not measured on the fly: it fixes the start, which
is where the problem halts training before it has even begun.

That leaves the part usually taken for granted: why the root and not $d_k$. The divisor has to take
away from the score exactly what the width put into it, and what the width put in is a factor
$\sqrt{d_k}$. Dividing by $d_k$ would leave the scores at a typical size of $1/\sqrt{d_k}$, closer
and closer to zero the wider the model; and scores that tend to zero give a softmax that tends to
the even share, $1/T$ in every cell, so the row stops choosing anything. Those are two failures,
one on each side, and the root is the only exponent that falls into neither.

## A closed row returns no gradient

If the scores measure $\sqrt{d_k}$, the *differences* between the scores of one row measure that
too, and what the softmax looks at is the differences:

$$
\alpha_{ij} = \frac{\exp\left(e_{ij}\right)}{\sum_{m=1}^{T}\exp\left(e_{im}\right)},
$$

with $m$ running over the positions of row $i$. Two scores $8$ apart (an ordinary gap when the
typical size is $8$, which is what $d_k = 64$ gives) end up a factor $\exp(8) \approx 2\,981$ from
each other, and with that the share-out is over: one position takes almost the whole unit.

A row that chooses firmly doesn't sound like a defect. The defect is in the derivative.

Differentiate $\alpha_{ij}$ with respect to the scores of its own row. It's the same calculation
<Leccion slug="funcion-de-perdida">the lesson on loss functions</Leccion> did with the softmax of
the output layer, and the same two cases come out, according to whether the index matches or not:

$$
\frac{\partial \alpha_{ij}}{\partial e_{ij}} = \alpha_{ij}\left(1 - \alpha_{ij}\right), \qquad
\frac{\partial \alpha_{ij}}{\partial e_{im}} = -\alpha_{ij}\,\alpha_{im} \quad (m \neq j).
$$

Now take the closed row, with one weight at almost $1$ and the rest at almost $0$. The diagonal
case gives $1$ times $0$; the cross case, in any cell, carries at least one factor that is almost
zero. Both derivatives switch off at once, and the whole row with them.

What that costs shows when you look at what comes before the scores. Only $\mathbf{W}^Q$ and
$\mathbf{W}^K$: the scores come out of $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}$ and nothing else goes in. If
the gradient doesn't get through the softmax, those two matrices receive nothing from that row, and
they are precisely the ones that would have to learn which positions each position should look at.
The layer goes on computing (a saturated output is a perfectly well-defined output); what it can
no longer do is change its mind.

It's worth stopping on why Block 2 never ran into this, because the difference is one line. There
the softmax was followed by the cross-entropy, and the $-\log$ of the loss supplies the exact
inverse factor: together the two gave
$\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\,\ell = \hat{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbf{y}$, which is $\hat{y}_c - 1$ in the
correct class and doesn't vanish however sure the network is. Here there is no logarithm behind the
softmax, only the multiplication by $\mathbf{V}$, the matrix of values. The Jacobian cancels
against nothing: it stays whole, zeros included.

And so two things the course had closed, in different blocks, turn up again. The curve
$\alpha\left(1 - \alpha\right)$ is the same as the derivative of the sigmoid, with its maximum of
$0.25$ in the middle and its two flat tails: the saturation of <Leccion slug="funciones-activacion">the
lesson on activation functions</Leccion>, in a layer with no sigmoid in it. And a gradient that
arrives switched off at the matrices that had to learn is the whole of Block 3, in an architecture
without a single recurrence. What joined them was never the sigmoid or the recurrent state: it was
that in both there was a function flattening out.

A whole row comes down to one number, which comes from adding the diagonal case over the $T$
positions:

$$
\sum_{j=1}^{T} \alpha_{ij}\left(1 - \alpha_{ij}\right) = \sum_{j} \alpha_{ij} - \sum_{j} \alpha_{ij}^{2} = 1 - \sum_{j=1}^{T} \alpha_{ij}^{2},
$$

where the last equality uses that the row sums to $1$. With the row shared out evenly it is
$1 - 1/T$; with the whole row on one position it is $0$. It's the sum of the diagonal of the
Jacobian, and it measures the same thing from both sides: how much that row shares out, and how much
it still has left to learn.

## The divisor, measured in NumPy

The first cell takes the calculation for the signs and checks it where it really applies: Gaussian
coordinates, five widths, the measured typical size set against the $\sqrt{d_k}$ column.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
N = 8000                                       # pairs of vectors per width

print(f"{'d_k':>5}   {'mean':>7}   {'typical size':>13}   {'sqrt(d_k)':>9}   {'divided':>12}")
for d_k in (4, 16, 64, 256, 1024):
    Q = rng.normal(size=(N, d_k))              # centred coordinates, of typical size 1
    K = rng.normal(size=(N, d_k))
    e = np.sum(Q * K, axis=1)                  # one score per pair
    typical = np.sqrt(np.mean(e ** 2))
    print(f"{d_k:5d}   {e.mean():7.3f}   {typical:13.2f}   {np.sqrt(d_k):9.2f}   {typical / np.sqrt(d_k):12.2f}")

The two middle columns agree as far as measuring with eight thousand pairs allows, and the one on
the right stays pinned at $1$: dividing puts the size in the same place, whatever the width. The
mean column is small next to the typical size on its row (at $d_k = 1\,024$, $0.236$ against
$32.03$), which is what $\mathbb{E}\left[e\right] = 0$ says when it is measured instead of proved.

The second cell moves from the scores to what comes out of them. Look above all at the last column,
which is the one that decides whether the layer can learn.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
T, N = 8, 4000                                 # 8 positions per row, 4000 rows measured


def softmax_rows(E):
    Z = np.exp(E - E.max(axis=1, keepdims=True))
    return Z / Z.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)

With the divisor in place, the scale of the scores stops depending on the width, and the share-out
and the gradient stop depending on it through the same calculation. But look at what the divisor
takes for granted: it receives $d_k$ already decided. Nothing we've worked out says how long a
query should be (only how to make up for whatever length it has), and so far $d_k$ is a number that
turned up because the projections of the previous lesson had to come out at some width.

There's a second thing this layer can't do, and it points to the same place. A row of $\mathbf{A}$
is a share-out, and only one: in <W>the keys to this car are here</W>, the position of <W>are</W>
has to decide with a single set of weights whether to look at <W>keys</W>, which is what it agrees
with, or at <W>to this car</W>, which is what stands between them; and with one row it can't do
both. The answer to both questions is the same and takes up <Leccion slug="multi-head">the next
lesson, on multi-head attention</Leccion>: several pairs of projections working in parallel, each
with its own share-out and its own width, split among them out of $d_{\text{model}}$. There $d_k$
stops being a given number and becomes a decision with a reason.
