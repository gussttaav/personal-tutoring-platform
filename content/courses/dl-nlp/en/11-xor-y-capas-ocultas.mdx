The previous lesson, on activation functions and non-linearity, finished off the neuron: a weighted
sum, topped with a curved $\varphi$ that has a usable derivative (sigmoid, hyperbolic tangent, or
ReLU) in place of the step that filled that slot at the start. Finished, but with one limit none of
the three candidates touches: the neuron still answers by which side of a hyperplane the input fell
on. That shifts the question elsewhere. It's no longer about which $\varphi$ to pick, but a question
about the data itself: which arrangements of examples fit inside a hyperplane, and whether there's
one that fits inside none at all.

Four phrases are enough to find one. Take <W>it's good</W>, <W>it's bad</W>, <W>not good</W> and
<W>not bad</W>, and classify them: the first and last speak well, the middle two speak badly. All
four are told apart by exactly two facts (whether <W>not</W> shows up, and whether the evaluative
word is <W>good</W> or <W>bad</W>), so two coordinates describe them completely. Now look at what
<W>not</W> actually does. It carries no sign of its own: it flips the sign of whatever word follows
it. And a weight, by contrast, is a fixed sign.

Those two coordinates fit neatly into the same drawing from <Leccion slug="la-neurona">the lesson on
the artificial neuron</Leccion>: each phrase becomes a point in the plane, its colour marks what it
says, and the neuron looks for a line that leaves one colour on each side. The second button of the
explorable loads exactly those four points.

<Explorable
  id="perceptron-boundary"
  caption='Press "Not separable" and let the line keep working at it: it never settles down, and it stays red and dashed because there is always some point sitting on the wrong side. Now drag one of the four corners towards the centre, and watch it find room and turn green. What made this impossible was never the number of points, only where they sat.'

The four points sit at the corners of a square, and each class occupies two opposite corners. A line
splits the plane into two halves, and there's no way to put two opposite corners in the same half
without dragging one of the other two along with them. The explorable shows this; it doesn't prove
it. A procedure failing to find a line only tells you the procedure failed, not that no line exists.
Every possible line has to be ruled out at once.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
When each class sits on a diagonal, no hyperplane can work, and swapping the step for any of the
other activations you've seen doesn't fix it either, because the boundary is still a hyperplane.
What does change the problem is putting neurons in front: the hidden layer defined further on exists
to carry the points into coordinates where a line finally can separate them.
</Callout>

## Four phrases no hyperplane separates

Let's fix the coordinates. Set $x_1 = +1$ when the phrase contains <W>not</W> and $x_1 = -1$ when
it doesn't, and set $x_2 = +1$ when the evaluative word is <W>good</W> and $x_2 = -1$ when it's
<W>bad</W>. The label $y$ is $1$ if the phrase speaks well and $0$ if it speaks badly, the same two
values the step returns.

| phrase | $\mathbf{x}$ | $y$ |
|---|---|---|
| <W>it's good</W> | $(-1,\, +1)^{\top}$ | $1$ |
| <W>it's bad</W> | $(-1,\, -1)^{\top}$ | $0$ |
| <W>not good</W> | $(+1,\, +1)^{\top}$ | $0$ |
| <W>not bad</W> | $(+1,\, -1)^{\top}$ | $1$ |

The label is $1$ exactly when the two coordinates differ. That function has had a name since long
before neural networks existed: the **exclusive or** (XOR), and its two classes are the two
diagonals of the square.

Suppose there exist $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^{2}$ and $b \in \mathbb{R}$ that let the neuron
$\text{step}\left(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b\right)$ get all four right. Getting the two
positives right means $z \geq 0$ on both, and getting the two negatives right means $z < 0$ on both.
Add each pair separately, with $z(\mathbf{x}) = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b$:

$$
z(-1, +1) + z(+1, -1) = \left(-w_1 + w_2 + b\right) + \left(w_1 - w_2 + b\right) = 2b,
$$

and for the other diagonal, with the same weights,

$$
z(-1, -1) + z(+1, +1) = \left(-w_1 - w_2 + b\right) + \left(w_1 + w_2 + b\right) = 2b.
$$

The weights cancel out in both, and the two sums land on the same number. But the first sum adds two
quantities that are $\geq 0$, so $2b \geq 0$; the second adds two quantities that are $< 0$, so
$2b < 0$. No single $b$ can satisfy both at once, so the neuron you assumed doesn't exist.

It's worth reading that cancellation straight off the drawing, because it shows exactly where it
comes from. The two segments, the one joining the two positive phrases and the one joining the two
negative ones, share the same midpoint: the origin. And because $z$ is affine, its value at the
midpoint of a segment is the mean of its values at the two endpoints. The origin would have to fall
on the positive side for sitting between two positives, and on the negative side for sitting between
two negatives.

<Details summary="The general version: two segments that cross always rule it out">

Nothing in the computation above depended on the coordinates being $\pm 1$, or on the four points
forming a square. Let $\mathbf{p}_1, \mathbf{p}_2$ be two examples of one class and
$\mathbf{n}_1, \mathbf{n}_2$ two examples of the other, and suppose the segment joining the first
pair crosses the segment joining the second pair at a point $\mathbf{q}$. Crossing means that point
can be written both ways at once:

$$
\mathbf{q} = \lambda \mathbf{p}_1 + (1 - \lambda)\, \mathbf{p}_2
           = \mu \mathbf{n}_1 + (1 - \mu)\, \mathbf{n}_2,
$$

with $\lambda, \mu \in [0, 1]$. An affine function distributes cleanly over combinations like these,
because the weights sum to $1$ and the bias comes through untouched:

$$
\begin{aligned}
z\left(\lambda \mathbf{u} + (1 - \lambda)\mathbf{v}\right)
&= \lambda \left(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{u} + b\right) + (1 - \lambda)\left(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{v} + b\right) \\[2pt]
&= \lambda\, z(\mathbf{u}) + (1 - \lambda)\, z(\mathbf{v}).
\end{aligned}
$$

If the neuron gets them right, $z(\mathbf{p}_1)$ and $z(\mathbf{p}_2)$ are both $\geq 0$, and
$z(\mathbf{q})$, a mean of the two with non-negative weights, must be too. Coming at it the other
way, $z(\mathbf{n}_1)$ and $z(\mathbf{n}_2)$ are both $< 0$, so $z(\mathbf{q})$ has to be $< 0$ as
well. The same point, with two incompatible signs.

With this in hand, the result doesn't change if you'd rather encode the phrases with $0$ and $1$
instead of $-1$ and $+1$: the four points then sit at the corners of the unit square, the two
segments cross at $(1/2,\, 1/2)$, and the exact same contradiction appears.

</Details>

### And no activation fixes it

The proof leaned on the step in exactly one place: to read "gets it right" as $z \geq 0$ or $z < 0$.
That's the only thing that needs checking again. Let $\varphi$ be strictly increasing (the sigmoid
and the hyperbolic tangent both are), and let $\theta$ be whatever threshold you use to read its
output as a yes. Being increasing makes it invertible on its range, so $\varphi(z) \geq \theta$ is
equivalent to $z \geq \varphi^{-1}(\theta)$, and the set of phrases that get a yes turns out to be

$$
\left\{\, \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{2} \;:\; \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b - \varphi^{-1}(\theta) \geq 0 \,\right\},
$$

which is exactly the half-plane of the same neuron, only with a different bias,
$b - \varphi^{-1}(\theta)$. The proof carries over without changing a single letter.

ReLU isn't strictly increasing, and it doesn't escape the argument either. With $\theta > 0$, asking
$\text{ReLU}(z) \geq \theta$ amounts to asking $z \geq \theta$, another half-plane; with
$\theta \leq 0$ it holds for every input, and the neuron answers yes to all four phrases, including
the two that speak badly. Half-plane or the whole plane, and neither one splits the diagonals.

## A layer is several neurons at once

What fails isn't the neuron itself: it's that there's only one of it. Put two neurons in front, both
looking at the same input, and a third looking at whatever those two hand back. That leaves three
columns, and each one has a name. The **input layer** is $\mathbf{x}$ itself and computes nothing
(it's drawn as a column of circles by convention, though there are no actual neurons there); the
**hidden layer** is the first two neurons, called hidden because its output isn't the final answer
and nobody outside ever reads it; and the **output layer** is the third neuron, the one that
actually answers.

With several neurons at one level, each neuron's weight vector becomes a row. Neuron $j$ of layer
$l$ keeps its weights in row $j$ of $\mathbf{W}^{(l)}$ and its bias in $b^{(l)}_j$, and what were the
scalars $z$ and $a$ in <Leccion slug="la-neurona">the lesson on the artificial neuron</Leccion> are
now the coordinates $z^{(l)}_j$ and $h^{(l)}_j$. It's the same object, only stacked; nothing about it
has changed on the inside. With $\mathbf{h}^{(0)} = \mathbf{x}$, the hidden layer computes

$$
\mathbf{z}^{(1)} = \mathbf{W}^{(1)}\mathbf{h}^{(0)} + \mathbf{b}^{(1)},
\qquad
\mathbf{h}^{(1)} = \varphi\left(\mathbf{z}^{(1)}\right),
$$

with $\mathbf{W}^{(1)} \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 2}$ and $\mathbf{b}^{(1)}, \mathbf{z}^{(1)},
\mathbf{h}^{(1)} \in \mathbb{R}^{2}$, with $\varphi$ applied coordinate by coordinate. The output
layer does the same thing to $\mathbf{h}^{(1)}$:

$$
\mathbf{z}^{(2)} = \mathbf{W}^{(2)}\mathbf{h}^{(1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(2)},
\qquad
\hat{y} = \varphi\left(z^{(2)}_1\right),
$$

with $\mathbf{W}^{(2)} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 2}$ and $\mathbf{b}^{(2)} \in \mathbb{R}^{1}$. A layer
with a single neuron amounts to a matrix with a single row, and writing it this way costs a couple
of extra subscripts now but saves having two separate notations later.

### Two parallel lines separate what one alone cannot

These particular weights solve all four phrases, with the step as $\varphi$ in both layers:

$$
\mathbf{W}^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix},
\quad
\mathbf{b}^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix},
\quad
\mathbf{W}^{(2)} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix},
\quad
\mathbf{b}^{(2)} = \begin{pmatrix} -\tfrac{1}{2} \end{pmatrix}.
$$

I chose them by hand, the same way I chose the eight weights of the review neuron back in
<Leccion slug="la-neurona">the lesson on the artificial neuron</Leccion>, because nothing in the
course yet finds them on its own. And I chose them so they'd be easy to read. The two rows of
$\mathbf{W}^{(1)}$ are identical, so the two hidden neurons look in the same direction, and only the
bias tells them apart: the first asks whether $x_1 + x_2 \geq -1$, the second whether
$x_1 + x_2 \geq 1$. They're two parallel lines, and the positive class is the strip running between
them. A strip is the intersection of two half-planes, and deciding that intersection is exactly what
the output neuron does: $z^{(2)}_1 = h^{(1)}_1 - h^{(1)}_2 - 1/2$ asks for a yes to the first and a
no to the second.

| phrase | $x_1 + x_2$ | $\mathbf{z}^{(1)}$ | $\mathbf{h}^{(1)}$ | $z^{(2)}_1$ | $\hat{y}$ | $y$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| <W>it's good</W> | $0$ | $(1,\, -1)^{\top}$ | $(1,\, 0)^{\top}$ | $+0.5$ | $1$ | $1$ |
| <W>it's bad</W> | $-2$ | $(-1,\, -3)^{\top}$ | $(0,\, 0)^{\top}$ | $-0.5$ | $0$ | $0$ |
| <W>not good</W> | $2$ | $(3,\, 1)^{\top}$ | $(1,\, 1)^{\top}$ | $-0.5$ | $0$ | $0$ |
| <W>not bad</W> | $0$ | $(1,\, -1)^{\top}$ | $(1,\, 0)^{\top}$ | $+0.5$ | $1$ | $1$ |

The $\mathbf{h}^{(1)}$ column is where the result actually shows up. Four input points have come out
the other side as only three: $(0,\, 0)^{\top}$, $(1,\, 0)^{\top}$ and $(1,\, 1)^{\top}$, and the
positive class is exactly the middle one. In these new coordinates there's now a line that leaves one
class on each side, and the output neuron does nothing a lone neuron couldn't already do. The hidden
layer hasn't added any extra power to decide with; it has changed where the deciding happens.

That's the insight worth holding onto, because the previous block was entirely about the very same
thing. There, the representations were chosen by a person (one dimension per vocabulary entry, then
counts, then dense vectors), and every choice was a decision written by hand. A hidden layer is one
more representation like those, with one crucial difference: its coordinates come out of weights, and
weights can be told to adjust themselves. A neural network with at least one hidden layer has a name
of its own, the **multilayer perceptron** (MLP), and it's the one that gives this block its title.

## Every line of a grid, and then the network

The cell does two things. First, it tries a whole grid of lines by brute force, to find the ceiling
on how many it can get right; then it builds the two-layer network using the weights above and runs
it on the four phrases. Run it and compare the two blocks of output.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

np.random.seed(0)

# --- The four phrases, with their two coordinates and their label.
phrases = [
    ("it's good", (-1.0,  1.0), 1),
    ("it's bad",  (-1.0, -1.0), 0),
    ("not good",  ( 1.0,  1.0), 0),
    ("not bad",   ( 1.0, -1.0), 1),
]
X = np.array([coords for _, coords, _ in phrases])    # (4, 2): one row per phrase
y = np.array([label for _, _, label in phrases])
print("X has shape", X.shape, " labels:", y)
print()

# 1. Brute force: 41 values per parameter, every combination.
g = np.linspace(-3.0, 3.0, 41)
G1, G2, GB = np.meshgrid(g, g, g, indexing="ij")
lines = np.stack([G1.ravel(), G2.ravel(), GB.ravel()], axis=1)   # (41^3, 3)
Z = lines[:, :2] @ X.T + lines[:, 2:3]                # one z per line and per phrase
correct = ((Z >= 0).astype(int) == y).sum(axis=1)

print("lines tried:         ", len(lines))
print("most correct:        ", correct.max(), "of 4")
print("lines with 3 correct:", int((correct == 3).sum()))
print("lines with 4 correct:", int((correct == 4).sum()))
print()

# 2. The network: two hidden neurons and one output, with the lesson's weights.
W1 = np.array([[1.0, 1.0],
               [1.0, 1.0]])
b1 = np.array([1.0, -1.0])
W2 = np.array([[1.0, -1.0]])
b2 = np.array([-0.5])


def step(z):
    return (z >= 0).astype(float)


print("phrase".ljust(12), "z(1)".rjust(12), "h(1)".rjust(9), "z(2)".rjust(6), "  y_hat", " y")
right = 0
for text, coords, label in phrases:
    x = np.array(coords)
    z1 = W1 @ x + b1
    h1 = step(z1)
    z2 = W2 @ h1 + b2
    y_hat = int(step(z2)[0])
    right += int(y_hat == label)
    print(text.ljust(12),
          ("(%+.0f, %+.0f)" % (z1[0], z1[1])).rjust(12),
          ("(%.0f, %.0f)" % (h1[0], h1[1])).rjust(9),
          ("%+.1f" % z2[0]).rjust(6),
          "%6d" % y_hat, "%2d" % label)
print()
print("correct:", right, "of", len(phrases))
print()

# 3. The two positive phrases land on the same point of the hidden layer.
h_good = step(W1 @ np.array([-1.0, 1.0]) + b1)
h_bad = step(W1 @ np.array([1.0, -1.0]) + b1)
print("h(1) of it's good =", h_good)
print("h(1) of not bad   =", h_bad)
print("same vector?", bool(np.array_equal(h_good, h_bad)))
`} />

The grid tries $41^3 = 68\,921$ lines. None gets all four phrases right, and $17\,123$ of them stop
at three. That figure comes with a limit worth stating up front: a grid leaves out every line it
didn't try, which is almost all of them, so on its own it proves nothing (that's what the computation
above is for, and why it comes first). What the grid does add is the ceiling. Three correct isn't a
halfway result that a finer search would eventually fix; it's the actual maximum, and that maximum
gets reached all over the grid.

The network gets all four right. The cell's table is the same one shown above, now actually
computed, and in it $z^{(2)}_1$ comes out to $+0.5$ on the two positive phrases and $-0.5$ on the two
negative ones, with an identical margin on both sides. The last check prints exactly what the hidden
layer does: <W>it's good</W> and <W>not bad</W>, sitting at opposite corners of the input square,
both come out as $(1,\, 0)^{\top}$. They started as far apart as two points can be and ended up in
exactly the same place.

I wrote the four numbers in $\mathbf{W}^{(1)}$ and the three biases by eyeballing the square, and
that's the second time this debt has come up. It costs more to settle now, too. With a single
neuron, you could at least look at each weight and say which word it had an opinion about; the
weights of a hidden layer don't have an opinion about the answer at all, they have opinions about
intermediate coordinates nobody ever sees, and whether those opinions are right or wrong only shows
up two layers further on. Sharing that responsibility across layers is what the rest of this block is
about.

But there's a smaller, more urgent problem first, and it's really a problem of notation. The network
in this lesson fits in a table because it only has three neurons; with a vocabulary of fifty thousand
entries and a couple of hundred hidden neurons, walking through it neuron by neuron fits on no page,
and in no loop worth running. What's needed is a form where an entire layer becomes a product of
matrices, with a great many examples passing through that same product at once: that's
<Leccion slug="forward-pass">the next lesson, on the forward pass</Leccion>.
