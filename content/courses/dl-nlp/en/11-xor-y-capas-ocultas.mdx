{/* COURSE-P5-02 — Block 2, Lesson 3. */}

The previous lesson, on activation functions and non-linearity, finished the neuron off: a weighted
sum, and on top a curved $\varphi$ with a usable derivative (sigmoid, hyperbolic tangent or ReLU) in
place of the step that filled that slot at the start. Finished, and with a limit none of the three
candidates touches: the neuron answers by which side of a hyperplane the input fell on. That moves
the question elsewhere. It is no longer which $\varphi$ to assign, but a question about the data:
which arrangements of examples fit inside a hyperplane, and whether there is one that fits in none.

Four phrases are enough to find it. Take <W>it's good</W>, <W>it's bad</W>, <W>not good</W> and
<W>not bad</W>, and classify them: the first and the fourth speak well, the second and the third
speak badly. All four are told apart by two facts (whether <W>not</W> appears, and whether the
evaluative word is <W>good</W> or <W>bad</W>), so two coordinates describe them completely. Now look
at what <W>not</W> does. It contributes no sign of its own: it flips the sign of the word that
follows it. And a weight is a fixed sign.

Those two coordinates fit in a drawing, the one from <Leccion slug="la-neurona">the lesson on the artificial neuron</Leccion>: each phrase is a point in the plane, its colour is what it says, and the neuron looks for a line
that leaves one colour on each side. The second button of the explorable loads exactly those four
points.

<Explorable
  id="perceptron-boundary"
  caption='Press "Not separable" and let the line keep working: it never comes to a stop, and it stays red and dashed because it always has some point on the side that is not its own. Then drag one of the four corners towards the centre and you will see it find room and turn green. What was impossible was not the number of points, but where they sat.'

The four points are the corners of a square, and each class occupies two opposite corners. A line
splits the plane into two halves, and there is no way to put two opposite corners in the same half
without dragging one of the other two along with them. The explorable shows this, it does not prove
it: that a procedure fails to find a line leaves open that the line exists and it did not hit on
it. They have to be ruled out all at once.

<Callout type="intuition" title="The idea in one sentence">
When each class occupies a diagonal, no hyperplane works, and swapping the step for another of the
activations we have seen does not fix it, because the boundary is still a hyperplane. What does
change the problem is putting neurons in front: the hidden layer we will define further on is there
to carry the points to coordinates in which a line does separate them.
</Callout>

## Four phrases no hyperplane separates

Let's fix the coordinates. Let $x_1 = +1$ when the phrase contains <W>not</W> and $x_1 = -1$ when it
does not, and let $x_2 = +1$ when the evaluative word is <W>good</W> and $x_2 = -1$ when it is
<W>bad</W>. The label $y$ is $1$ if the phrase speaks well and $0$ if it speaks badly, the two values
the step returns.

| phrase | $\mathbf{x}$ | $y$ |
|---|---|---|
| <W>it's good</W> | $(-1,\, +1)^{\top}$ | $1$ |
| <W>it's bad</W> | $(-1,\, -1)^{\top}$ | $0$ |
| <W>not good</W> | $(+1,\, +1)^{\top}$ | $0$ |
| <W>not bad</W> | $(+1,\, -1)^{\top}$ | $1$ |

The label is $1$ exactly when the two coordinates differ. That function has had a name since long
before neural networks: it is the **exclusive or** (XOR), and its two classes are the two diagonals
of the square.

Suppose there exist $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^{2}$ and $b \in \mathbb{R}$ with which the neuron
$\text{step}\left(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b\right)$ gets all four right. Getting the two
positives right is $z \geq 0$ on both, and getting the two negatives right is $z < 0$ on both. Let's
add each pair separately, with $z(\mathbf{x}) = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b$:

$$
z(-1, +1) + z(+1, -1) = \left(-w_1 + w_2 + b\right) + \left(w_1 - w_2 + b\right) = 2b,
$$

and for the other diagonal, with the same weights,

$$
z(-1, -1) + z(+1, +1) = \left(-w_1 - w_2 + b\right) + \left(w_1 + w_2 + b\right) = 2b.
$$

The weights cancel in both, and the two sums give the same number. But the first adds two quantities
$\geq 0$, so $2b \geq 0$; and the second adds two quantities $< 0$, so $2b < 0$. One $b$ cannot
satisfy both, so the assumed neuron does not exist.

It is worth reading that cancellation off the drawing, because it says where it comes from. The two
segments (the one joining the two positive phrases and the one joining the two negative ones) have
the same midpoint, the origin. And $z$ is affine, so its value at the midpoint of a segment is the
mean of its values at the endpoints. The origin would have to fall on the positive side for lying
between two positives, and on the negative side for lying between two negatives.

<Details summary="The general version: two segments that cross always rule it out">

Nothing in the computation above depends on the coordinates being $\pm 1$ or on the four points
forming a square. Let $\mathbf{p}_1, \mathbf{p}_2$ be two examples of one class and
$\mathbf{n}_1, \mathbf{n}_2$ two of the other, and suppose the segment joining the first pair crosses
the one joining the second pair at a point $\mathbf{q}$. Crossing means that point can be written
both ways,

$$
\mathbf{q} = \lambda \mathbf{p}_1 + (1 - \lambda)\, \mathbf{p}_2
           = \mu \mathbf{n}_1 + (1 - \mu)\, \mathbf{n}_2,
$$

with $\lambda, \mu \in [0, 1]$. An affine function distributes over those combinations, because the
weights sum to $1$ and the bias survives intact:

$$
\begin{aligned}
z\left(\lambda \mathbf{u} + (1 - \lambda)\mathbf{v}\right)
&= \lambda \left(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{u} + b\right) + (1 - \lambda)\left(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{v} + b\right) \\[2pt]
&= \lambda\, z(\mathbf{u}) + (1 - \lambda)\, z(\mathbf{v}).
\end{aligned}
$$

If the neuron gets them right, $z(\mathbf{p}_1)$ and $z(\mathbf{p}_2)$ are $\geq 0$, and then
$z(\mathbf{q})$ (a mean of the two with non-negative weights) is too. By the other route,
$z(\mathbf{n}_1)$ and $z(\mathbf{n}_2)$ are $< 0$ and $z(\mathbf{q})$ has to be $< 0$. The same point
with two incompatible signs.

With this, the result does not change if you prefer to encode the phrases with $0$ and $1$ instead of
$-1$ and $+1$: the four points are then the corners of the unit square, the two segments cross at
$(1/2,\, 1/2)$ and the same contradiction appears.

</Details>

### And no activation fixes it

The proof used the step in one specific place: to read "gets it right" as $z \geq 0$ or $z < 0$. That
is the only thing to check. Let $\varphi$ be strictly increasing (the sigmoid and the hyperbolic
tangent are), and let $\theta$ be the threshold at which you decide to read its output as a yes.
Being increasing, it is invertible on its range, so $\varphi(z) \geq \theta$ is equivalent to
$z \geq \varphi^{-1}(\theta)$, and the set of phrases that get a yes is

$$
\left\{\, \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{2} \;:\; \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b - \varphi^{-1}(\theta) \geq 0 \,\right\},
$$

which is the half-plane of the same neuron with bias $b - \varphi^{-1}(\theta)$. The proof applies
without changing a letter.

ReLU is not strictly increasing and does not escape either. With $\theta > 0$, asking
$\text{ReLU}(z) \geq \theta$ is asking $z \geq \theta$, another half-plane; with $\theta \leq 0$ it
holds for every input and the neuron answers yes to all four phrases, including the two that speak
badly. Half-plane or the whole plane, and neither one splits the diagonals.

## A layer is several neurons at once

What fails is not the neuron: it is that there is only one. Put two looking at the same input and a
third looking at what those two hand back. That leaves three columns, and each one has a name. The
**input layer** is $\mathbf{x}$ and computes nothing (it is drawn as a column of circles by custom,
but there are no neurons there); the **hidden layer** is the first two neurons, hidden because its
output is not the final answer and no one reads it from outside; and the **output layer** is the
third, the one that answers.

With several neurons at one level, each one's weight vector becomes a row. Neuron $j$ of layer $l$
keeps its weights in row $j$ of $\mathbf{W}^{(l)}$ and its bias in $b^{(l)}_j$, and what in
<Leccion slug="la-neurona">the lesson on the artificial neuron</Leccion> were the scalars $z$ and $a$ are now the coordinates $z^{(l)}_j$ and $h^{(l)}_j$. It is the same object stacked; inside, nothing
has changed. With $\mathbf{h}^{(0)} = \mathbf{x}$, the hidden layer computes

$$
\mathbf{z}^{(1)} = \mathbf{W}^{(1)}\mathbf{h}^{(0)} + \mathbf{b}^{(1)},
\qquad
\mathbf{h}^{(1)} = \varphi\left(\mathbf{z}^{(1)}\right),
$$

with $\mathbf{W}^{(1)} \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 2}$ and $\mathbf{b}^{(1)}, \mathbf{z}^{(1)},
\mathbf{h}^{(1)} \in \mathbb{R}^{2}$, and with $\varphi$ applied coordinate by coordinate. The output
layer does the same on $\mathbf{h}^{(1)}$:

$$
\mathbf{z}^{(2)} = \mathbf{W}^{(2)}\mathbf{h}^{(1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(2)},
\qquad
\hat{y} = \varphi\left(z^{(2)}_1\right),
$$

with $\mathbf{W}^{(2)} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 2}$ and $\mathbf{b}^{(2)} \in \mathbb{R}^{1}$. A layer
of a single neuron is a matrix of a single row, and writing it this way costs a couple of subscripts
now and saves having two notations later.

### Two parallel lines separate what one alone cannot

These weights solve the four phrases, with the step in $\varphi$ in both layers:

$$
\mathbf{W}^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix},
\quad
\mathbf{b}^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix},
\quad
\mathbf{W}^{(2)} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix},
\quad
\mathbf{b}^{(2)} = \begin{pmatrix} -\tfrac{1}{2} \end{pmatrix}.
$$

I chose them by hand, like the eight weights of the review neuron in <Leccion slug="la-neurona">the lesson on the artificial neuron</Leccion>, because there is still nothing in the course that finds them on
its own. And they are chosen so they can be read. The two rows of $\mathbf{W}^{(1)}$ are identical, so
the two hidden neurons look in the same direction and only the bias tells them apart: the first asks
whether $x_1 + x_2 \geq -1$ and the second whether $x_1 + x_2 \geq 1$. They are two parallel lines,
and the positive class is the strip between them. A strip is the intersection of two half-planes, and
deciding that intersection is what the output neuron does: $z^{(2)}_1 = h^{(1)}_1 - h^{(1)}_2 - 1/2$
asks for a yes to the first and a no to the second.

| phrase | $x_1 + x_2$ | $\mathbf{z}^{(1)}$ | $\mathbf{h}^{(1)}$ | $z^{(2)}_1$ | $\hat{y}$ | $y$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| <W>it's good</W> | $0$ | $(1,\, -1)^{\top}$ | $(1,\, 0)^{\top}$ | $+0.5$ | $1$ | $1$ |
| <W>it's bad</W> | $-2$ | $(-1,\, -3)^{\top}$ | $(0,\, 0)^{\top}$ | $-0.5$ | $0$ | $0$ |
| <W>not good</W> | $2$ | $(3,\, 1)^{\top}$ | $(1,\, 1)^{\top}$ | $-0.5$ | $0$ | $0$ |
| <W>not bad</W> | $0$ | $(1,\, -1)^{\top}$ | $(1,\, 0)^{\top}$ | $+0.5$ | $1$ | $1$ |

The $\mathbf{h}^{(1)}$ column is the one with the result. Four input points have come out turned into
three: $(0,\, 0)^{\top}$, $(1,\, 0)^{\top}$ and $(1,\, 1)^{\top}$, and the positive class is exactly
the middle one. In those new coordinates there is now a line that leaves one class on each side, and
the output neuron does nothing a lone neuron could not do. The hidden layer has not added any power to
decide: it has changed the place from which the deciding is done.

That is the turn worth keeping, because the previous block is entirely about the same thing. There the
representations were chosen by a person (one dimension per vocabulary entry, then counts, then dense
vectors), and each choice was a decision written by hand. A hidden layer is one more representation,
with one difference: its coordinates come out of weights, and weights can be asked to adjust
themselves. A neural network with at least one hidden layer has a name of its own, the **multilayer
perceptron** (MLP), and it is the one that gives this block its title.

## Every line of a grid, and then the network

The cell does two things. First it tries a grid of lines by brute force, to see where the ceiling of
correct answers is; then it builds the two-layer network with the weights above and runs it over the
four phrases. Run it and compare the two blocks of output.

<PyCell packages={["numpy"]} code={`
import numpy as np

np.random.seed(0)

# --- The four phrases, with their two coordinates and their label.
phrases = [
    ("it's good", (-1.0,  1.0), 1),
    ("it's bad",  (-1.0, -1.0), 0),
    ("not good",  ( 1.0,  1.0), 0),
    ("not bad",   ( 1.0, -1.0), 1),
]
X = np.array([coords for _, coords, _ in phrases])    # (4, 2): one row per phrase
y = np.array([label for _, _, label in phrases])
print("X has shape", X.shape, " labels:", y)
print()

# 1. Brute force: 41 values per parameter, every combination.
g = np.linspace(-3.0, 3.0, 41)
G1, G2, GB = np.meshgrid(g, g, g, indexing="ij")
lines = np.stack([G1.ravel(), G2.ravel(), GB.ravel()], axis=1)   # (41^3, 3)
Z = lines[:, :2] @ X.T + lines[:, 2:3]                # one z per line and per phrase
correct = ((Z >= 0).astype(int) == y).sum(axis=1)

print("lines tried:         ", len(lines))
print("most correct:        ", correct.max(), "of 4")
print("lines with 3 correct:", int((correct == 3).sum()))
print("lines with 4 correct:", int((correct == 4).sum()))
print()

# 2. The network: two hidden neurons and one output, with the lesson's weights.
W1 = np.array([[1.0, 1.0],
               [1.0, 1.0]])
b1 = np.array([1.0, -1.0])
W2 = np.array([[1.0, -1.0]])
b2 = np.array([-0.5])


def step(z):
    return (z >= 0).astype(float)


print("phrase".ljust(12), "z(1)".rjust(12), "h(1)".rjust(9), "z(2)".rjust(6), "  y_hat", " y")
right = 0
for text, coords, label in phrases:
    x = np.array(coords)
    z1 = W1 @ x + b1
    h1 = step(z1)
    z2 = W2 @ h1 + b2
    y_hat = int(step(z2)[0])
    right += int(y_hat == label)
    print(text.ljust(12),
          ("(%+.0f, %+.0f)" % (z1[0], z1[1])).rjust(12),
          ("(%.0f, %.0f)" % (h1[0], h1[1])).rjust(9),
          ("%+.1f" % z2[0]).rjust(6),
          "%6d" % y_hat, "%2d" % label)
print()
print("correct:", right, "of", len(phrases))
print()

# 3. The two positive phrases land on the same point of the hidden layer.
h_good = step(W1 @ np.array([-1.0, 1.0]) + b1)
h_bad = step(W1 @ np.array([1.0, -1.0]) + b1)
print("h(1) of it's good =", h_good)
print("h(1) of not bad   =", h_bad)
print("same vector?", bool(np.array_equal(h_good, h_bad)))
`} />

The grid tries $41^3 = 68\,921$ lines, none gets all four phrases right and $17\,123$ stop at three.
That figure has a limit worth stating: a grid leaves out every line it did not try, which is almost
all of them, so on its own it proves nothing (that is what the computation above is for, and why it
comes first). What it adds is the ceiling. Three correct is not a halfway result that finer searching
would fix; it is the maximum, and the maximum is reached all over the place.

The network gets all four. The cell's table is the same one from above, now computed, and in it
$z^{(2)}_1$ is $+0.5$ on the two positive phrases and $-0.5$ on the two negative ones, with the same
margin on both sides. And the last check prints what the hidden layer does: <W>it's good</W> and
<W>not bad</W>, which occupy opposite corners of the input square, both come out as $(1,\, 0)^{\top}$.
They began as far apart as they could be and ended in the same place.

I wrote the four numbers of $\mathbf{W}^{(1)}$ and the three biases by looking at the square, and that
debt is now the second time it appears. And it costs more to pay now. With a single neuron you could
at least look at each weight and say which word it had an opinion about; the weights of a hidden layer
have no opinion about the answer, they have opinions about intermediate coordinates no one sees, and
whether they are right or wrong shows only two layers further on. Sharing the responsibility across
layers is what the rest of the block is about.

Before that there is a smaller and more urgent problem, and it is one of writing. The network of this
lesson fits in a table because it has three neurons; with a vocabulary of fifty thousand entries and a
couple of hundred hidden neurons, walking it neuron by neuron fits on no page and in no loop worth
running. What is needed is the form in which a whole layer is a product of matrices, and a great many
examples go through the same product at once: it is <Leccion slug="forward-pass">the next lesson, on the forward pass</Leccion>.
